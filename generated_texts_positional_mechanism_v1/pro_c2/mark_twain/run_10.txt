The livery stable smelled of hay and horses and the kind of lazy sweat that only comes on a real hot July day. Most of the men wasn’t working, just sitting on barrels and whittling, or chewing and spitting into the dust. Nobody had much to say. Then we heard the bell from the courthouse start to ringing, not like for a fire, but slow and steady, one ring at a time.

A feller spits a long stream of tobacco juice. “That’ll be old Judge Thatcher,” he says. “He’s gwyne to make his speech.”

Another man, one with a patch over his eye, he laughs a little. “A speech about what? The price of corn? Or is he gwyne to tell us all how we’re sinners again?”

Pretty soon folks started drifting over towards the courthouse square. It wasn’t a big crowd, just the usual loafers and some women from the general store wanting to see what the fuss was. I tagged along, too, because there warn’t nothing else happening. Judge Thatcher was standing on the courthouse steps. He was a tall, thin man, with white hair that was always a little wild, and he wore a black coat even when it was hot enough to cook a fish on the sidewalk. He waited till there was maybe thirty or forty people looking up at him.

He cleared his throat and says, real loud and shaky: “Friends! Neighbors! I have called you here on a matter of principle! A matter of justice!”

Somebody in the back yelled, “Did somebody steal your chickens again, Judge?”

A few people laughed, but the Judge, he didn't pay it no mind. “A viper has come among us!” he shouted, and he was starting to get worked up now, his face getting red. “A man of low character and even lower morals! I speak of the man they call Jedediah Stone!”

Well, everybody knew Jedediah Stone. He was a big, broad man with a black beard who ran the new saloon down by the crick. He’d only been in town a few months, but he’d already been in three fights and was known for winning at cards a little too often.

The Judge went on, his voice cracking like a whip. “He sells poison to our sons and he cheats our husbands at his gambling table! He is a blight on this town, a pestilence! And I say he must go!”

The Judge kept on like that for another ten minutes, calling Jedediah Stone every name he could think of and saying it was the town’s duty to run him out on a rail. The crowd just listened, some of them nodding, some of them looking bored. The Judge was always having a crusade about something. When he was all done, he wiped his face with a handkerchief and looked like he’d just run a race.

Then, a voice come from the edge of the crowd, real calm and deep. “You got something to say to me, Judge, you say it to my face.”

It was Jedediah Stone himself. He was leaning against the big oak tree, just watching, with his arms crossed over his chest. He hadn’t been there a minute ago. The crowd shuffled and parted to let him be seen. He didn’t look mad, just tired.

The Judge’s face went pale. He straightened his coat and says, “I have said it, sir. You are a scoundrel and a corrupter of this town’s virtue.”

Stone pushed himself off the tree and started walking slow towards the steps. He warn’t a big man like I thought, just solid. He says, “You’ve been saying things about me for weeks, Judge. Behind my back. In the church. In the store. I’ve let it be. But a man’s got a right to his good name, such as it is.” He stopped at the bottom of the steps. “I’ll give you till the church bell rings for evening prayer to take it all back. You tell these same folks you were wrong.”

The Judge puffed out his chest. “I will do no such thing! I will not be intimidated by a ruffian!”

Stone just looked at him, and his eyes was as cold as river water in winter. “Evening prayer, Judge. That’s all the time you get.” Then he turned and walked away, and the crowd moved to let him pass, nobody saying a word.

Well, the show was over for now. People started talking low to each other, wondering what would happen. I seen a few men go talk to the Judge, telling him he ought to just let it go, that Stone didn’t seem like a man to make idle threats. But the Judge, he was stubborn. He kept saying, “The law is on my side! Decency is on my side!”

I went and sat on a bench where I could see both the courthouse and the saloon down the street. The sun got lower and the shadows got long. I was thinking maybe Stone had just been bluffing. Then the church bell started to ring. It rang once, twice, three times.

On the fifth ring, the door to the saloon opened and Jedediah Stone stepped out. He didn’t have a gun or nothing, just his hands in his pockets. He walked up the street slow and deliberate, right towards the courthouse where the Judge was still standing on the steps talking to a couple of men.

When Stone was about halfway across the square, the Judge seen him coming. The other men with the Judge, they backed away quick. The Judge stood his ground, but he looked mighty small all of a sudden.

Stone stopped about ten feet away. The bell was still ringing. “Well, Judge?” he says, his voice not loud but it carried in the quiet.

The Judge’s voice was all quavery. “Get away from this courthouse, you devil! I’ll have the sheriff on you!”

“You’re a liar, Judge,” Stone said, plain and simple, like he was talking about the weather. “And you’re a coward.”

“I am not!” the Judge shrieked, and he pointed a finger at him. “You are the one who will be judged! You are filth and, ”

He never finished. Stone took two quick steps forward. He didn’t punch him. He just reached out and grabbed the front of the Judge’s coat with both hands and he shook him. He shook him once, hard, and the Judge’s head snapped back and then forward, and it made a sickening little crack. The Judge’s eyes went wide and then they went empty, and his whole body went limp like a rag doll. Stone let go and the Judge just crumpled down onto the steps in a heap.

Stone stood there for a second, looking down at him. The church bell stopped ringing. The whole square was dead silent. Then Stone turned and walked back to his saloon and went inside, and the swinging doors flapped shut behind him.

The crowd stared for a good long while, and then there was a rush. Everybody surged forward to the steps, pushing and shoving to get a look at the Judge. I got shoved right up to the front. The Judge was laying all twisted, with his mouth open and one shoe off. His wild white hair was full of dust. Somebody says, “He’s dead. He broke his neck.”

The sheriff finally come and took the Judge away, and then everybody started saying Stone ought to be hanged for it. In about a minute, that’s all you could hear, and a crowd started heading for the saloon.

It wasn’t Stone that come out. It was a little feller, old and stooped, with a bald head and a white apron on. He was Stone’s swamper, the one they called Pip. He shuffled out onto the porch and held up a shaky hand. “Gentlemen,” he squeaked, his voice thin as a thread. “Gentlemen, Mr. Stone, he asks you to please disperse.”

The blacksmith let out a great big laugh. “He asks us, does he? Well you go back in there and tell that murdering coward that we ain’t asking for him to come out, we’re telling him!”

The crowd laughed with him, and it broke the spell. They started shouting again, meaner this time, because they’d been scared and were ashamed of it.

Pip looked like he was about to cry. He opened his mouth to say something else, but just then the doors swung open again behind him, slow and easy.

Stone stepped out onto the porch.

He hadn’t changed his clothes. He still had on his white shirt and black vest. He wasn’t holding a pistol or a shotgun or nothing. His hands was empty and hung loose at his sides. He put one hand on Pip’s shoulder and gently moved the old man behind him. Then he stood there and looked at us.

He didn’t say a word. He just looked. His eyes moved over the crowd, slow and patient, from one side to the other. He wasn’t frowning and he wasn’t smiling. He just looked, and it was the most awful quiet I ever heard. All the yelling and shouting just died in a thousand throats. The only sound was a horse stamping its foot somewhere down the street. I was in the front part of the crowd, pushed up there against my will, and I could see his face clear. It was like looking at a rock.

He let the quiet stretch out and get heavy. Then he spoke, and his voice wasn’t loud, but it cut through the air like a razor.

“Well, now,” he says, soft-like. “Look at all the brave men.”

He took a step forward, right to the edge of the porch. Nobody moved.

“I see you, Lem,” he says, looking right at the big blacksmith. “You owe me for three weeks’ worth of whisky. Said your wife was sick. I reckon she’s feeling better now.”

The blacksmith’s face got red, and he looked down at his boots. He didn’t look so big anymore.

“And there’s Carter,” Stone says, his eyes moving on. “The man that runs the feed store. You told me last Tuesday the town needed more men of principle. Is this your principle, Carter? Hiding behind Lem?”

The feed store man, a feller with whiskers, he tried to shrink back into the crowd, but there was nowhere to go.

“Thompson,” Stone says, looking at another man. “Your boy was in here last night, crying drunk because you’d whipped him for stealing apples. I gave him coffee and sent him home. And here you are, ready to tear my place down.”

He went on like that. He knew them all. He named maybe ten or twelve men, one after another. He told something on every one of them. A debt they owed, a lie they told, a time they’d come to him for a handout or a favor. He didn’t raise his voice and he didn’t cuss. He just laid their business bare in the street for everyone to hear. With every name he called, the crowd seemed to get smaller and smaller, though nobody had left. The men he named, they wilted. They looked at the ground, they looked at each other, they looked anywhere but at Stone. The rest of the crowd, the ones he didn’t name, they started shuffling their feet and looking uneasy, maybe wondering what he knew about them.

The great big animal that had been roaring for blood was all broke up now, turned back into a bunch of shamed-looking shopkeepers and farmers that wanted to be somewhere else.

When he was done naming names, he let the silence come back.

“You want to lynch me,” he says, still in that quiet voice. “You want to hang me from a tree. But you ain’t men enough. You’re a pack of dogs that only barks when you’re all together. There ain’t a man in the lot of you. A real man don’t come in a crowd. He comes by himself.”

He looked right over all their heads, like he was looking at something a long way off.

“The Judge was a thief and a coward,” he says. “He took this town’s money and he hid behind his office. He wasn’t the law. He was a maggot that wore a black coat. I done this town a service.”

He dropped his eyes and looked at them again. They was shuffling backwards now, a few at a time. The edges of the crowd was melting away.

“Now you go on home,” Stone says. “Go on home and tell your wives you’re brave men. I’ve got a floor to sweep.”

He turned around, not even waiting to see what they’d do, and went back inside the saloon. The swinging doors flapped shut behind him. The old man Pip gave the crowd one last scared look and scurried in after him.

For a minute, nobody moved. Then the blacksmith, Lem, he turned around without a word and walked away, fast. Then Carter the feed store man went. And then it was like the dam breaking in reverse. Everybody turned and hustled off, not looking at each other. They didn’t run, but they walked fast, peeling off down the side-streets. In about two minutes, the whole street was empty except for me and a couple of stray dogs and the bottle somebody had throwed. It was quieter than Sunday morning.

I stood there for a spell, just looking at the saloon doors. It was a powerful strange thing to see. A whole mob, full of whisky and righteousness, all ready to kill a man, and he just talked them down. He didn’t shoot nobody, didn’t even threaten to. He just held up a mirror to them, and they couldn’t stand to look. It was more frightening than Colonel Sherburn’s pistol, in a way. Sherburn told them what they were; Stone showed them.

Pretty soon the Sheriff come moseying down the street. He was an old man, Sheriff Bell, with a big drooping mustache and sad eyes. He hadn’t been anywhere near the crowd. He walked up to the saloon and stood there looking at the doors a minute, just like I was. Then he sighed, a big long sigh, and pushed one of the doors open and went in.

I was powerful curious, so I snuck up to one of the side windows where the shutter was broke and peeked in. The saloon was dark and smelled of stale beer. Sheriff Bell was standing just inside the door, with his hat in his hands. Stone was behind the bar, wiping it down with a rag. He didn’t look up.

“That ain’t for you to decide,” the Sheriff says. “He was a duly elected judge. You can’t just go around breaking their necks in the town square. It ain’t civilized.”

“That may be,” the Sheriff says, and he shifted his feet. “But the law is the law. The circuit prosecutor is due in town next week. The respectable folks, the ones who wasn’t in that mob, they’re going to be demanding I do something. They’re scared of you, Henry. And they’re more scared of a town with no rules at all.”

Stone didn’t say nothing. He just looked at him.

“I got to take you in,” the Sheriff says, and he looked miserable about it. “I ain’t got a choice.”

I figured right then there’d be another killing. I figured Stone would reach under the bar and pull out a shotgun and that would be the end of old Sheriff Bell.

The town was quiet the rest of the day. It was like everybody was holding their breath. People stayed indoors mostly. You’d see a face at a window, but the streets was mostly empty. The news got around that Stone was locked up in the jail, and it made people feel a little braver, I reckon. By suppertime, you started to hear fellers talking on their front porches, their voices low at first, then getting a little louder. I made up my mind I’d go to the circus. There was a circus come to town that morning, and I’d seen the bills posted up. I reckoned a circus would be a heap more cheerful.

So I went on down to the edge of town where the circus tent was. It was a big white tent, and you could hear the band playing inside. I had a dollar, so I paid my fifty cents and went in. It was packed full of people, all the country folks and town folks that warn’t mixed up in the shooting. The tent was hot and smelled like sawdust and horses and peanuts.

I found a seat up high where I could see everything. The band was playing a lively tune, and pretty soon a man in a black suit with a long whip comes out and cracks it, and says, “Ladies and gentlemen!” and then a lot of horses come trotting into the ring with men and women in spangled clothes on their backs. They rode around and around, standing up, and jumping from one horse to another. Then a clown come out and tried to ride a mule that kept throwing him off, and everybody laughed fit to kill.

It was a splendid circus. By-and-by a horse comes galloping out, and a beautiful lady in pink tights was standing on his back, blowing kisses to the crowd. She was the loveliest sight. She balanced on one foot, and jumped through paper hoops, and never missed a step. The ringmaster, he kept calling her by a long, fancy name and telling everybody how she was the queen of the arena.

Then, just when she’d galloped out, a man come stumbling into the ring. He was drunk, I reckoned, or pretended to be. He was dressed in shabby old clothes and had a slouch hat on. He kept trying to get into the ring, and one of the circus men would try to push him back, but he’d dodge and stumble back in again. He kept yelling that he was a rider, and he could ride any horse that ever was.

He says, “I’m a cavalryman from the old school! Let me at him! I’ll show you riding!”

The ringmaster got all mad. He says, “Get out of this ring, you drunken fool! This is a respectable performance!”

But the man wouldn’t go. He kept staggering around, and the crowd was just a-hollering with laughter. They thought it was the best part of the show. I felt kind of sorry for the ringmaster, he looked so mortified.

Finally, the ringmaster, he says, “All right, you fool! You want to ride? You’ll ride!” He says to one of the circus men, “Bring out the wild Arabian steed of the desert! This gentleman wishes to perform!”

They brought out a beautiful big horse, all fire and ginger, and it was bucking and rearing. The drunk man, he looks at it and says, “That’s the one! Just give me a leg up!”

The circus men helped him up, laughing at him, and the horse went tearing around the ring, bucking and kicking something awful. The man was hanging on by the mane, with his legs flying every which way. The crowd was just screaming with laughter. I thought for sure he’d be killed. He lost his hat, and then one of his boots went flying.

But then, a funny thing happened. He started to sit up straighter. He got his legs sorted out and pretty soon he was sitting on that horse like he was born there. He started to ride. And my, but he could ride. He stood up on the saddle, and then on the horse’s bare back. He leaned way down and picked a handkerchief up off the ground. He rode backwards. He done everything the pretty lady in the pink tights had done, and more. He was the most gracefulest and skillfulest rider I ever see.

The crowd stopped laughing. They got real quiet, and then they started to clap. And when he was done, he rode up to the ringmaster and made a bow, and the whole tent just exploded with cheering and stomping. It was a wonderful thing to see. I was all excited. I thought he was just a regular drunk from the town, like Boggs, who had wandered in and made a spectacle of himself, but he was part of the show all along. He’d fooled everybody. I thought it was one of the slickest things I ever see.

After the circus was over, I felt a lot better. I went on down to the river and untied the skiff from where I’d hid it, and paddled out to the raft. It was getting on towards evening, and the river was calm and cool.

The duke and the king was on the raft. They’d been in town too, but they hadn’t been to the shooting or the circus. They’d been trying to work up a lecture on temperance or some such rot, but they said the town was too busted and excited to pay for any uplift. They was powerful glum about it.

“Not a single soul, your majesty,” the duke says. “Not one with a lick of interest in the evils of the demon rum. Their minds is all took up with this here murder and the circus.”

The king, he was laying on his back with his head on a coil of rope. He let out a groan. “It’s a low-down, ignorant town, that’s what it is, duke. No appreciation for the higher arts. All they want is blood and foolishness.”

I told them about the circus, and about the drunk rider who wasn’t a drunk. The duke, he set up straight.

“Say, that’s an idea, your majesty,” he says. “That’s a notion. They was fooled, you say? The whole crowd?”

“Every last one of them,” I says. “They thought he was a goner for sure.”

The duke, he started to pace up and down the raft, which warn’t very big, so he had to turn around a lot. He was thinking. “Fooled ‘em, eh? Laughed at him first, and then paid to see him a second time if he’d come back, I’ll be bound.”

“What you gettin’ at, duke?” the king says, lazy-like.

“I’m gettin’ at human nature, your majesty,” the duke says, all excited. “What these Arkansaw lunkheads want is something low-down and ornery. They don’t want Shakespeare. They don’t want temperance. They want to be humbugged. They want to laugh at something they think is foolish, and they’ll pay for the privilege.”

He stopped his pacing and slapped his thigh. “By the ghost of Garrick, I’ve got it! We’ll give ‘em a show they won’t forget.”

“What kind of a show?” I asks.

“A show that’s short on costumes and long on cheek,” the duke says, grinning. “We’ll put out a bill that’ll fetch ‘em. Something with a little mystery to it.”

So he got out a piece of paper and a bit of charcoal from the fire, and he set to work making up a handbill. The king, he got interested now, and he set up to watch. The duke, he wrote for a while, chewing on the end of the charcoal stick, and then he read it out loud.

He’d wrote:

“AT THE COURTHOUSE!
FOR THREE NIGHTS ONLY!
The World-Renowned Tragedians
DAVID GARRICK THE YOUNGER!
AND
EDMUND KEAN THE ELDER!
Of the London and Continental Theatres,
In their Thrilling Tragedy of
THE KING’S CAMELEOPARD
OR
THE ROYAL NONESUCH!!!
Admission 50 cents.”

Then he stopped and looked at the king. “There,” he says. “How’s that for high-toned?”

The king, he frowned. “Cameleopard? What’s a cameleopard?”

“It ain’t anything,” the duke says. “It’s just a word to get ‘em curious. But that ain’t the best part.” He took the charcoal again and down at the bottom he wrote in big, bold letters:

“LADIES AND CHILDREN NOT ADMITTED.”

The king, his eyes bugged out. “By the souls of my ancestors,” he says, his voice all shaky with admiration. “That’s it, duke. That’s the very ticket. ‘Ladies and Children Not Admitted.’ Why, they’ll break the door down to get in. They’ll think it’s the wickedest thing that ever was. But what are we a-gwyne to do for a show?”

“Leave that to me,” the duke says, looking pleased with himself. “It don’t need to be long. Just a minute or two of soul-stirring drama. We’ll practice tomorrow. You, your majesty, will be the star.”

“Me?” the king says, puffing out his chest. “Well, I have trod the boards before. I have a certain presence.”

“You do for a fact,” the duke says. “For this part, you’ll need to get in touch with your inner animal, so to speak. It’s a part that calls for… well, for a lack of adornment.”

The king didn’t quite catch on, but he was all puffed up with the idea of being a star again. I reckoned it was another one of their foolish plans, but that line about “Ladies and Children Not Admitted” did seem pretty fetching. I figured the men in that town was just about ornery enough to fall for it. After seeing them try to lynch a man in broad daylight and then get turned back by a single speech, I reckoned they was ready to be humbugged by just about anything. We was going to stay in that town a few more days, and I had a feeling things warn’t done being interesting yet.

So the next morning we was up early. The king was still puffed up about being the star of a new show, and the duke, he was all business. He says, “First things first, we need to get these bills printed and pasted up all over town. The sooner they’re up, the sooner the pot begins to boil. Huck, you come with me. Your majesty, you can commence your artistic preparations. Find a secluded spot and meditate upon the primal nature of the cameleopard.”

The king, he looked a little confused by that, but he just nodded his head real grand-like and says, “A true artist must have his solitude. I shall commune with the muses.” Then he wandered off towards a thicket of woods down by the river bank, looking mighty important.

Me and the duke went on into town. It was still early and things was quiet. The dust from yesterday’s commotion had mostly settled, and you could see where they’d marked out the spots for Boggs and Sherburn, but folks was walking over them now like it was just regular ground again. We found the printing office. It was a little one-room shack with a shingle out front that said “ARKANSAW SENTINEL & JOB PRINTING.”

Inside, it was stuffy and smelled of ink and old paper. A little man with spectacles on the end of his nose and ink stains all up his arms was hunched over a big flat press. He looked up when we come in, and he didn't look too friendly.

The duke, he takes off his hat and makes a little bow. “Good morning, sir,” he says, smooth as butter. “A fine establishment you have here. The very bedrock of a civilized community. We’ve come on a matter of public edification.”

The printer, he just blinked at him. “What d’you want?” he says.

The duke unrolled the paper with the writing on it. “We require a number of these handbills, my good man. A work of dramatic art is soon to grace your fair city, and we wish to apprise the populace of the coming cultural feast.”

The printer took the paper and read it. His eyebrows went up higher and higher. When he got to the “LADIES AND CHILDREN NOT ADMITTED,” he took off his spectacles and wiped them on his sleeve. He looked at the duke, and then at me, and then back at the duke.

“The Royal Nonesuch?” he says, his voice all dry. “What in tarnation is a Royal Nonesuch?”

“Ah,” says the duke, putting a finger alongside his nose. “That is the mystery. That is the allure. It is a spectacle of such profound and startling realism that it is deemed… unsuitable for the delicate sensibilities of the fairer sex and the innocent minds of youth. It is high tragedy, sir, from the courts of Europe.”

The printer snorted. “Looks like high humbug to me. And I ain’t printing no bill that’s likely to get my press busted up by an angry mob. This town’s had enough trouble for one week.”

The duke, he didn’t miss a beat. He leaned in confidential-like. “Sir,” he says, his voice low. “I perceive you are a man of business, a man of keen intellect. You understand that the common man is a creature of curiosity. He is told he cannot see something, and that is the very thing he must see. This is not humbug; it is psychology. And we, sir, are prepared to pay in advance. In cash.”

He pulled out a couple of dollars from his pocket, which was most of the money we had left, and laid them on the counter. The printer’s eyes sort of followed the money. He looked at the dollars, and then at the handbill again. He chewed on his lip for a minute.

“How many you want?” he says, finally.

“A hundred,” says the duke. “Posted prominently before noon.”

“It’ll cost you,” the printer says, but he picked up the dollars.

So we left him to it and went back towards the raft. When we got to the woods, we heard a terrible groaning and roaring coming from the trees. It sounded like a bear with a bellyache. I was all ready to skin out of there, but the duke just grinned.

“His majesty is rehearsing,” he says.

We pushed through the bushes and there was the king. He’d took off all his clothes except for some raggedy old drawers, and he was down on his hands and knees, crawling around in a circle and letting out these awful moans.

“Is that the cameleopard?” I asks.

“That’s the raw material,” the duke says. “We’ve yet to apply the finishing touches.”

He called out to the king, “Your majesty! Splendid! The pathos is overwhelming! But we need more… more ferocity! And less clothing.”

The king, he stopped his crawling and got up, wiping sweat off his bald head. He was all red in the face and panting. “By thunder, duke, this is demeaning work for a man of my lineage! Crawling about in the dirt like a beast of the field! It ain’t got no dignity.”

“Dignity!” the duke says, throwing his hands up. “This is art! Art scoffs at dignity! Did Garrick trouble himself with dignity when he played the mad king? No! He gave himself over to the role. This, your majesty, is your mad king. Now, the drawers must go. A true cameleopard wears no undergarments.”

The king, he grumbled something awful. “It’s indecent,” he says. “And what if somebody sees?”

“That’s why we are in this secluded bower,” the duke says. “Now, for the adornment.”

He’d brought a bucket with him, and he started mixing things in it. He put in some mud from the riverbank, and some berry juice that was a dark purple color, and then he crumbled up some charcoal from our fire. He stirred it all up with a stick until it was a nasty-looking striped paste.

“What’s that for?” I asks.

“For the stripes,” the duke says, looking proud. “A cameleopard is a creature of magnificent stripes and spots. It lends him an exotic and terrifying appearance. We shall paint his majesty from head to heel.”

Well, it took him the better part of an hour. The king stood there, naked as the day he was born, while the duke painted him all over with that stinking mud-paint. He painted yellowy-mud stripes, and purple blotches, and black spots, until the king didn’t look like a person at all, but something you’d see in a nightmare. It was a dreadful sight. The king kept complaining about the mud being cold and the berry juice being sticky, but the duke just told him to suffer for his art.

When he was done, the duke stood back to admire his work. “Magnificent!” he says. “The very image of savage nobility! Now, your majesty, on all fours. And when I give the signal, you come crawling out, roaring. Not a long roar, mind you. A short, sharp, terrifying roar. Then a crawl across the stage, a final bellow, and back you go. The whole performance should not last above a minute. Leave them wanting more, that’s the soul of theatre.”

The king practiced it a few times, crawling around with his painted stripes and looking miserable. It was the pitifulest thing I ever see. I felt so ashamed of the whole business, I went off and sat by the raft and didn't want to look no more. Making a fool of a whole town of people was one thing, but making a old man paint himself up and crawl around naked for a few dollars felt powerful low down and mean, even for a fraud like the king.

By noon, the handbills was up. We seen them plastered on the courthouse door, and on the tavern, and the hotel. Everywhere you looked, there was “THE ROYAL NONESUCH” in big black letters. And pretty soon, the whole town was buzzing about it. You’d see men get together in little bunches, and one of them would point to the bill, and then they’d all look at the part about “LADIES AND CHILDREN NOT ADMITTED” and they’d nudge each other and whisper and laugh. They was all trying to act like they was too high-toned to be interested in such a thing, but you could see they was just itching to know what it was. I heard one man say to another, “You a-gwyne to see the Nonesuch tonight, Jim?” And the other one, he says, “Well, I dunno. I got chores to do. But I might just wander by, see what all the fuss is about.” They was all hooked, just like the duke said they’d be.

That night, they held the show in the courthouse. It was the same room where the trial would be, I reckoned. They’d pushed all the benches to the walls and hung up a old blanket on a rope for a curtain. The duke stood at the door, taking the money. He’d got a cigar box to put it in, and he was acting very grand and important, telling folks to “step right in for the theatrical event of a lifetime.”

The place was packed. It was mostly the men from the town, the same loafers and shopkeepers and farmers that had been at the lynching. They was all crowded in there, sweating and talking loud, and you could smell the whisky on their breath. They was all trying to pretend they was there by accident, but their eyes was all shining with curiosity. I was hiding in a dark corner by the door, feeling like a thief.

When the duke figured he’d got every last half-a-dollar he could squeeze out of that crowd, he locked the door and come up to the front. He held up his hands for quiet.

“Gentlemen!” he says. “You are about to witness a spectacle that has stunned the crowned heads of Europe! A performance so raw, so potent, that we dare not admit the gentler sexes. Prepare yourselves for… The Royal Nonesuch!”

Then he pulled the blanket aside.

For a second, nothing happened. The room was dead quiet. You could hear men shuffling their feet. Then, from the little side room, there come a low growl. And then the king come out.

He was on all fours, just like he’d practiced, buck naked and painted all over with them ridiculous stripes and spots. The paint was starting to run a little under the hot lights from the tallow candles, which made him look even worse. He crawled out into the middle of the floor, let out a kind of a sad-sounding roar, and then he just crawled around a little bit, looking lost. Then he stopped, lifted his head and give another one of them bellows, and crawled back behind the curtain.

The duke swished the blanket closed. “And that, gentlemen,” he says, with a big sweep of his arm, “was The Royal Nonesuch!”

The whole room was silent as a tomb. Nobody moved. Nobody said nothing. The men just stared at the blanket where the king had disappeared. Their mouths was hanging open. I seen one man look at his neighbor, and his neighbor look at him, and they both looked like they’d been struck by lightning.

Then a big, burly man in the front row, a blacksmith I reckoned by the size of his arms, he gets to his feet.

“Well, I’ll be,” he says, slow and dangerous. “We paid fifty cents for THAT?”

Another man jumped up. “That’s it? That’s the whole show?”

And then the whole crowd was on its feet, and they started to yell. “Humbug!” one of them shouts. “Fraud!” yells another. “We’ve been swindled! Get the tar and feathers!”

They started surging towards the front, and I thought for sure they was going to tear the duke limb from limb. My heart was in my throat, and I was looking for a window to climb out of. But the duke, he was cool as a cucumber. He jumped up on a bench and held up his hands again, looking not scared at all, but real indignant.

“Gentlemen, gentlemen!” he hollered, and his voice was so powerful it cut right through the noise. “Hold on a moment! Are you going to sit there and tell me you’ve been swindled?”

They all yelled, “Yes!”

“And are you going to let the whole town know it?” he asks. “Are you going to let them all laugh at you? Think about it! You’re the first citizens of this town. You came to see this exclusive, high-toned entertainment. Now, what are you going to do? Go out there and tell everyone you got taken for fools by a one-minute show? Tell them you paid fifty cents to see a naked old man crawl on the floor?”

He paused and let that sink in. The men stopped yelling and looked at each other. They was looking mighty sheepish.

“Or,” the duke went on, his voice getting all smooth and friendly, “are you going to go out there and tell them it was the most amazing, thrilling, rip-roaringest show you ever saw in your lives? Are you going to tell your friends and neighbors they’d be fools to miss it? Think of the fun you’ll have tomorrow night, sitting here with them, watching their faces when the Royal Nonesuch appears! Don’t you want them to be in on the joke, too?”

There was a long silence. Then the big blacksmith, he started to grin a little. Then another man let out a chuckle. And then the whole crowd busted out laughing. They wasn’t mad anymore; they was laughing at themselves, and at the idea of getting their friends and neighbors in on the same swindle.

“By jings, he’s right!” the blacksmith yells. “It was the gol-darndest show I ever did see! Worth every cent!”

And everybody started cheering and slapping each other on the back, and they all filed out of the courthouse telling each other how they was going to tell everybody they knew that they just had to come see the Nonesuch tomorrow night.

The duke, he just stood there on the bench, smiling to himself and watching them go. When the last one was out, he hopped down and started counting the money in the cigar box. He had over eighty dollars.

“What did I tell you, Huck?” he says, winking at me. “Psychology. The human race is a curious thing. They’d rather be in on a joke than be the butt of one. We’ll play to a full house tomorrow, I guarantee it.”

And he was right. The next day, the whole town was talking about nothing else. The men that had been there the first night, they went around with a sly look on their faces, telling everybody it was a show that had to be seen to be believed. They wouldn't say what it was, just that it was wild and foreign and worth ten times the price.

So the second night, the courthouse was jammed again, even fuller than before. Men had come in from miles around. Me and the duke and the king played out the whole thing just the same. The duke took the money, the king crawled around naked and painted, and the crowd had that same minute of dead quiet. But this time, the men from the first night was there, and they started cheering and stomping right away, and the new men, not wanting to look like they didn't get it, they started cheering and stomping too. It was a sight to see. They all left happy as clams, planning on bringing more people for the third and final night.

That night we counted the money and we had near two hundred dollars altogether. The king and the duke was drinking whisky and singing and carrying on, they was so pleased with themselves. But I noticed the duke was also looking thoughtful.

“Your majesty,” he says to the king, “I believe our engagement in this particular city is drawing to a close. The well of public enthusiasm is deep, but not bottomless. I suspect that by the third night, the joke may have run its course.”

“Nonsense, duke!” the king says, taking a swig. “We’re a sensation! We could play here for a month!”

“Perhaps,” the duke says. “But I have a feeling the audience on the third night might be bringing more than just their fifty cents admission. I’ve noticed a run on eggs at the general store, and a distinct shortage of overripe vegetables. It’s a sign, your majesty. A theatrical sign that one’s tour is complete.”

So he laid out his plan. The third night, we’d sell the tickets just like before. But we wouldn’t be staying for the performance.

The next night, it was just like the duke predicted. The place was packed to the rafters. You couldn’t have squeezed another man in there with a shoehorn. And I could see what the duke meant. Men had lumpy pockets. I saw one fellow trying to hide a dead cat under his coat. They all had a mean, excited look in their eyes. They wasn’t there for a show; they was there for revenge.

The duke stood at the door, cool as ever, raking in the money. He kept stuffing it into a big canvas sack. He’d smile and bow and say, “A wise choice, sir, a wise choice! You won’t regret it!” I stood behind him, and my insides was all cold and shaky.

When the last man was in, the duke didn’t lock the door. He just give me a nod. He slung the heavy sack of money over his shoulder and says to the crowd, “Gentlemen, if you’ll kindly take your seats! The performance will commence in a moment!”

Then he just walked casual-like out the front door, and I slid out right behind him. We didn’t run. We just walked quick down the street, away from the courthouse. Behind us, we could hear the crowd getting impatient. Somebody started stomping their feet, and then the whole crowd started stomping, like thunder. Then somebody yelled, “Bring on the Nonesuch!”

We turned a corner and started to run. We ran down the dark alleys, our feet pounding in the dirt. We could hear a big crash from back at the courthouse, like a door breaking down, and then a giant roar of anger from a whole town of men who’d finally figured out they’d been had for the third time.

We didn’t stop running till we got to the raft. The king was already there, with his clothes on this time, thank goodness, and he had the raft untied and ready to go. We all jumped aboard and shoved off into the current just as we heard a mob of men yelling and coming down the bank towards the river, carrying torches. Their lights bobbed in the dark, but we was already in the middle of the river, floating silent in the darkness.

They stood on the bank and cursed us, but they couldn’t see us, and pretty soon we was too far downriver to hear them anymore.

The duke, he dropped the money sack on the deck. It made a heavy, clinking sound. He lit a lantern and him and the king sat down and dumped it all out. It was four hundred and sixty-five dollars. They was rich. They laughed and carried on and the king said the duke was the cleverest man in the world, and the duke said the king was the bravest actor.

I didn’t say nothing. I just sat on the edge of the raft and watched the dark banks slide by. We had the money, but it felt dirty. I thought about poor old Boggs laying dead on the floor of the drugstore, and his daughter crying, and Colonel Sherburn walking away so proud, and the big mob that was ready to lynch a man but got turned back with a few words, and then paid all their money to see a naked old fool crawl on the floor. It seemed like the whole world was mean and foolish and ugly. And we was right in the middle of it, floating down the big, quiet river, away from one mess and right on towards the next one.

so away they went, a-hollering and a-whooping for the courthouse square, every man wanting to be the first to lay a hand on Sherburn. It was a solid river of people, and I got swept along with it, not because I wanted to lynch nobody, but because there warn’t no other way to go.

The mob fetched up in front of Colonel Sherburn’s house. It was a nice house, for that town; two stories, with a white picket fence all around a tidy yard full of flowers. It looked too peaceful for what was about to happen. The men bunched up at the gate, shoving and looking over each other’s shoulders, but nobody seemed in a great hurry to be the first one to go in. They was loud when they was all together, but take them one by one and they warn’t so brave.

Pretty soon Colonel Sherburn himself steps out onto the roof of his front porch, holding a double-barrel shotgun, same as if he was expecting company. He didn’t look scared, not one bit. He just looked tired and disgusted. The noise quieted down some, till you could hear men shuffling their feet in the dust.

Sherburn let his eyes travel over the whole crowd, slow and deliberate, and then he give a little smile, but it warn’t the friendly kind.

“The idea of you lynching anybody!” he says, and his voice was as clear and cold as well-water. “It’s pathetic. The idea of you thinking you had pluck enough to lynch a man. Because you’re brave enough to tar and feather poor friendless cast-out women that come along here, did that make you think you had grit enough to lay your hands on a man? Why, a man’s safe in the hands of ten thousand of your kind, as long as it’s daytime and you’re not behind him.”

He took a breath, and nobody in the crowd made a sound. They just stood there and took it.

“Do I know you?” he says. “I know you clear through. I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average all around. The average man’s a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a humble spirit to bear it. In the South one man, all by himself, has got to go out in the middle of the street and watch another man pump him full of lead, and as long as he’s got his family and his friends at his back, he’ll do it. But he’ll do it by himself. He won’t get down on his knees and beg for half a man’s chance. A mob like this, it don’t fight with courage you can see. It goes by night, with masks on.”

He looked them all over again, one by one. “Now the thing for you to do is to droop your tails and go home and crawl in a hole. If any real lynching’s going to be done it will be done in the dark, Southern fashion; and when they come they’ll bring their masks, and fetch a man along. Now leave, and take your half-a-man with you.”

He cocked his shotgun, and you never see a crowd melt away so fast. They didn’t run, exactly, but they just sort of dissolved, turning and walking off in twos and threes, looking at the ground. In a minute the street was empty. I slid on out of there myself, and didn’t look back. It was a curious thing. One man had faced down a whole town, just with words. It made what the duke and the king done seem like cheap folderol.

We laid low on the raft for a day or two after that, just drifting. The duke and the king, they was in high feather, counting their money and planning what they’d do with it. The king, he wanted to start a new church where folks paid for pardons, but the duke said religion was getting to be mighty crowded territory.

“No, your majesty,” the duke says, a-combing the whiskers on his chin, “what we require is a new angle, a fresh assault on the ramparts of human credulity. Theatricals is fine, and so is salvation, but we’ve wrung them rags dry in this particular stretch of river. We need to tap into a deeper well of foolishness.”

He sat thinking for a spell, looking up at the stars. We was tied up in a little willow thicket off the main channel, and the night was thick and quiet, except for the frogs. Jim was patching one of his shirts by the light of the lantern, and I was just laying on my back, watching the sparks from the fire drift up into the dark.

“Spirits,” the duke says, all of a sudden. He slapped his knee. “That’s it! Not the kind you drink, your grace, but the kind that rattle chains and moan in the attic.”

The king looked puzzled. “Spirits? You mean to play we’re ha’nts?”

“Better,” says the duke. “We ain’t the spirits, we’re the ones that can talk to ’em. Spiritualism, they call it. All the rage back East. Folks pay a pretty penny to have a word with their dear departed Aunt Matilda.”

The king’s eyes lit up. He was always partial to a line of work that involved dressing up and putting on airs. “By the great hook-nosed Cæsar, duke, it’s an inspiration! A new testament of fakery! I can see it now. I shall be the vessel, the sacred conduit to the world beyond!” He struck a pose, with one hand on his chest and the other pointing at the sky.

“You’ll be the medium, yes,” the duke says, looking a little sour at the king stealing his thunder. “And I shall be your manager, a man of science, a professor of the metaphysical arts, documenting your incredible gift for the betterment of mankind. We’ll need a town, a recent and wealthy bereavement, and a whole lot of dark rooms.”

So that was the new plan. For the next few days, whilst we drifted, they practiced. The king would go into a “trance,” which mostly consisted of him rolling his eyes back in his head and talking in a high, squeaky voice that was supposed to be a ghost. The duke made up a lot of fancy words like “ectoplasm” and “trans-etheric communication,” and wrote them down on a piece of paper so he wouldn’t forget. It was all so ridiculous I had to stuff my hat in my mouth to keep from laughing out loud.

Jim, he didn’t think it was funny. He’d sit and listen, with his face all troubled, and shake his head.

“Huck,” he says to me one night when the frauds was asleep, “dis truck heah… it ain’t right. It’s one thing to be a-prancin’ on a stage for fools dat pays to see it. It’s another thing to be meddlin’ with folks’ grief. De dead is de dead. Dey deserves to rest easy.”

I knowed he was right. It felt different from the Royal Nonesuch. That was just making fools of a bunch of loafers and toughs. This felt like robbing a graveyard. But there warn’t nothing I could do about it. When the duke and the king got an idea in their heads, you just had to ride it out and hope you didn’t get drowned in the process.

We come to a town called Laconia, a sleepy-looking place with big white houses set back from the river under old oak trees. The duke went ashore first, to what he called “reconnoiter the terrain.” He come back a few hours later, rubbing his hands together and looking mighty pleased.

“Gentlemen,” he says, “Providence has smiled upon our humble enterprise. The richest man in the county, a one Mister Silas Kincaid, passed from this mortal coil but three weeks ago. Fell off his horse. Left behind a considerable fortune and a widow who, I’m told, is inconsolable with grief. The fruit, my friends, is ripe for the plucking.”

The next day, the king and the duke dressed up in their black suits, the ones they used for the funeral back at the Wilkses’ before we got there. The duke had a leather satchel full of papers and charts of the stars, and the king carried a big Bible, though he held it upside down till I pointed it out to him. They looked solemn and important. They called themselves Professor Alistair Finch and the Reverend Brother Ebenezer.

They went into town and rented a room at the hotel, and the duke put an advertisement in the local paper, a little hand-bill he’d printed up himself on his little press. It said:

COMMUNE WITH THE BEYOND!
Professor Alistair Finch, noted scholar of the Unseen World,
presents for a limited engagement
THE REVEREND BROTHER EBENEZER
whose miraculous gifts allow for Direct Conversation with those
who have passed through the Veil!
Are you bereaved? Do you seek comfort? Do you long for one last word?
The SPIRITS are waiting.

They held a little show in the hotel parlor that night. It cost a quarter to get in. There was about twenty people there, mostly curious old ladies and a few fellows that looked like they’d come to scoff. The duke give a long speech about magnetic fields and spiritual vibrations, and then they turned down the lamps.

The king sat down in a chair and commenced to groan and twitch. Pretty soon he was talking in that silly squeaky voice, pretending to be the dead mother of one of the old ladies. He was saying things like, “Martha, my child, I am at peace, and the gardens here are beautiful.” The old lady started to cry, and the other folks looked impressed. Then the king, he pretended to be old Judge Peters, who’d been dead for ten years, and said he knowed that one of the scoffers, a fellow named Jed, had stole a pig from his neighbor two years back. Jed turned red as a beet and slunk out of the room, and the rest of the crowd looked plain scared. The duke had been in the saloon all afternoon, you see, listening to gossip.

The show was a big success. The next day, the whole town was talking about the amazing Reverend Brother and the wise Professor. And just like the duke planned, a letter comes to the hotel. It was from Mrs. Kincaid, the rich widow, begging them to come to her house for a private session.

That’s when they decided they needed my help, and Jim’s.

“For a private consultation of this magnitude,” the duke says, “we require… atmospheric effects. A disembodied rap upon the table, a ghostly moan from the hallway. Huck, you’re small and quick, you can hide under the sofa. Jim, your voice has a natural, mournful timbre. You will be our spectral chorus.”

Jim put his foot down. “No, sah, I ain’t gwyne do it. I ain’t gwyne mock no grievn’ woman. It’s bad luck, and it’s jes’ plain wrong.”

The king’s face got ugly. “Wrong? You listen to me, you runaway. You belong to me, bought and paid for. You’ll do as you’re told, or I’ll sell you down to the first man I see for twenty dollars, and you’ll wish you was a ghost for real.”

Jim looked at the king, and then he looked at me, and his shoulders slumped. He seen there was no way out of it. I felt sick to my stomach. This was worse than I thought.

That night we went up to the Kincaid house. It was the biggest house I’d ever seen up close, with tall white columns like a courthouse and a lawn so big you could have a horse race on it. A servant in a black coat let us in. The inside was dark and grand, full of heavy furniture covered in cloths, and portraits on the wall with eyes that seemed to follow you.

Mrs. Kincaid was waiting for us in the parlor. She was a thin, pale woman with big, sad eyes, dressed all in black. Her son was with her, a young man about twenty-five with a hard jaw and a suspicious look about him. He didn’t look like he believed in spirits one bit.

“Professor, Reverend,” the widow says, her voice all shaky. “Thank you for coming. I… I just need to know if my Silas… if he is at peace.”

“Madam,” the duke says, bowing low, “the veil is thin tonight. If your husband’s spirit is reachable, Brother Ebenezer will find him.”

They sat down at a big, heavy mahogany table in the center of the room. The duke had me sneak in beforehand and hide under a big sideboard against the wall, behind a long curtain. Jim was outside, under an open window, with instructions to make a low moaning sound when the duke coughed three times.

They turned down the lamps till the room was almost pitch black, with just one candle flickering on the table. The widow and her son sat on one side, and the king and the duke on the other. For a long time, nothing happened. The only sound was the widow sniffling into a handkerchief.

Then the king started in with his groaning. He shivered and shook and spoke in tongues, a lot of gibberish that didn’t mean nothing.

“He is entering the trance,” the duke whispered in an awed voice. “The connection is being established.”

The king’s head lolled back. “I… I see a light,” he mumbled. “A man… on a horse… he is smiling…”

Mrs. Kincaid let out a little gasp. “Silas! Oh, Silas, is it you?”

“He hears you, madam,” the duke said. “Speak to him.”

“Silas, my love,” she cried, “are you happy? Are you waiting for me?”

The king, in his squeaky ghost voice, says, “Adelaide… my darling Adelaide… the pain is gone. I am in a place of light.”

The widow was sobbing for joy now. Even in the dim light I could see tears running down her face. It was pitiful to watch. The son, though, he just sat there stiff as a board, his eyes narrowed, watching the king.

“Ask him something only he would know,” the son says, his voice sharp. “Ask him where he left the deed to the north pasture.”

The duke coughed. “The spirits are not concerned with such earthly matters, young man. It is a trial for them to communicate at all.”

But the king, he was quicker. He must have heard something in town. “The deed…” the squeaky voice said. “It is… in my hunting jacket… the oiled one… in the back of the wardrobe.”

The son’s eyes went wide. He looked at his mother, and for the first time, a little bit of doubt crept onto his face. He’d been bested.

The duke saw his chance. “The connection is strong,” he says. “The spirit wishes to impart a message of great importance.” He coughed three times, loud and clear. That was the signal for Jim.

We all waited. Nothing happened. The only sound was the candle sputtering.

The duke coughed again, louder this time, a real hacking sound. Still nothing. The king was starting to look uneasy, even in his trance. I could feel the sweat trickling down my own back. I peeked through a crack in the curtain. Outside, in the moonlight, I could just make out Jim’s shape, standing by the window. He was just standing there, with his head hung low, not moving. He wasn’t going to do it.

“What was that?” the son said, suddenly. “That coughing. It sounds like a signal.”

“Nonsense, my boy,” the duke said, trying to sound smooth, but his voice was a little tight. “A tickle in my throat, nothing more. The spiritual energies in the room are quite taxing.”

He stomped his foot on the floor, hard. A new signal. This one was for me. I was supposed to make three sharp raps on the underside of the sideboard.

I had my knuckles all ready. I looked at the poor widow, who was leaning forward, hanging on every word, her face full of hope and grief. I thought of what Jim said. It was one thing to fool a bunch of loafers. It was another to trample on a person’s heart when it was already broken. My hand wouldn’t move. I couldn’t do it.

The duke stomped his foot again, so hard the floorboards shook. “A powerful manifestation!” he announced. “Did you feel it? The very foundations tremble!”

But the son was already on his feet. He was quicker than I thought. He strode over to the window and threw it open wide. There was Jim, standing right there in the flowerbed, looking like he’d been caught robbing a chicken coop.

“Well, well,” the son says, his voice like ice. “What have we here? A spectral chorus?”

He turned and grabbed the lamp, turning it up high. The room flooded with light. The king sat there blinking, his trance gone, looking old and foolish. The duke had gone pale as a sheet.

Mrs. Kincaid looked from Jim in the window to the king at the table, and her face, which had been so full of hope, just crumpled. The sob that come out of her then warn’t one of joy. It was the most awful, heartbroken sound I ever heard.

“You… you frauds,” the son snarled. He took a step towards the duke. “You miserable, ghoulish thieves.”

That was all the duke needed. He grabbed the satchel and bolted for the door. “A misunderstanding!” he yelled. “A crossed spiritual wire!” The king was right behind him, moving faster than I’d ever seen him move, knocking over a little table with a vase on it.

I didn’t wait for an invitation. I scrambled out from under the sideboard and shot out the door after them. The son was yelling for the servants, and I could hear them coming, shouting and running down the halls. We busted out the front door into the night and didn’t stop running. We tore across that big lawn, the king puffing and wheezing, and the duke cursing a blue streak, blaming the king, blaming Jim, blaming me. We could hear them coming after us, a whole pack of them with lanterns and dogs barking.

We didn't head for the hotel. We ran straight for the willows where the raft was hid. Jim had got there first; he must have run soon as the son spotted him. He was already untying the rope, his hands shaking.

“Hurry, Huck, hurry!” he panted.

We all piled onto the raft just as the mob come bursting through the trees onto the riverbank. They had torches now, and we could see their faces, twisted with anger.

“There they are!” somebody shouted, and a rock flew past my head and splashed in the water.

Jim and I shoved with the poles, and the raft slid out from the bank and got caught by the current. It swung us out into the middle of the river, into the blessed dark where their torchlight couldn’t reach. They stood on the bank hollering and cursing at us, but we was safe. We was floating away again, leaving another town and another mess behind us.

For a long time nobody said nothing. Then the king, he turned on Jim.

“You! You worthless, black traitor!” he bellowed, his voice shaking with rage. “You cost us a fortune! A fortune! I had her eating out of my hand! I ought to throw you in this river right now!”

“It was yo’ own fault for being a fool!” the duke shot back, just as mad. “Your squeaky little ghost voice sounds like a pig stuck in a fence! And your signals! Stomping on the floor like a jackass! You gave the whole game away!”

They went at it then, cussing and blaming each other, and for a minute I thought they was going to come to blows right there on the raft. But then they both turned on Jim again, because he was the easiest to blame.

“He’s the one,” the duke says, pointing a finger at Jim. “Disobedient property. We should have sold him when we had the chance.”

Jim didn’t say nothing. He just stood by the steering-oar, his back to them, looking out at the black water. I went and stood next to him.

“He didn’t do nothing wrong,” I said. My voice was shaky but I said it loud enough for them to hear. “He just… he couldn’t do it. It warn’t right.”

The duke give me a long, cold look. “And you, under the sideboard? I didn’t hear no rapping from your corner, neither. Seems we got a mutiny on our hands, your majesty.”

The king just grunted and went to find his jug. He and the duke sat down together on the other end of the raft, muttering to each other and shooting us dark looks. They left us alone for the rest of the night.

So me and Jim, we stayed by the steering-oar and kept watch. The river was wide and peaceful, and the stars was thick as spilt salt overhead. After a while, Jim puts his hand on my shoulder.

“You was brave, Huck,” he says, his voice low and soft. “Standin’ up to ’em like dat. I… I’s proud of you.”

I didn’t say nothing. I just looked out at the river. We hadn’t got a cent out of that town, and now the king and the duke was madder at us than ever. It was bound to lead to more trouble. But for the first time in a long time, I didn’t feel dirty. Lying on your back and watching the stars, that’s one thing. But standing up, in the dark, next to your friend, that was something else altogether. It felt quiet, and clean, and right.

so away they went, and me right along with them. Not because I thought lynching was a good idea, or even a bad one; I didn’t have much of a thought about it at all. It was just that there was a powerful lot of excitement, and when there’s excitement a boy is bound to follow it, like a piece of wood in a flood.

The crowd was a solid lump of men, mostly, surging down the main street. They was all talking at once, and some was waving their fists and some was just hollering for the sake of making a noise. The lanky man with the stovepipe hat was right in the middle of it, still carrying his crooked-handled cane. He warn’t leading, exactly, but everybody was sort of clumping around him, like he was the stone and they was the moss. He was still telling everybody how it happened, only now he’d added a few things. He was saying Sherburn laughed an evil laugh before he pulled the trigger, and that Boggs had begged for his daughter on his knees, which I know for a fact he hadn’t. But the crowd didn’t care; they ate it up and yelled louder.

They went straight for Sherburn’s house. It warn’t hard to find. It was the best house in the town, a two-story white frame house with a big porch all the way around and a neat white picket fence. There was green shutters on the windows and a yard full of rosebushes. It looked quiet and proud, and it didn’t seem to care a bit that a whole mob of angry men was coming to tear it down.

The crowd washed up against the fence like a wave hitting a breakwater. They stopped there, bunching up and pushing from behind, but the ones in the front didn’t seem so eager to climb over. They just stood there yelling things.

“Come out, Sherburn! Come out and take what’s comin’ to you!”
“Murderer!”
“We’ll show you what Arkansaw justice is!”

They hollered and cussed and shook their fists at the house, but the house just sat there. The door was shut, the windows was shut, and for a minute I reckoned maybe Sherburn had lit out the back way. That seemed like what a smart man would do.

Then the front door opened.

Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the porch. He didn’t have a coat on, just his shirt and trousers, and he was holding a double-barreled shotgun. He didn’t raise it, just held it comfortable-like in the crook of his arm. He walked to the edge of the porch and stood there, looking down at them. He warn’t smiling and he warn’t frowning. His face was just calm and still, like it was carved out of stone.

The yelling kind of trickled to a stop. The men in the front shuffled their feet and looked at the men behind them, and the men behind them craned their necks to see what was happening. A dog barked somewhere down the street, and it was so quiet you could hear it plain.

Sherburn let the quiet stretch out for a long time. Then he spoke, and his voice warn’t loud, but it was clear and cold as well-water, and it carried over the whole crowd.

“You can’t lynch a man in the daytime,” he says, slow and easy. “You can’t do it. You’re cowards.”

Nobody said nothing. They just stared up at him.

“You pretend to be brave,” he went on, his eyes moving over the crowd, looking at each man one by one. “You get your courage out of a bottle and you get it out of a crowd. But a man, a real man, he don’t need a crowd. He stands on his own.”

He took a step forward, right to the railing. “The idea of you lynching anybody! It’s a joke. You brought a man with you to tell you how to do it, that long-geared imitation of a man down there with the ridiculous hat. And what did he do? He acted it out. He played a part. That’s all you are. A bunch of boys playing a game.”

The lanky fellow, he sort of shrunk down into his collar, and tried to push his hat down further on his head, but it was already as far as it would go.

“Why don’t you lynch a man in the daytime?” Sherburn says again. “Because you’re afraid. You’re afraid of the daylight that shows your faces. You need the dark. You need masks. So you can pretend it ain’t you. So you can go home to your wives and children and pretend you’re decent men.”

He looked them all over again, his gaze sharp enough to cut. “Your newspapers talk about Southern chivalry. Where is it? Your preachers talk about bravery. Where is it? It’s in your talk, and that’s all. You shout and you threaten, but when it comes time to do something, you get one man to do it for you, and then you hide behind him.”

He shifted the shotgun in his arm a little. “Now, which one of you is the man? Who’s coming over that fence? I’m ready for you. But you ain’t coming. You’re going to turn around and you’re going to slink away, and you’re going to tell everybody what you was *going* to do. But you ain’t going to do nothing.”

He stared hard at them. “The average man’s a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a humble spirit to bear it. In the South one man, all by himself, has to stand up to a mob of you. Now, listen to me. Drop the noise. Go home. Break up and go home, and don’t ever talk about lynching a man in the daylight again.”

He rested the butt of the shotgun on the porch floor, still holding the barrels, and just stood there, waiting.

For a long time, nobody moved. The whole street was dead silent. I could hear my own heart thumping in my ears. I was hiding behind a big sycamore tree across the way, and I peeked around the trunk to see. The men in the crowd looked down at their feet. They looked at each other. Somebody in the back coughed. Then a couple of men at the very edge of the crowd, they turned around slow and just walked away. Then another one. And another.

It was like a barrel of molasses that got a hole punched in the bottom; it didn’t all run out at once, but it was leaking for sure, and pretty soon there warn’t going to be nothing left. The big solid lump of a mob just sort of dissolved. Men peeled off in twos and threes, not looking at Sherburn, not looking at each other, just heading off down the street, their shoulders all slumped. In a minute, the whole street was empty except for the lanky man. He stood there for a second, looking at his cane, and then he looked up at Sherburn, and then he turned and walked off too, faster than the others.

Colonel Sherburn watched them all go. When the last man was out of sight, he picked up his shotgun, turned around without another look, and went back inside his house. The door clicked shut.

I stayed behind my tree for a while. The town was quieter than I’d ever heard it. The excitement was all gone, and what was left felt sour and empty. I thought about Boggs, and his daughter screaming. And I thought about Colonel Sherburn on his porch, and how he was right about them all being cowards. But he was a murderer, too. He shot Boggs down when Boggs was being hurried away and had his hands up. It was all twisted up and I couldn’t make no sense of it. It seemed like being a man was a mighty complicated and dangerous thing.

I slid out from behind the tree and started making my way back through the town. I didn’t want to be there no more. The sun was hot and the dust was thick and the whole place felt sick. I kept to the back alleys, past sleeping dogs and piles of trash. I wanted to get back to the river. The river was waiting, like it always was. It didn’t have no opinions and it didn’t get into fights. It just flowed.

It took me the better part of an hour to sneak back to where we’d tied up the raft. It was hid good in a little cove thick with willows, and for a minute I was scared somebody had found it. But it was there, bobbing easy on the water. And Jim was there, sitting on the edge, looking worried.

When he seen me, his face lit up. “Huck! I was gettin’ pow’ful uneasy. I heard all dat hollerin’ from town, and den it got all quiet. I was afeared dey’d done somethin’ to you.”

“I’m all right, Jim,” I says, and I climbed aboard. I was so tired I just about fell down. I laid on my back on the rough planks and looked up at the green leaves of the willows.

“What was it, Huck? What was all de fuss?”

I told him how they tried to get Boggs to go home, and how his daughter came running. I told him about the shooting, and how Boggs fell, and the Bibles they put on him, and the way his chest lifted them up and down till he died. I told him about the crowd at the window, and the lanky man acting it all out, and how everybody decided to lynch Sherburn.

Jim listened, his eyes getting wider and wider. He didn’t say nothing, just listened.

Then I told him about the mob at Sherburn’s house, and how Sherburn came out with his shotgun and faced them all down. I told him the things Sherburn said, about how they was all cowards.

When I was all done, it was quiet for a long time. Jim just sat there, looking out at the river.

The raft slid out from the willows into the main channel, and the town and all its meanness started to fall away behind us. We didn’t see no lights from Sherburn’s house, or from anywhere else much. It was like the whole town had gone to bed early, ashamed of itself.

Jim’s eyes got big. “Lynch him? For shootin’ dat man Boggs?”

“That’s what they’re saying.”

“But Huck, dey was all laughin’ at Boggs jes’ a little while ago. Dey didn’ think nuthin’ of him. Now dey’s ready to kill for him?”

“Well,” I says, “I don’t reckon it’s so much about Boggs anymore. I think they’re just full of whisky and excitement, and they got a taste for meanness today.”

We sat there quiet for a bit, listening to the noise from the town. It rose up in a big wave of sound, a lot of yelling and then a different kind of sound, a single voice cutting through it all, sharp and cold like a razor. Then the yelling kind of sputtered out, and it got real quiet. Then we heard the sound of feet, a lot of them, shuffling and scraping, not running, but just walking away, ashamed-like.

“Sounds like the lynching is over,” I says.

“Dey do it so fas’?” Jim says, his voice all shaky.

“I don’t think they done it at all,” I says. “I think Colonel Sherburn done it to them.”

And so it was. A little while later a feller come down to the river to get some water, and he was telling another feller all about it. He said the mob went to Sherburn’s house and was fixing to break down the door when Sherburn comes out onto the roof of his front porch with a shotgun in his hands. He just stood there and let them yell themselves out. Then he made them a speech. He called them cowards, and told them they didn’t have the sand to lynch a man in the daylight. He said a real man wouldn’t be part of a mob, and that they was just the tail end of a man, hiding behind each other’s backs. He told them that the man they was so brave about lynching now, they was laughing at an hour before. He just peeled the hide right off them with his words, and not a one of them had anything to say back. When he was done, he told them to go home and crawl in a hole, and they done it. They just broke up and slunk away.

I told Jim, and he shook his head. “Dat Sherburn, he a cold man, Huck. A cold, proud man. But he ain’t no fool.”

“No,” I says. “He ain’t no fool.”

We judged it was safe to wait till dark. The town felt sick and sour, and we wanted no more of it. When the last of the light was gone and the first stars was pricking out in the sky, we untied the raft and let the current take us. It was a relief to be back on the water, with the wide, dark river all around us. The air was clean out here, and the only sounds was the gentle slap of the water and the croaking of the frogs. It felt like we’d escaped from a prison.

We drifted on for two or three nights, mostly sleeping in the day and traveling when it was dark. It was a peaceful, lazy time. We’d fish a little, and swim when it got too hot, and just let the raft drift. There warn’t nothing to see but the river, and the trees, and the sky. And there warn’t nothing to hear but the river, and the birds, and Jim and me talking. It was splendid.

But our vittles was getting low. We was down to our last bit of bacon and a handful of cornmeal.

“Jim,” I says, “we got to land somewheres and get some supplies. We can’t live on catfish forever.”

“Dat’s a fact, Huck. But where we gwyne to land? After dat las’ town, I ain’t got much appetite for what folks is like on de shore.”

“I know,” I says. “But we got to. Maybe we can find a little one-horse town, a place that ain’t got no colonels or lynch mobs in it.”

The next morning, we saw a little town tucked into a bend in the river. It warn’t much to look at. Just a few dozen houses, a little church with a crooked steeple, and a steamboat landing that looked like it hadn’t seen a steamboat in a year. The whole place looked sleepy and forgotten, like it was taking a nap in the morning sun.

“That looks like the place,” I says. “It’s too small for much trouble. I’ll go in and buy some bacon and cornmeal and coffee. You stay here with the raft, hid in the willows.”

Jim didn’t like it, but he knowed we had to. So I took the canoe and a dollar we had saved up, and paddled over to the landing.

The town was even quieter up close. There was a general store with a sagging porch, a blacksmith shop that was cold and empty, and a few houses with their shutters closed. There warn’t a soul in sight. It was plumb spooky. I tied up the canoe and walked up the main street, which was just a dirt track with grass growing in the middle. The only sound was my own feet on the dirt and the buzzing of flies.

I was beginning to think the whole town was dead when I heard a sound. It was singing. A whole lot of people singing, coming from the little church with the crooked steeple. It was a hymn, but not like any I’d ever heard. It was loud and full of a kind of wild joy, with people clapping their hands and stomping their feet.

Well, I was curious, so I crept up to the church and peeked in through a broken window pane. The place was packed to the rafters. It looked like every man, woman, and child in the county was in there. The air was hot and thick, and smelled of sweat and sawdust and cheap perfume. They was all sitting on rough wooden benches, their faces turned towards the front, where a man was standing on a little platform.

He was a big man, with a mane of white hair that fell down over his shoulders and a long white beard that covered his chest. He was wearing a shiny black suit that was too tight for him, and he was sweating something awful. He had his eyes closed and his face turned up to the ceiling, and he was leading the singing with a voice that was as big as he was.

When the song was over, he held up his hands, and the whole place went silent.

“Brothers and sisters!” he booms, his voice filling up the little church. “Are you washed in the blood? Are you cleansed of your sins? Have you cast out the devil that lurks in your hearts?”

A chorus of “Amen!” and “Hallelujah!” went up from the crowd.

“Because the devil is here!” he roars. “He is here in this valley of tears! He is in the whisky bottle! He is in the deck of cards! He is in the covetous heart and the lying tongue! He is walking amongst you, seeking whom he may devour!”

He started pacing back and forth on the platform, his big boots thumping on the wood. He was a master showman, I had to give him that. He’d lean forward and whisper, and the whole crowd would lean forward with him, holding their breath. Then he’d rear back and bellow, and they’d all jump.

“I have seen the fires of perdition!” he yells, pointing a long, bony finger at the congregation. “I have seen the pits of hell, where the sinners gnash their teeth and the worms do not die! And I have seen the angel of the Lord, who told me to come to you, the good people of this benighted land, and offer you the sweet balm of salvation!”

He went on like that for a good long while, painting a picture of hell that was so real you could almost smell the brimstone. He told stories about sinners who had died in their beds and been dragged down to the fiery pits, and about gamblers who had looked down at their hands and seen the devil’s own face grinning back at them from the cards. He had women sobbing into their handkerchiefs and strong men looking pale and scared.

I seen this kind of thing before, at camp-meetings and the like. It was a regular performance. This feller was a professional, though. He knowed just how to play that crowd, like a fiddler playing a fiddle.

Then he changed his tune. His voice got all soft and full of tears.

“But there is hope!” he cries, his voice breaking. “There is redemption! There is a fount of grace, flowing for the thirsty soul! All you have to do is come and drink! Confess your sins! Lay down your burdens! The Lord is waiting with open arms!”

He opened his own arms wide, and a couple of women in the front row started wailing like their hearts was breaking. Then one of them, a young girl with braids, gets up and stumbles down the aisle, crying and calling out, “I’m a sinner! Oh, Lord, save me!”

She throws herself down at the preacher’s feet, and he lays a hand on her head.

“The Lord has heard you, my child!” he says, his voice all gentle now. “Your sins are washed away!”

Well, that started it. It was like a dam had broke. People started pouring down the aisle, weeping and confessing all sorts of things. One feller said he’d stolen a chicken. Another said he’d coveted his neighbor’s ox. An old woman confessed to having evil thoughts about the baker’s wife. It was a regular flood of sin.

The preacher took it all in. He’d pat one on the head, and hug another, and pray over them all, his big voice rolling out the words of absolution. His assistants, two scrawny fellers in dusty coats, was working the crowd, too, encouraging people to come forward, and passing around collection plates. I noticed the plates was already getting heavy with coins and bills.

I watched for a while, and it was better than a circus. But my stomach was rumbling, and I remembered what I’d come for. I slipped away from the church and went back to the general store. The door was unlocked, so I went in. The place was empty. The counter was covered in a thick layer of dust, and the shelves was half-bare. It looked like the owner had gone to the revival with everybody else.

I felt a little bad about it, but necessity is a powerful persuader. I found some bacon, and a sack of cornmeal, and a small tin of coffee. I took them up to the counter and put my dollar down on top of the dust. I reckoned that was a fair price, maybe even a little more than fair. Then I let myself out and went back down to the canoe.

I was just about to push off when I seen the preacher and his two helpers come out of the church. The congregation was still inside, singing another hymn, but the three of them was heading for a wagon that was hitched up behind the church. They was laughing and slapping each other on the back. The preacher had took off his coat and was wiping his face with a big handkerchief.

“Well, Brother Absalom,” one of the scrawny fellers says, “that was a mighty harvest for the Lord’s vineyard today.” He was holding a canvas bag that clinked when he moved.

“The Lord provides for his faithful servants,” the preacher says, and he lets out a big laugh. It warn’t the holy, gentle laugh he’d been using in the church. It was a loud, greasy kind of laugh. “And these poor sheep was ripe for the shearing.”

He takes the bag from the other feller and peeks inside. “Is this all of it?”

“Every last cent,” the feller says. “They’re a poor flock, Brother Absalom, but a generous one.”

“They are indeed,” the preacher says. He tucks the bag into his coat. “Now let’s be on our way before the spirit of gratitude wears off and they start asking for their money back.”

They climbed into the wagon and rode off, heading out of town on the inland road, leaving a cloud of dust behind them. They didn’t even look back.

I sat there in the canoe for a minute, thinking. That preacher, he was a kind of a colonel, too, in his own way. He hadn’t used a gun, but he’d used words and fear, and he’d robbed that whole town just as sure as if he’d held them all up at gunpoint. And the crowd, they was just as foolish as the one that had tried to lynch Sherburn. They’d let this feller fill their heads with fire and brimstone, and then they’d paid him for the privilege of being scared.

I paddled back to the raft, feeling mighty low. It seemed like everywhere you went, there was a feller like Sherburn or a feller like Brother Absalom, ready to take advantage of the fools. And there was always plenty of fools to be taken advantage of.

I got back to the raft and told Jim all about it. He listened, shaking his head slow-like.

“So he jes’ took their money an’ run?”

“That’s about the size of it,” I says.

“An’ dey thinks dey’s saved?”

“I reckon they do. For a little while, anyway. Till they find out their money’s gone.”

Jim was quiet for a long time, looking out over the water. The raft was drifting along nice and easy, and the sun was warm on our backs.

“Huck,” he says finally, “it seem to me dat dere’s two kinds o’ men dat does de most harm in dis worl’. Dere’s de proud, cold ones like dat Colonel, who thinks dey’s better’n everybody else an’ can do as dey please. An’ den dere’s de slick, smilin’ ones like dat preacher, who pretends to be your friend while he’s pickin’ your pocket.”

“I reckon you’re right, Jim,” I says.

“An’ de rest of us,” he goes on, “de common folks, we’s caught in de middle. We’s either gettin’ shot at by de proud ones or gettin’ fleeced by de slick ones.”

He sighed, a deep, heavy sound. “Sometimes I think dis whole worl’ is jes’ one big con game.”

I didn’t have an answer for that. I just sat there and helped myself to some of the bacon I’d bought. It was good bacon, and I was hungry. We fried it up with some cornmeal, and made some coffee, and for a little while, we didn’t talk about colonels or preachers or mobs. We just floated down the river, eating our dinner, with the big, quiet sky above us and the steady, honest water below. The river didn’t try to fool you. It didn’t make any promises it couldn’t keep. It was what it was. And right then, that seemed like the only thing in the whole world you could trust.

drifting along like that for a day or two, just me and Jim, and the river. The world on the banks seemed to fall away behind us, like a bad dream you was glad to wake from. We didn’t talk much about Sherburn or that preacher. It was like they was ghosts, and speaking their names might call them up again. We kept to the middle of the river, mostly, especially at night, letting the current do the work. It was peaceable, and I wouldn’t have traded it for the best bed in the best house in St. Petersburg.

One night, it was awful dark and still. The moon was hiding behind a mess of clouds, and you couldn’t hardly tell where the water stopped and the sky started, except for the stars. We was just floating, not a sound but the little lapping noises the water made against the logs, and sometimes a bullfrog talking to his relations over on the bank. Jim was dozing at the steering oar, and I was laying on my back, watching the stars peek out and go back in again.

By-and-by, I heard something. It warn’t a frog, and it warn’t the river. It was a kind of a low, groaning sound, like a big animal in pain. It come from down around the next bend. I sat up.

“Jim,” I says, soft-like. “You hear that?”

He was awake in a second. He was always like that, sleeping with one ear open. “Hear what, Huck?”

“That noise. Sounds like trouble.”

We both listened. It come again, louder this time. A long, drawn-out moan, and then a kind of a splintering crash. It was a sound that didn’t belong on the quiet river.

“Dat ain’t no animal,” Jim whispered, his eyes wide in the dark. “Dat’s wood. Wood breakin’.”

We didn’t say nothing more. I took the other oar to help steer, and we let the raft drift on down towards the sound, keeping quiet as mice. The current was pulling us right to it. As we come around the bend, we saw a light. It warn’t a steady light, like from a cabin, but a jumpy, flickering one, low down on the water. It looked like a jack-o-lantern someone had dropped in the river.

The groaning sound got louder, and now we could hear voices, too. Not regular talking voices, but shouting, and one of them was high and screechy, like a woman.

“Looks like a wreck, Jim,” I says.

“Looks like it,” he says. “We ought to stay clear, Huck. Ain’t no tellin’ who’s on it.”

He was right, of course. The last thing we needed was to get mixed up in somebody else’s trouble. We’d had our fill of that. I started to try and paddle us over to the other side of the channel, away from the light. But then a voice comes across the water, clear as a bell, but shaky.

“Help!” it yells. “For the love of God and all the muses, help! We perish!”

Well, I couldn’t just paddle away after that. It wouldn’t be right. Even if it was more fools, or more colonels, you can’t just let folks perish if you can help it.

“All right,” I says to Jim. “Let’s see what it is. But we’ll be careful.”

We let the raft get closer, and the flickering light grew. It was coming from a couple of lanterns on what looked like a little one-deck steamboat, but it was all gussied up with paint and carvings. It was a showboat. It had run aground hard on a sandbar that stuck out into the river, and its hull was busted open on one side. The whole thing was tilted over, looking like a sick duck about to go under for good. There was a passel of people on the deck, maybe five or six of them, running around and waving their arms.

As we floated nearer, I could see them better. And it was the strangest sight I ever see. They was all dressed in outlandish clothes. One man had on red tights and a little velvet hat with a feather in it. A woman wore a long, sparkly dress that looked like it was made out of moonlight, but it was all torn and muddy at the hem. Another feller, a big man with a booming voice, was wearing some kind of a blanket pinned over his shoulder, and a crown of fake-looking leaves on his head. He looked like one of them statues in a book.

The big man with the leaves in his hair seen us first. He struck a pose, one hand on his hip and the other pointed at us.

“Behold!” he hollered, and his voice rolled across the water like thunder. “Salvation! Sent by the gods of the river to rescue our noble enterprise from this watery grave! Approach, humble servants, and attend your betters!”

I looked at Jim. Jim looked at me. Humble servants, he says. It had the same kind of ring to it as that preacher.

“Dey’s play-actors, Huck,” Jim whispered.

“I reckon so,” I says.

We poled the raft up close to the wreck. The boat was groaning something awful, settling deeper onto the sandbar. The man in the red tights was trying to bail water with a bucket that had a hole in it. The lady in the sparkly dress was crying.

The big feller with the leaf-crown, who I reckoned was the boss, strode to the edge of his sinking deck. He was a sight. He had a big, handsome face, but it was starting to go puffy around the gills, and his eyes was a little wild.

“You, there, on the floating contrivance!” he boomed. “I am Tiberius Maximus Montague, proprietor of the Montague Thespian Company and Floating Palace of Dramatic Art! We have met with a slight nautical misadventure. You will convey myself and my troupe, along with our most essential properties, to the safety of yon shore.”

He didn’t ask, he told. Just like Colonel Sherburn. He had that same way of talking, like he owned the whole river and everybody on it.

“Our boat’s sinkin’,” I says. “We ain’t a ferry.”

He laughed, a big, phony-sounding laugh. “My dear boy, a raft is a raft, a boat is a boat. What matters is the will to command! We are artists, sir! The purveyors of culture to this benighted wilderness! You would not see Hamlet perish for want of a log, would you?”

I didn’t know who Hamlet was, but I didn’t want to see nobody perish.

“What happened?” I asked.

“A navigational oversight!” he declared, waving a hand. “Our pilot, a provincial dullard with the eyes of a bat, misjudged the current. He has, I regret to say, deserted his post and swum for the shore, the coward. Leaving us, the talent, to our fate!”

While he was talking, the others was gathering up bundles and bags and what looked like a couple of painted wooden swords. The lady in the sparkly dress, she looked over at our raft. She had a pale, tired face under all the paint.

“Please, boy,” she says, and her voice warn’t loud and booming, but small and scared. “Please help us. We’ve got a little girl with us.”

She pointed, and I seen for the first time a little girl, maybe eight or nine years old, huddled in a corner, wrapped in a blanket. She was looking at the dark water with big, frightened eyes.

Well, that settled it. You can’t say no to that.

“All right,” I says. “Climb aboard. But we can’t take much. This raft ain’t the Great Eastern.”

Tiberius Maximus Montague clapped his hands. “Excellent! You show a proper spirit of deference. Quick, my players! Gather the Bard’s sacred vestments! The crowns! The skulls! We must not leave Yorick to the fishes!”

It was the dad-blamedest mess you ever saw. They started handing things over to us. Bundles of cheap-looking costumes made of velvet and satin, all damp and smelling of mildew. A big cardboard chest painted to look like it was full of treasure. A bunch of swords and daggers. The man in the red tights, whose name was Cyril, he almost fell in the river trying to save a stuffed owl. The lady, Miss Eva, clambered onto the raft with the little girl, who was her daughter, and wrapped her up tight in the driest spot she could find, right by our tent.

Last to come was Mr. Montague himself. He didn’t just climb on. He leaped. He landed in the middle of the raft, almost tipping us all over, and struck another pose.

“Thus I escape the clutches of Neptune!” he roared. “Now, onward! To the nearest settlement, where we shall regale the local peasantry with the glories of Shakespeare and replenish our coffers!”

Jim and me, we just stood there with the oars in our hands, staring at this new cargo. Our nice, quiet, peaceable raft was all of a sudden cluttered up with a bunch of crazy play-actors and their junk. They was loud and they was strange, and Mr. Montague smelled of whisky and something like cheap perfume.

We pushed off from the wreck and let it go. It give one last groan, and slid off the sandbar into the deep channel with a big sighing sound, and the lanterns went out. Then it was gone.

“A tragedy,” Mr. Montague said, but he didn’t sound very sad. He sounded like he was enjoying the sound of his own voice. “But the show, as they say, must go on! Boy! What is your name?”

“Huck,” I says.

“Huck,” he repeated, tasting the name. “A rustic appellation, but serviceable. And your dusky companion?”

Jim stiffened up. He didn’t like being called a dusky companion.

“This is Jim,” I says, sharp-like.

“Jim,” Montague says, nodding like a king. “Very well. Huck and Jim. You are now part of the Montague Thespian Company. You will serve as our transportation and our stage crew. In return, you will be permitted to witness our art. A fair bargain, I think.”

I didn’t think it was a bargain at all. I thought it was a raw deal. But there they was, on our raft, and there wasn’t much we could do about it now.

The next few days was misery. Pure, solid misery. Our raft, which had been a kind of a kingdom for me and Jim, was turned into a madhouse. Cyril and another young feller named Francis was always practicing sword-fighting, clanking their tin blades together and almost knocking us in the river. Miss Eva mostly kept to herself with her little girl, patching their clothes and looking sad. But Mr. Montague, he was the worst. He was always performing. He never just walked, he strode. He never just talked, he declaimed.

He’d stand up on the edge of the raft in his blanket-toga, with the sun coming up behind him, and yell things at the trees on the bank.

“O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!” he’d bellow. And then he’d turn to me, while I was trying to fish for our breakfast, and say, “Boy, is there not a melancholy in my bearing? Do you not see the soul of a Danish prince wrestling with the coils of mortality?”

“I reckon you just need some breakfast,” I’d say.

He treated Jim even worse. He called him Caliban and ordered him around to fetch and carry his things, until one time Jim just stood there and looked at him with his steady, quiet eyes, and didn’t move. Montague blustered for a minute, and then went and got it himself. He was like Colonel Sherburn in his pride, but he didn’t have the sand to back it up. He was all talk and no shoot.

He was another one of Jim’s slick fellers, but a different kind. He warn’t selling salvation like the preacher; he was selling make-believe. He was so full of it, I think he even believed it himself, half the time.

One evening, we was all sitting around the little fire we’d made on the clay hearth. The actors was eating some of our corn-pone and bacon, and complaining that it warn’t fit for a king. Mr. Montague had been drinking from a flask he’d saved from the wreck, and he was feeling mighty theatrical.

He pointed a wobbly finger at Jim. “Tell me, my good man,” he slurred. “You who are a child of this great, muddy artery of a nation. What tales does the river tell you? What dark secrets does it whisper in the night?”

Jim looked at the fire for a long time. Then he looked up at Montague.

“De river,” he says, his voice low and deep, “it don’t tell no secrets. It jes’ is. It’s de folks on de river dat’s full o’ secrets an’ lies.”

Montague stared at him, his mouth a little open. For once, he didn’t have a speech ready.

Then Miss Eva’s little girl, who hadn’t said much of anything the whole time, piped up. “The river took my dolly,” she said, and her lip started to tremble.

Miss Eva hugged her tight. “I know, sweetie. I know.” She looked over at Montague, and for the first time I seen something other than sadness in her eyes. I seen fire. “Yes, Tiberius. The river took her dolly. And our boat. And all our money. Perhaps it is time for less poetry and more of a plan.”

Montague puffed out his chest. “A plan! Of course there is a plan! There is always a plan! We shall descend upon the next hive of humanity like gods from Olympus! We shall present them with ‘The Tragical History of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark’! We shall astound them, baffle them, and, most importantly, relieve them of their surplus currency.”

He grinned, a slick, greasy grin. “And I have the perfect addition to the cast.” He looked right at me and Jim. “You, my boy, shall play the gravedigger! And you, Jim, with your natural gravitas, shall be the ghost of Hamlet’s father!”

He thought this was a great idea. He laughed and slapped his knee. The other actors looked at us, and then looked away, embarrassed.

I knew right then we had to get rid of them. They was worse than the Duke and the King, in a way, because they was so useless you almost felt sorry for them. Almost.

The next day, we saw a town up ahead. It warn’t much of a town, just a few shabby-looking buildings huddled on a high bank, with a little wood-yard and a pier. It was called Pikesville.

“There!” Montague shouted, jumping up. “Our stage! Our audience! Our salvation! Huck, my boy, steer us towards yon metropolis!”

So I did. I steered the raft right up to that rickety pier. Montague was all excitement.

“Quick, now!” he commanded. “Unload the properties! Find me the town hall, the church, the largest barn! We must secure a venue! Cyril, find a printer! We need handbills! ‘For One Night Only! The World-Renowned Montague Thespian Company!’”

They all scrambled to obey him, grabbing their bundles of costumes and their tin swords. They was like a bunch of children let out of school. They loaded themselves down with all their junk and went clambering up the muddy bank towards the town, Mr. Montague in the lead, his blanket-toga flapping behind him. He didn’t even look back.

Miss Eva was the last one. She stopped before she got off the raft and looked at me and Jim. The little girl was holding her hand tight.

“Thank you,” she said, and her voice was quiet. “For the ride.”

“It warn’t nothing,” I says.

“He’s not a bad man,” she said, nodding towards Montague, who was now trying to commandeer a mule from a farmer. “He just lives in a world he makes up in his head. It’s the only one he has.”

Then she turned and hurried her daughter up the bank, after the rest of them.

Me and Jim stood on the raft and watched them go. They disappeared over the top of the bank, a sad, ridiculous parade of make-believe kings and queens heading into a real, hard town that probably wouldn’t give them the time of day, let alone a dollar for Hamlet.

I looked at Jim. He looked at me. A little grin started at the corner of his mouth.

“Dey forgot deir ghost an’ deir gravedigger, Huck,” he said.

“I reckon they did, Jim,” I says.

I didn’t waste no time. I grabbed a pole, and Jim grabbed the steering oar. We shoved off from that pier as quiet as we could. The current caught us, and the raft swung out into the middle of the river again. Nobody on the shore noticed. They was all probably staring at the feller in red tights trying to explain what a thespian was.

We floated on down the river. The raft felt huge and empty and clean again. The sun was warm, and the water was making its old friendly noises. I laid down on the boards and looked up at the big blue sky. It was a powerful sight, better than any show.

After a while, Jim spoke. “Huck,” he says.

“Yeah, Jim?”

“Dat Mr. Montague feller, he warn’t a proud, cold man like de Colonel. An’ he warn’t a slick, smilin’ man like de preacher, not really.”

“No,” I says. “He was somethin’ else.”

“He was,” Jim says, thinking hard. “He was jes’… empty. Like a fancy bottle wid nuthin’ inside but air. An’ he had to keep makin’ noise so nobody’d notice.”

I thought about that. An empty bottle. That was it, exactly. All that booming talk and dressing up was just to hide the fact that there warn’t nothing there.

“It’s a lonesome way to be,” Jim said, more to himself than to me.

We was quiet then, for a good long while. The town of Pikesville got smaller and smaller behind us, until it was just another little smudge on the edge of the world. The river was wide and steady, and it was carrying us on, away from all the colonels and preachers and play-actors, away from all their noise. For the first time in days, I felt like I could breathe again. The river didn’t put on a show. It didn’t make any speeches. It just was. And we was on it, me and Jim, free and easy, and heading south.

away they went, a-whooping and a-yelling, down the street towards Sherburn’s house. I judged it was a good time to not be there. When a crowd gets to hollering about lynching, they ain’t particular about who they string up, so long as they get to feel righteous about it afterwards. I slid along the wall of the drug store and squeezed betwixt a couple of fellers that was arguing over whether Boggs was shot with a pistol or a derringer, and then I was free. I didn’t run, because running just makes you look like you got a reason to, but I walked brisk, and I didn’t stop walking till the noise of the mob was just a dull buzz behind me, like hornets that has lost their nest.

I made it back to the little patch of woods where I’d hid the canoe. Everything was quiet there, with the leaves whispering and the birds carrying on with their business like there warn’t no such thing as murder and mobs in the world. It felt good to be there. I set in the canoe and shoved off into the river, and paddled out to the little island where we’d stashed the raft. It was getting on towards evening, and the sun was making a grand, gaudy show of itself, all red and gold over the Arkansas shore.

When I got to the raft, Jim was there, mending one of the sweeps with some cord. He’d been worried, I could tell. He didn’t say nothing at first, just looked at me with them big, patient eyes of his.

“Dey git him, Huck?” he says, finally.

“They got Boggs,” I says. “The Colonel shot him. Right in the street.”

Jim shook his head slow. “Dat’s pow’ful bad. A man oughtn’t to go out like dat, hollerin’ and bein’ made a fool of.”

“No,” I says. “And then they went to lynch the Colonel.”

Jim’s eyes got wider. “Lynch de Colonel? De man dat done de shootin’?”

“That’s the one,” I says. I told him all about it; about Sherburn standing on his porch roof and telling them all what cowards they was, and how they was borrowing their courage from each other and from the dark. I told him how the crowd just sort of melted away, looking ashamed and foolish, and how Sherburn turned his back on them and went inside.

Jim, he listened to the whole thing, and when I was done he just set there for a long time, looking out at the river.

“People is mighty strange, Huck,” he says. “Dey’s loud an’ dey’s quiet. Dey’s brave an’ dey’s scared. An’ sometimes dey’s all of it at de same time. It’s a puzzle.”

I reckoned it was. We didn’t say no more about it. We had our supper, which was some corn-pone and bacon and a fish I’d catched earlier, and then we untied the raft. The current took us, and the town with all its troubles and its dead men got smaller and smaller behind us, until the lights blended together and looked like a little handful of fallen stars.

We had a couple of days that was mighty fine. The weather held good and the nights was clear and starry. There ain’t nothing like floating down the big river on a raft, specially at night. You can lay on your back and look up at the stars, and they’re so thick and bright it makes you feel powerful small and lonesome, but not in a bad way. It’s a good lonesome. You feel like you’re the only people in the whole world, you and your friend, and nothing can touch you. We’d let the raft drift, and we wouldn’t talk, just listen to the river talking to itself with all its little chuckles and whispers. Sometimes a steamboat would come pounding up the river, a great big monster all lit up and trailing smoke and sparks, and we’d have to paddle like the nation to get out of its way. But mostly it was just us and the river and the sky.

We passed little towns that was just a light or two on the bank, and wood-yards where they’d have a lantern hanging out, and sometimes we’d hear a dog bark, or a fiddle playing way off somewhere. It was all peaceful. I was starting to think maybe we could go on like this all the way to Cairo and be done with all the troubles of the shore.

But the river, it don’t stay peaceful forever.

It was the third night after the Sherburn business. We was floating along, same as ever. The moon was up, but it was hiding behind a mess of clouds that was getting thicker and darker all the time. The air got heavy and still, the way it does before a big storm. There warn’t a breath of wind, and the water was slick and black as oil.

“Huck,” Jim says, and his voice was low. “I doan like de look of dis. De river’s holdin’ its breath.”

He was right. You could feel it. The world was waiting for something. Pretty soon, we heard it. It was a low moaning sound, way off down the river. It warn’t a steamboat. It was the wind, coming for us. Then we seen a line of white on the black water, coming at us fast. It was the waves, being kicked up by the wind.

“Get the sweeps, Jim!” I yelled. “We got to make for the bank!”

But it was too late. The storm hit us like a fist. One second it was dead calm, and the next the wind was trying to tear the hair off our heads and the rain was coming down so hard it felt like somebody was throwing buckets of water in our face. The raft, which always felt so big and solid, was getting tossed around like a chip of wood. Waves was breaking over the side, and the whole thing was bucking and heaving. Lightning would split the sky open, and for a second you could see everything, the churning, muddy water, the trees on the bank whipping back and forth, and the rain driving down in solid white sheets. Then it would be blacker than the inside of a cow, and the thunder would come, a great big tearing crash that shook the air and the water and your own bones.

We couldn’t see nothing and we couldn’t hear nothing but the storm. I was holding on to the corner of the wigwam for dear life, and Jim was somewhere on the other side doing the same. We was being pushed towards the shore, but it was a wild, rough ride. I was scared the raft would get busted to pieces on a snag. Every time the lightning flashed, I’d look for Jim, and I’d see him, his face all wet and his eyes big, holding on tight.

After what felt like a year, I felt a great big jolt that near about throwed me in the river. We’d hit something. The raft shuddered and groaned, and then it sort of settled down, caught on whatever it was we’d run into. The back end of it was still swinging around in the current, but the front was stuck fast.

“Jim!” I hollered. “You all right?”

“I’s here, Huck!” he hollered back. “We’s grounded!”

The storm was still raging, but not so bad as it was. The wind was slacking off a little. In the next flash of lightning, I could see what happened. We’d been driven into a little cove, and the front of the raft was wedged hard in the mud and roots of the bank. The trees hung right over us, their branches dripping and groaning.

We was soaked to the bone and shivering. Staying on the raft was miserable, with the waves still washing over it.

“We got to get on shore,” I says. “We’ll catch our death out here.”

“Dey might be people,” Jim says.

“Might be,” I says. “But I’d druther risk people than get drownded or struck by lightning.”

So we clambered off the raft and up the muddy bank. It was slick and steep, and we had to pull ourselves up by grabbing onto roots and bushes. When we got to the top, we was in a thick stand of trees. The rain was still coming down, but the trees broke it up some. We stood there, shivering and dripping and trying to get our bearings.

It was then I seen it. Through the trees and the rain, not more than a hundred yards away, was a light. A single, yellow light in a window.

“Looky there, Jim,” I says, pointing. “A house.”

We looked at each other. A house meant people, and people meant trouble, most times. But it also meant a roof and a fire. And right then, a fire sounded like the best thing in the whole world.

“We got to be keerful, Huck,” Jim whispered.

“I know,” I says. “We’ll just take a look. If it looks bad, we’ll come right back.”

We crept through the woods towards the light. It was slow going in the dark and the mud. The house was bigger than I thought. It was a two-story frame house, but it looked old and lonesome. The paint was all peeled off, and one of the shutters was hanging by a single hinge, banging against the wall every time a gust of wind come along. It looked like the kind of house that had stories to it, and none of them was cheerful.

There was only the one light, in a downstairs window. We snuck up to the side of the house and I peeked in. It was a main room, what you might call a parlor. There was a fireplace with a small fire going in it, and it made the whole room look warm and shaky. Sitting in a rocking chair in front of the fire was an old woman. She was just sitting there, staring into the flames. She was thin and bony, with her gray hair pulled back so tight it looked like it hurt. She had a shawl around her shoulders, and she was rocking, slow and steady. There warn’t nobody else in the room.

“It’s just an old woman by herself,” I whispered to Jim.

Jim, he peeked too. “She look lonesome,” he said.

“She does,” I says. “Maybe she’ll be charitable to a couple of drownded rats.”

I was still nervous, but being cold and wet is a powerful persuader. I decided to risk it. We went around to the front door and I knocked. It was a weak little knock, because I was half froze.

We waited. The shutter banged. The wind moaned in the trees. I was about to knock again when we heard shuffling inside. A bolt slid back with a rusty screech, and the door opened a crack. An eye looked out at us.

“Who’s there?” a voice says. It was a creaky voice, like the door hinge.

“Ma’am,” I says, trying to make my teeth stop chattering. “Our raft got wrecked in the storm. Me and… and my man here, we’re near about froze to death. We was just wondering if we could maybe warm up by your fire for a spell. We won’t be no trouble.”

The eye looked me up and down, then it looked past me at Jim, who was standing back in the shadows a little. I couldn’t tell what she was thinking.

“Just the two of you?” she says.

“Yes, ma’am,” I says. “Just us.”

She was quiet for another long time. I figured she was going to shut the door in our faces. But then the bolt screeched again, and the door opened up all the way.

“Come in,” she says. “Can’t leave a dog out in a night like this, let alone a boy. Come in and dry yourselves.”

We was so grateful we could have cried. We shuffled inside and she shut the door behind us, cutting off the noise of the storm. The room was just like I’d seen it. There was some old furniture, a table and a few chairs, and the fireplace. It smelled like woodsmoke and dust and old things.

“Go on, get up to the fire,” the old woman said. “You look like a pair of half-drowned cats.”

We did. We stood in front of that fire, and the warmth soaked into us, and it was the best feeling I ever had. We was dripping all over her floor, but she didn’t seem to mind.

She looked us over. “You’re a young one to be out on the river. Where’s your folks?”

It was time for a story. I had plenty of them ready.

“My folks is dead, ma’am,” I says, looking pitiful. “I was living with my uncle on a farm up by Napoleon, but he passed on, too. This here is Jim. He belonged to my uncle. I’m taking him down to my other uncle, my Pap’s brother, who’s got a plantation down near Greenville. He’s the rightful owner now.”

It was a thin story, but it was the best I could come up with. It explained me and Jim being together, and why we was heading south.

The old woman just nodded, her eyes watching me sharp. “Greenville. That’s a good ways yet.” She looked at Jim. He was standing quiet, his clothes steaming in the heat of the fire. “He a good hand, is he?”

“Oh, yes, ma’am,” I says. “The best. My uncle wouldn’t have no other kind.”

“I see,” she says. She went over to a cupboard and took out a couple of tin cups and a jug. “You boys could probably use something to warm your insides, too.” She poured some coffee into the cups and handed them to us. It was hot and bitter, but it felt good going down.

“My name is Gable,” she says, settling back into her rocker. “Mrs. Gable. I been living in this house for forty year. Lived here with my husband till the river took him.”

“The river took him, ma’am?” I says.

“He was a pilot,” she says, her eyes on the fire again. “Best pilot on the lower Mississippi, they used to say. Knew every snag and sandbar from St. Louis to New Orleans. But the river changes. It’s always changing. One night, in a fog, he hit a snag he swore warn’t there the week before. Went down with his boat. They never even found him.” She rocked for a minute. “The river gives, and the river takes away. Mostly it takes.”

She looked so sad and old sitting there, I felt sorry for her.

“We’re mighty obliged to you for letting us in, Mrs. Gable,” I says.

“It ain’t nothing,” she says. But then she got a strange look in her eye. A calculating look. She looked from me to Jim and back again. “Times is hard,” she says, more to herself than to us. “Awful hard since Mr. Gable passed. The house is falling down around my ears, and there ain’t a dollar to be had for love nor money.”

She stopped rocking and leaned forward a little. Her voice dropped. “Tell me, boy. You seen any trouble on the river? Any sign of the patrols?”

I felt a cold knot in my stomach. “Patrols, ma’am?”

“For the runaways,” she says, her eyes flicking over to Jim again, just for a second. “I hear tell there’s a sight of them making a break for the north these days. A wicked thing, stealing themselves like that. There’s good money in it for a person that helps the law. A good reward.”

The coffee turned sour in my mouth. Jim didn’t move a muscle, but I could feel how stiff he’d gone standing next to me.

“No, ma’am,” I says, trying to keep my voice steady. “We ain’t seen a thing. We just been minding our own business.”

“Two hundred dollars,” she says, her voice a dry whisper. “That’s what they’re paying for a healthy runaway slave in some parts. Two hundred dollars. A body could live a long time on that. Could fix a roof. Could live without being afeard of starving come winter.”

The room was warm, but I was cold again, colder than I was out in the storm. She wasn’t just a lonesome old woman. She was a lonesome, desperate old woman. And that was a whole lot more dangerous. She knew. I don’t know how, but I knew she knew. Maybe my story was too thin. Maybe she saw the look in Jim’s eyes. But she knew he was a runaway, and she was sitting there, rocking in her chair, figuring out the value of him.

“Well,” she says, her voice turning friendly again, too friendly. “The storm’s about passed. But you boys are tired. You can’t go back out on that river tonight. I got a dry place in the barn you can sleep. You’ll be safe as houses till morning, and you can fix your raft when the sun’s up.”

She was going to keep us there till morning. Then she’d send for the sheriff. Or go herself. The barn was a trap.

“That’s mighty kind of you, ma’am,” I says, playing along. “We’re tuckered out, for a fact. We’ll just finish this here coffee and then we’ll get out of your way.”

“No trouble at all,” she says, smiling a thin little smile. “No trouble at all.”

We stood there and drank the coffee. It was the longest five minutes of my life. Every time I looked at her, she was looking at Jim with that hungry, calculating look. She was seeing two hundred dollars, not a man. I thought about Boggs getting shot down in the street, and Colonel Sherburn facing down the mob, and all the meanness and foolishness I’d seen. And I thought, here it is again. It ain’t always loud and yelling. Sometimes it’s quiet and smiling, sitting in a rocking chair in a warm room, just waiting.

When we finished, I handed her my cup. “Thank you again, ma’am. If you’ll just show us to the barn?”

“Of course,” she says, getting up. She lit a lantern and led us to the back door. “It’s just out back. The loft is full of hay. You’ll sleep warm.”

She opened the door and handed me the lantern. The rain had stopped, and the moon was trying to break through the clouds.

“Good night, then,” she says, and she stood in the doorway, watching us. She was going to make sure we went into the barn.

“Good night, ma’am,” I says.

We walked out into the yard. The barn was a dark shape a little ways off. I walked towards it, holding the lantern up. Jim was right behind me. I didn’t dare look back at the house. I could feel her eyes on us.

We got to the big barn door and I made a show of trying to pull it open. It was heavy. I set the lantern down and me and Jim both put our shoulders to it. It scraped open just enough for us to slip through. I slid it shut behind us.

It was black as pitch inside, and smelled of hay and manure and damp earth.

“Huck?” Jim whispered. His voice was trembling. “Dat woman…”

“I know, Jim,” I whispered back. “She was measuring you for a pair of handcuffs. We got to get out of here. Now.”

“How, Huck? She’s a-watchin’.”

“We ain’t staying,” I says. I blew out the lantern. “We’re going out the back.”

We felt our way through the dark, bumping into stalls and piles of junk. My heart was hammering in my chest. I was sure she was going to come out and check on us, or that we’d make a noise. We found a small door on the other side of the barn, held shut by a wooden latch. I lifted it as quiet as I could. It didn’t make a sound.

I peeked out. The back of the barn faced away from the house, towards a thick patch of woods. We was clear.

“Come on, Jim,” I breathed. “And don’t make a sound.”

We slipped out and pulled the door shut. We didn’t run. We crept along the edge of the woods, staying in the shadows, making our way back around towards the river and the raft. When we got to where we could see the house again, I looked. The back door was shut. But there was a light in an upstairs window now. She was up there, watching the barn, waiting for morning.

A shiver went down my spine that had nothing to do with the cold.

We got back to the raft without her seeing us. It was still wedged in the mud, but the river had gone down some. It took us near half an hour of pushing and pulling and prying with a pole, sweating and grunting in the dark, to get it free. When it finally slid loose into the current, I almost hollered I was so relieved.

We didn’t use the sweeps. We just laid low on the deck and let the current take us away, silent as a ghost. We drifted down the river for a good mile, not saying a word, until the light in that lonesome house was long gone. The clouds broke up for good, and the moon came out, big and white, and laid a silver track on the water.

Jim let out a long breath. “Huck, dat was too close. My heart is still knockin’ like a cooper on a barrel.”

“Mine, too, Jim,” I says. I was still shaking.

“Dat woman,” he says, shaking his head. “She talk about her husband de river took. But she was fixin’ to let de river take me, too. Fo’ two hundred dollars.”

I laid down on the planks and looked up at the sky. All the stars was out again. It was all so peaceful and big and clean out here. It was hard to believe there was people on the shores that was so twisted up and desperate that they’d sell a man for the price of a new roof. It made you feel tired of the whole human race.

“I reckon we can’t trust nobody, Jim,” I says. “Not a soul.”

“Nobody but us, Huck,” he says. And his voice was so low and sure, it made me feel a little better.

“Yeah,” I says. “Nobody but us.”

We floated on, me and Jim, down the big, quiet river. We was free again, but it felt different this time. It felt like a freedom we had to steal over and over again, every day, from a world that was set on taking it away.

I was right in the thick of it, not because I wanted to be, but because when a hundred men decide to run one way, a boy ain’t got much choice but to be carried along or get trampled. It was a sight to see, and a sound to hear. Men that was hiding under awnings and in doorways when Sherburn had his pistol out was now the loudest ones, hollering about rope and a tall oak tree. The lanky man with the stovepipe hat was kind of leading it, not by saying “follow me,” but by being the first one to take the biggest strides and yell the bloodiest things.

“He’s got to pay!” one feller with a face like a boiled ham kept shouting. “An eye for an eye, says the good book!”

“Boggs was a fool but he was our fool!” another one yelled, and a good many of them nodded at that, like it made a whole lot of sense.

I reckoned it was a powerful lot of courage they’d found in the bottom of their bottles since Sherburn had walked off. They wasn’t a mob when he was there, facing them down; they was just a crowd. But now that his back was turned, they’d found their spirit again, or what passed for it.

We all went piling down the main street and took a turn towards the nicer part of town, where the houses was bigger and had fences. Sherburn’s house was the biggest of them all. It was two stories high and painted so white it hurt your eyes in the sun, with big tall windows and a porch that run all the way around. It had a iron fence all around it with sharp points on the top, like it was daring anybody to try and get in.

The mob come to a halt at the gate, and all that hollering and shoving sort of petered out. They all bunched up there like sheep that don’t want to go in a pen. The lanky man, he stood at the front, but he didn’t make a move to open the gate. He just stood there, looking at the house. Everybody was looking at the house. It was dead quiet. You couldn’t hear nothing but the wind rustling the leaves in the big chinaberry trees in the yard.

“Well, go on,” somebody says from the back, in a voice that was about half a whisper.

“You go on,” another one says.

The lanky man, he cleared his throat. He looked back at the crowd, and his face didn’t look so brave now. He looked like a boy that’s been dared to jump off a roof.

“Sherburn!” he yelled, but his voice cracked a little. “Come on out, Sherburn! We want a word with you!”

Nothing happened. The house just sat there, big and white and silent. It seemed like it was looking back at us, and it warn’t impressed.

“He’s in there!” a man says. “I seen him walk this way!”

“Bust the gate down!” somebody else says, and a few fellers made a little push, but nobody wanted to be the first one to touch that iron.

Then the front door of the house opened.

A hush fell over the whole crowd so deep you could have heard a pin drop in the dust. Every man there held his breath. It warn’t Sherburn. It was a tall, thin black woman in a white apron. She didn’t look scared at all. She just stood there in the doorway, holding a silver tray, looking at us all with these calm, tired eyes. She didn’t say a word. She just looked.

The lanky man, he shuffled his feet. “We’ve come for Colonel Sherburn,” he says, trying to sound official.

The woman just kept looking at him. Then she looked over the whole crowd, one by one, it seemed like. Her eyes passed right over me. Then she turned her head a little and spit a stream of snuff juice into a flowerpot on the porch.

“He ain’t here,” she says. Her voice was low and smooth as river mud.

“He is too here!” the ham-faced feller shouts. “We seen him! He murdered Boggs in cold blood!”

The woman sighed, a long, slow sound, like she had heard this kind of foolishness a thousand times before. “I said, he ain’t here. He took his horse and rode out of town right after he left the street. Said he had business over to the next county. Said he’d be back in a day or two.”

She looked at us all again. “You gentlemen finished making all this noise in front of a respectable house?”

Nobody said nothing. You could feel the wind go out of the mob. All that rage and whisky courage just evaporated into the afternoon air. They’d worked themselves up to storming the castle, only to find the king had gone out for a ride. They stood there looking at each other, and at the ground, and at the tops of their own boots. They looked foolish, and they knowed it.

The lanky man took off his stovepipe hat and wiped his forehead with his sleeve. “Well,” he says. “Well, then. I reckon… I reckon we’ll wait for him to get back.”

He said it like he was making a promise, but it sounded more like a boy whistling past a graveyard. The woman just gave a little nod, like she didn’t believe it for a second, and then she turned and went back inside, shutting the big door with a solid, final-sounding click.

The crowd started to break up, not all at once, but in little clumps. They didn’t talk loud no more. They mumbled to each other. Some of them was saying Sherburn was a coward for running off. Others was saying he was smart, and that they’d have done the same. I thought he was smart, too, but I didn’t think he was a coward. A coward wouldn’t have turned his back on a whole town and just rode away slow.

I seen my chance and slipped away from the edge of the crowd, keeping close to the fences. I’d had enough of town for one day, and for a whole year, maybe. The whole thing made me feel sick and hollow. First Boggs getting shot for being a loud-mouthed fool, and then the whole town turning into a pack of dogs, and then turning back into men with their tails between their legs because a woman on a porch told them to go home. It was all so mixed up and ugly. I wanted the river.

I was making my way back towards the wood-yards where we’d tied up the raft when I heard a new kind of commotion. It warn’t angry like the mob, but it was just as loud. It was a drum, somebody banging on a drum, and a feller with a voice like a foghorn. A crowd was already gathering in a little square near the edge of town, and being a boy, my feet just sort of took me over there to see what it was, even though my head was telling me to get along.

It was a medicine show. A big wagon, painted all over with red and yellow letters, said “PROFESSOR MERRICK’S MIRACLE ELIXIR, THE PANACEA FOR ALL PAIN.” On a little wooden stage set up next to the wagon stood the professor himself. He was a sight. He had long black hair, slicked down with some kind of grease, and a shiny silk vest that was so tight you could see his dinner pushing against it.

“Friends! Neighbors! Fellow sufferers in this vale of tears!” he boomed, spreading his arms wide. “Do you find yourselves afflicted? Do you wake in the morning with a creak in your bones and a fog in your mind? Does the weight of the world bear down upon your weary shoulders?”

The crowd, the same fellers that was hollering to lynch Sherburn not an hour ago, was now gawking up at this man like he was Moses come down from the mountain. They all nodded and murmured.

“Of course you do!” the professor shouted. “You are beset by humors, plagued by miasmas, tormented by the thousand invisible demons of ill health! The local sawbones will bleed you, the parsons will tell you to pray, but I, Professor Merrick, I bring you science! I bring you salvation! I bring you… the Miracle Elixir!”

He held up a little brown bottle. It glinted in the setting sun.

“Distilled from the rarest roots of the Amazon jungle, blended with the secret minerals of the Carpathian mountains, and sanctified by the wisdom of the ancient pharaohs!” he says. “This is not mere medicine, my friends. This is liquid life! It cures consumption, catarrh, colic, and cowardice! It banishes boils, banishes back-ache, banishes baldness! It will mend a broken bone and it will mend a broken heart!”

I knowed it was all a humbug, of course. It was the sort of thing the duke and the king would have done, only this feller was better at it. He had a style they couldn’t touch.

“Now, who among you will be the first to taste the sweet nectar of vitality?” he says, looking over the crowd. “Who is brave enough to cast off the shackles of suffering? A volunteer! I need a volunteer with a visible affliction!”

His eyes scanned the crowd, and then, just like it was by accident, they lit on a feller leaning on a crutch near the front. The feller was all stooped over and looked pitiful.

“You, sir!” the professor says. “You with the look of a man who has carried a heavy burden for many a mile. What ails you?”

The man, in a weak, shaky voice, says, “It’s my leg, professor. Broke it in the war. Hasn’t been right since. A constant torment, it is.”

“A torment no more!” the professor declared. He hopped down from the stage and rushed over to the man. He uncorked the bottle and waved it under the man’s nose. “Inhale the vapors of vigor!” Then he poured a little bit into a spoon and fed it to him. “And now, ingest the elixir of excellence!”

The man swallowed, and his eyes went wide. He shook his head a little. He took a deep breath. Then he stood up a little straighter.

“I… I feel a warmth,” he says, his voice stronger now. “A tingling, professor!”

“It is the Miracle at work!” the professor yelled to the crowd. “The forces of rejuvenation are battling the demons of decay! Now, my good man, take a step. A small step for man, a giant leap away from your infirmity!”

The man took a shaky step without his crutch. The crowd gasped. He took another, more solid one. He started to smile. He took another, and another, and then he was walking a little circle, his limp getting less and less with every step.

“I’m cured!” he hollered, his voice booming now. “It’s a miracle! I’m cured!” He threw his crutch up in the air, and the crowd went wild. They cheered and whistled and clapped.

Right then, I knowed for certain the man was a shill, part of the show. I’d seen him before the show started, over behind the wagon, sharing a pipe with the professor’s drummer. And his crutch didn’t even have no scuff marks on the bottom of it. But nobody else seemed to notice or care. They was all caught up in the magic.

“And now, my friends!” the professor bellowed, climbing back on his stage. “For the paltry sum of one silver dollar, you too can possess this bottled blessing! A dollar to end your suffering! A dollar for a lifetime of health and happiness! Who will be first?”

Hands shot up all over the crowd, waving dollar bills and half-dollars. The professor and his drummer and the feller who was just cured started handing out bottles and raking in the money. I just stood there and watched. It was the second big show I’d seen that day. The first one was about death, and this one was about life, but they was both full of lies and play-acting. The only difference was that this one made people feel good for a little while, and nobody got shot.

I felt a little tired of the whole human race right then. They’d pay a dollar for a bottle of brown water and lies, but they wouldn’t risk their skins to stand up for a man who was already dead. It didn’t make no sense.

I slipped out the back of the crowd and didn’t stop walking till I could smell the mud and the willows of the riverbank. The sun was down by then, and the sky was all purple and orange over the dark line of trees on the other side.

The raft was right where I left it. I untied the rope and was about to shove off when I seen Jim’s head pop up from behind a pile of stuff.

“Huck! Is dat you? I was gettin’ pow’ful worried.”

“It’s me, Jim,” I says, climbing aboard. I was so glad to see him it was like a weight lifted off me. “Shove us off into the current.”

He didn’t ask no questions. He just took the pole and pushed us out into the deep water, and soon the town and all its foolishness was just a few twinkling lights behind us. We drifted for a while in the quiet, just listening to the water lapping against the side of the raft and the frogs starting up their nightly chorus on the bank.

“Town was mighty loud today, Huck,” Jim says after a time. “Sounded like de whole world was comin’ to an end over dere.”

So I told him. I told him everything. About old Boggs getting drunk and cussing Sherburn, and how Sherburn warned him and then shot him down right in the street, with his daughter crying over him. I told him about the lanky man and the mob, and how they all went to lynch Sherburn and then backed down when they got to his house. And then I told him about Professor Merrick and his Miracle Elixir and the feller that was cured of his fake limp.

Jim listened to it all, his eyes wide in the starlight. He shook his head slow when I told him about the shooting.

“Dat’s a hard world, Huck,” he says, his voice low and sad. “A hard world dat lets a man get shot down like a dog for runnin’ his mouth. An’ den de same folks dat was laughin’ at him wants to go hang de man dat done it. It’s all mixed up.”

“That’s what I thought, Jim,” I says. “It’s all mixed up and backwards.”

“An’ dis professor man,” he says. “Sellin’ bottles of hope dat’s jes’ river water an’ brown sugar, mos’ likely.”

“Most likely,” I says. “But they was buying it. They was happy to buy it.”

Jim was quiet for a long time, looking out at the big, dark river moving us along.

“Well,” he says finally. “I reckon people on de shore is jes’ so full up wid trouble and fear dat dey’ll buy anythin’ dat promises to take it away for a little while. A bottle of fake medicine, or a good hangin’. Sometime, I reckon it all feels de same to dem. Jes’ somethin’ to make ‘em forgit what dey is for a spell.”

I laid on my back on the planks and looked up at the stars. There was millions of them, sharp and cold and clean. They didn’t care nothing about Sherburn or Boggs or mobs or medicine shows. They was just there, doing what they was supposed to do. The river was like that, too. It just flowed, steady and sure, taking us away from all the trouble.

“Jim,” I says. “I’m glad we’re on this raft.”

“Me too, Huck,” he says. “Me too.”

And we didn’t say no more. We just floated on down the river, me and him, in the quiet and the dark, and it felt like we was the only two people in the whole world that was sane.
