The sun in that part of the territory didn’t just shine, it cooked you right in your boots. Most days in Prairie Dog Point was just waiting for the sun to go down again. The stage only come through twice a week, and when it did it was mostly just mail and some drummer trying to sell tinware or corsets to folks who had no use for either. We was a town that time was mostly forgetting.

It was one of them days when the heat made everything blurry and folks was too tired to even spit. I was sitting on a barrel outside the mercantile, trying to whittle a piece of pine into a dog, but it was looking more like a pig. That’s when I heard the hollering from down at the saloon. It wasn’t happy hollering. It was the kind that gets your ears to prick up.

Pretty soon Jedediah Mills comes stumbling out, his face redder than a spanked baby. He was a big feller, strong as an ox and about half as smart, and he’d been working his father’s claim since he was big enough to hold a shovel. Behind him comes a man I ain’t seen before. He was tall and thin as a fence post, and wore a black coat even in that heat. He didn’t look like he was sweating a drop. He had a face like a hawk and little silver spectacles perched on his nose.

Jedediah turns around in the street and points a shaky finger. “You’re a cheat, is what you are! A two-bit cardsharp, and I want my money back!”

The man in the black coat, he just stood there on the saloon porch and cleaned his spectacles with a white handkerchief. He took his time. The whole street, what there was of it, had gone quiet. A couple of old-timers stopped their checker game.

The man puts his spectacles back on and says, his voice all calm and smooth like creek water over stones: “The game was fair, my friend. Your luck was not.”

“Luck!” Jedediah yelled, and he kicked at a rock in the dust. “You call that luck? You been here three days and ain’t lost a hand! You swindled me, and you swindled ol’ man Hemlock, and now you’re gonna pay it back!”

The man in black sighed a little, like he was getting bored. “I have no intention of paying anything back. But I am a reasonable man. Apologize for your accusation before the sun touches the top of that church steeple, and I will forget this discourtesy.” He didn’t even raise his voice. Then he turned and went back inside the saloon.

Well, that just made Jedediah madder. He stood in the street cussing this feller out, calling him every kind of swindler and snake-oil peddler he could think of. A crowd started to gather up. Some of Jedediah’s friends was clapping him on the back and telling him he was right to stand up for himself. But I seen some of the older fellers shaking their heads. One of them, Mr. Abernathy, he says to another, “That boy is writing a check his body cain’t cash. I seen that stranger’s hands. He ain’t no farmer.”

But Jedediah wouldn’t listen to nobody. He was too full of whisky and pride. He stomped up and down the street, telling everyone how he was going to tan that stranger’s hide and use it for a saddle blanket. He said he’d wait right there till sundown and if the feller didn’t come out with his money, he’d drag him out.

I watched the sun creep down the sky. It got lower and lower, and the shadow of the church steeple got longer and longer till it stretched clear across the street and touched the saloon porch. The street was full of people now, all waiting. It was quiet, too. Nobody was saying nothing.

Just as the tip of the shadow hit the wood, the saloon doors swung open. The man in black stepped out. He wasn’t carrying a pistol that I could see. He just stood there, looking at Jedediah.

“It is time,” he says, his voice still quiet, but it carried in the still air.

Jedediah puffed out his chest. “I ain’t apologizing! I want my money!” He started walking toward the porch, his fists all balled up.

The stranger didn’t move an inch. He just watched him come. When Jedediah was about ten feet away, the stranger’s right hand moved. It wasn’t fast like a flash, it was just sort of easy and smooth, and there was a knife in it. It wasn’t a big pig-sticker, just a little thing with a shiny blade.

Jedediah, he stopped. He looked at the knife and then at the man, and for the first time he looked scared. He opened his mouth, but nothing come out.

The stranger took one step off the porch. “You were warned,” he says. Then he took another step, and another. He moved so light on his feet. Jedediah took a step back, and then another, and he tripped over his own boots and fell down in the dust. He scrambled up, all wild-eyed, and the crowd sort of gasped and shuffled back.

The stranger lunged, but it warn’t a big wild lunge. It was short and quick, like a sewing needle going into cloth. Thump. That’s the only sound it made. Jedediah made a noise, a sort of choked gurgle, and he clutched at his chest with both hands. He stood there for a second, looking surprised, and then his knees gave out and he fell forward on his face. He didn’t move no more.

The stranger looked down at him, then wiped the little knife on his pants leg, folded it, and put it in his pocket. He turned and looked at the crowd, and nobody said a word. We just stared. He walked right through the middle of us, not fast and not slow, got on his horse that was tied to the rail, and rode out of town heading west. He never even looked back.

Soon as he was gone, everybody rushed in. They rolled Jedediah over. There was a dark wet spot on his shirt, right over his heart, not very big. His eyes was open and staring at the sky. His father come running, yelling his name, and fell down on his knees beside him, and just started sobbing, not making much sound, just his whole body shaking. People crowded around to get a look, stretching their necks, and the ones in front was telling the ones in back what they saw. One feller, a mule skinner with one eye, he started showing everyone how the stranger did it, using his thumb for a knife and poking the air, saying, “It was just like this, see? Quick as a blink. The boy never had a chance.” And a bunch of fellers got out their bottles and passed them around, their voices all low and excited. I figured there was nothing more to see, so I slid out of the crowd and went on home.

The lanky man with the stovepipe hat was kind of at the front, not leading exactly, but just happening to be there and talking the loudest. He wasn’t carrying his cane no more, he’d throwed it down somewhere back by the drug store, but he was still waving his arms and telling everybody what they ought to do to Sherburn.

“We’ll string him up to his own porch roof!” he’d yell, and a part of the crowd would roar back at him. “No, we’ll get a rail and ride him out of town first!” another feller would shout, and they’d roar at that, too. They was all full of whisky and righteousness, which is a combination that is bound to make a powerful lot of noise and not much sense. They passed bottles back and forth as they went, and some of them was singing, only it wasn't a church kind of singing, it was a wild, ugly sound that made your skin crawl.

We turned the corner and there was Sherburn’s house. It was the finest house in the town, painted white with green shutters and a big porch that run all the way around. It had a neat white fence around the yard, with a gate in the middle. The yard itself was full of flowers and bushes, all looked after and tidy, not like the rest of the yards in town which was mostly packed dirt and jimson weeds. The house just sat there, quiet and proud, with the afternoon sun shining on it, and it seemed to make the crowd even madder. It was like the house was looking down on them, same as Sherburn done.

The mob swarmed up to the fence and stopped. The yelling sort of died down a little, and they just stood there, a big packed mass of men, staring at the front door. The lanky man, he pushed his way right up to the gate. He put his hands on it but he didn’t open it. He just stood there and hollered.

“Sherburn! Come on out, you murdering dog! We’re here to give you what you deserve! Come out and face the people!”

The whole crowd took it up then, and the noise was deafening. “Come out, Sherburn! Come out, you coward!” They was shaking their fists and their faces was red and twisted up with hate. I pushed up onto a woodpile a little ways down so I could see over their heads. I didn’t want to miss what happened.

But nothing happened. The house stayed quiet. All the windows was shut and the curtains was drawn. You couldn’t see a thing. It was like there was nobody home. This seemed to puzzle the crowd. They had come for a fight, and Sherburn wasn’t giving them one. The yelling started to get ragged, with some fellers shouting and others just muttering to each other.

“What’s he doin’ in there?” one man says, close to me. “Loadin’ his guns, I reckon.”

“He ain’t got enough lead to stop all of us,” another one says, but he didn’t sound as sure as he wanted to.

The lanky man at the gate was getting impatient. He rattled the gate and yelled again. “Don’t you make us come in there after you, Sherburn! It’ll be the worse for you if we do!”

Still nothing. The sun was getting lower, and long shadows was stretching across the yard. The quiet from that house was starting to feel bigger and heavier than all the noise the crowd was making. Some of the men at the back was starting to look around, like they was remembering they had chores to do at home. The whisky was starting to wear off a little, I reckoned.

Then the lanky man, he gets a new idea. He turns to a big, burly feller next to him, a blacksmith I’d seen around, and says, “Buck, you’re the strongest man here. Bust that gate down!”

The blacksmith, Buck, he looked at the gate, and then at the silent house, and he shuffled his feet. “Why me?” he says. “You’re the one doin’ all the talkin’. You bust it down.”

“I ain’t got the heft for it,” the lanky man says, puffing out his chest. “It’s a job for a man of strength. We’re all countin’ on you, Buck.”

Buck looked around at the faces staring at him, and he didn’t look happy. He didn’t want to be the first one through that gate. I could see it plain. He spit on the ground. “Well,” he says, slow-like, “I suppose somebody’s got to.” He took a step back to get a run at it.

And that’s when it happened.

It warn’t the front door that opened. It was a window on the second floor, right above the porch. It slid up with a quiet little scrape, and there was Colonel Sherburn. He didn’t have a pistol this time. He was holding a long rifle, and he rested the barrel of it on the window sill. He warn’t aiming it at anybody particular, just had it laying there, ready. He hadn’t put his coat back on; he was still in his shirtsleeves. His face was as ca’m as if he was about to ask what time it was.

The whole crowd went dead silent. You could have heard a pin drop in the dust. Everybody was looking up at him. Buck, the blacksmith, he was frozen in place, one foot back, ready to charge. He looked mighty foolish.

Sherburn let his eyes travel over the crowd, slow and deliberate. He looked at them for a long time, not saying a word. I felt my hands get sweaty. I never saw a man look so much by himself and so little afraid. He finally settled his gaze on the lanky man by the gate.

“You, with the ridiculous hat,” he says. His voice warn’t loud, but it cut through the air like a razor. “You seem to be the ringleader of this cowardly circus.”

The lanky man swallowed hard. His face warn’t red no more, it was pale. “We’re here for justice!” he squeaked out, but his voice was thin and shaky.

“Justice,” Sherburn says, and it sounded like he was tasting a bad piece of meat. “You cry for justice for a drunken fool who spent his life threatening men he was too scared to fight. A man you all laughed at and baited and used for your own amusement not an hour ago.” He moved his eyes over the rest of them. “And you, the rest of this brave army. You didn’t have the stomach to stop him when he was alive and making a nuisance of himself, but you’ve found your courage now that he’s dead and I’m one man alone in my house.”

Nobody said nothing. They just stared, their mouths hanging open. Some of them was looking at the ground, some at their feet. They looked like schoolboys caught by the teacher.

“I know you,” Sherburn went on, his voice still low and even. “I see you every day. You’re the farmers who cheat on the weight of your cotton, the merchants who sell sand for sugar, the loafers who live off your wives’ washing. The sound of your own voices has made you drunk on your own bravery. But you are not men. You’re the hind end of men. You brought a rope to lynch a man, but you’re afraid to come and get him. You’re cowards, every last one of you.”

He paused and let that sink in. The silence was terrible.

“Now listen to me,” he says, and his hand moved just a little on the stock of his rifle. “I am giving you one chance. Turn around and go home. Disperse. The first man who lays a hand on my fence, or my gate, or sets one foot inside my yard, I will kill him. I will not wound him. I will kill him as I would a dog.”

He stopped talking and just looked at them. The lanky man, he took a step back from the gate, real quiet. Then he took another one. He looked like he wanted to run but was too scared to turn his back.

For maybe a whole minute, nobody moved. The whole mob was just a statue. Then, from the back, a feller coughed and shuffled away. Then another one. It was like a dam breaking, only in reverse. A few at a time at first, and then in a rush, the crowd just melted away. They didn’t run, mostly. They just walked off, quiet, not looking at each other. They peeled off down the side streets and alleys, trying not to look like they was retreating. In less than five minutes, the street was empty.

All that was left was me on my woodpile and the lanky man’s white stovepipe hat, which had fallen off and got kicked into the ditch. The street was full of dust settling in the evening light.

Up in the window, Colonel Sherburn watched them go. He watched until the last man was out of sight. Then, slow and steady, he drew the rifle back inside, and the window slid shut. The house was quiet again. It was like the whole thing never happened.

I slid off the woodpile and my legs was shaking a little. I never saw anything like it. One man, he had faced down the whole town and made them back down without firing a shot. He just used words. And it was more frightening than if he’d shot a dozen of them. I went and picked up the stovepipe hat out of the ditch. It was all bent and dusty. I figured the lanky man wouldn’t be wanting it back right away.

I went on back toward the main part of town. It was getting on for supper time, but nobody seemed to be going home. People was gathered in little knots in front of the stores and on the corners, talking low. The excitement was gone, and something else was in its place. It felt like shame. They wouldn’t look you in the eye. They’d talk for a minute, and then just drift apart. The whole town felt like it had been whipped.

The next day was even quieter. It was Sunday, but it was quieter than any Sunday I ever seen. People went to church, and I heard later the preacher gave a long sermon about the evils of mob violence and the sanctity of human life. The same fellers that had been hollering for a rope yesterday was sitting in the pews looking pious and solemn and nodding their heads at everything the preacher said. It was enough to make you sick.

Boggs’s daughter, the sweet-looking girl who was about sixteen, she was taken in by the cooper’s wife. The town ladies, the same ones whose husbands had been in the mob, they took to visiting her all the time. They’d bring her cakes and pies and sit with her and pat her hand and tell her what a good man her father was, deep down. They told her the whole town was grieving with her. I seen them coming and going from the cooper’s house, with their baskets and their long faces, clucking like a bunch of hens that had just discovered a snake in the henhouse they’d been ignoring for years.

They had the funeral for Boggs on Monday. Pretty much the whole town turned out. They buried him in the little cemetery on the hill. The preacher said a lot of fine things about him, how he had a generous spirit and a good heart, and how he was a friend to all. You’d have thought he was the mayor, the way they went on. A few fellers that had got their fun out of Boggs for twenty years was crying into their handkerchiefs. I reckoned they was crying because their fun was over. His daughter stood by the grave, pale as a sheet, not making a sound. She was the only person there that looked like she was feeling anything real.

And Colonel Sherburn, he went on just like before. Monday morning, right after the funeral, he walked out of his white house and down to his store. I was sitting on a barrel across the street, whittling a stick, and I seen it. As he come down the street, people just moved out of his way. They didn’t run, they just sort of drifted to the other side of the street or stepped into a doorway to let him pass. The talking stopped. The whole street went quiet. He walked right down the middle of it, proud and straight, looking neither to the right nor the left. He unlocked his store, went inside, and the day’s business begun, just like any other day.

Only it warn’t like any other day. Everything was different. Before, Sherburn was just a proud, rich man that people resented a little. Now, he was something else. He was the man who killed Boggs in broad daylight and then faced down a lynch mob and shamed them into running away. They didn't lynch him, and they didn't arrest him. The constable, he made himself scarce for two days, said he had business over in the next county. Nobody did nothing.

A few days later, I was loafing down by the steamboat landing, and Sherburn comes walking along the riverbank. He was alone, like always. He stopped not far from me and stood looking out at the water. The river was high that spring, and it was chewing away at the bank, just like the passage said. A big chunk of mud and roots slid into the water with a soft sucking sound while he stood there.

He must have known I was there, but he didn’t look at me for a long time. Then he turned his head, slow.

“Boy,” he says. “You were there. At my house.”

I didn’t know what to say, so I just nodded. My heart was thumping.

“You saw it all,” he says. It warn’t a question. He looked back at the river. “What do you think of this town?”

I swallowed. “I, I don’t know, sir,” I says. “I reckon it’s just a town.”

He stood there for another minute, and then he turned and started walking back toward the town. He stopped and looked back at me one more time.

“You’re a floater, ain’t you, boy? No folks here.”

“No, sir,” I says.

The town couldn't let it go, though. They couldn't get at Sherburn, so they took to doing good works. It's a thing I've noticed. When folks is feeling bad about themselves for being cowards, they get awful busy with charity. It sort of salves the wound, I reckon. The main person they got busy on was Boggs’s daughter.

She was staying with the Baptist preacher and his wife, a woman with a face like a dried apple and a heart just as squeezed. The ladies of the town took to visiting her every afternoon. They’d bring her little plates of pound cake and pots of jam, and sit in the preacher’s parlor fanning themselves and saying how it was their Christian duty to look after the poor orphan. I’d see them coming and going, all dressed up in their Sunday best, their voices low and sweet like they was at a funeral, which in a way, I suppose they was.

I was fixing a loose board on the preacher’s fence for a dime one afternoon and I could hear them through the open window.

“The poor dear,” one of them says. “She just sits and stares. Hasn’t said ten words all week.”

“It’s the shock,” another one says. “To see her own father struck down in the street. It’s a horror that would mark a soul for life.”

“And that man,” the first one whispers, and I knowed she meant Sherburn, “to walk the streets as bold as brass. It’s a scandal against God and man.”

Then the preacher’s wife chimes in. “The Lord works in mysterious ways,” she says, like that explained everything. “We must pray for the girl’s soul, and for this town’s deliverance from sin.”

But I noticed they never prayed for Sherburn’s soul. He was past praying for, I reckoned. Or maybe they was just too scared to mention his name to God, for fear he might overhear.

The whole thing come to a head with that lanky man, Pettibone. After getting shamed by Sherburn on his own porch, he laid low for a day or two. Then he come out with a new idea. He said the town had a moral obligation. He said that since they couldn’t provide justice, they must provide succor. He used big words like that, words he’d likely read in a book somewhere. What he meant was, they ought to take up a collection for Boggs’s girl.

Well, the town took to that idea like a tick to a dog. It was perfect for them. It let them feel righteous and brave, all without having to do anything dangerous. They could fight Sherburn with their wallets, which was a good deal safer than fighting him with fence rails.

Pettibone was in his glory again. He was the man with the plan. He set up a table outside the courthouse with a big jar on it, and a sign he’d painted himself. It said, “FOR THE ORPHAN OF OUR FALLEN BROTHER. LET BRICKSVILLE SHOW ITS HEART.” He stood there all day, taking dimes and quarters and making a great show of it. Every time someone dropped a coin in, he’d boom out, “The Lord loves a cheerful giver!” and the whole street would know another soul had done its duty.

They decided to have a big to-do at the town hall to present the money to the girl. It was to be a proper event, with speeches and prayers. Pettibone was to be the master of ceremonies. It was all anybody talked about for a week. They was more excited about giving away that money than they was about anything since the circus left town. It was another show, you see. And that town loved a show.

The night of the presentation, the hall was packed. Everybody was there, all sweating in their stiff collars and their best dresses. The air was thick with the smell of cheap perfume and self-satisfaction. They had the girl, Mary was her name, sitting on the stage in a hard-backed chair. She was all in black, and she looked like a little half-drowned sparrow between the preacher’s wife and Pettibone, who was puffed up like a toad on a hot rock.

I’d snuck in and found a spot in the back, behind a pillar. I didn’t want to be there, but I had to see it. It was like watching a snake eat a frog; you want to look away, but you can’t.

The preacher said a long prayer that was mostly about how good and charitable the people of Bricksville was. Then a choir of ladies sung a hymn that was all mournful and droopy. Finally, it was Pettibone’s turn.

He stood up and walked to the front of the stage, holding a canvas bag full of the collection money. He let the bag drop onto the table with a heavy clink, and a satisfied murmur went through the crowd.

“Friends! Neighbors! Citizens of this great and compassionate community!” he started, his voice ringing out. He went on like that for what seemed like an hour. He talked about tragedy and community, about Christian duty and civic pride. He called Boggs a “fallen son of Arkansaw,” which was laying it on a bit thick for a man who was mostly known for his monthly drunk. He called the girl a “tender blossom blighted by a cruel frost.” He was sweating and waving his arms, and the crowd was eating it up. They’d clap and say “Amen!” every time he’d pause for breath. He never once said Sherburn’s name, but he was there in every word, a big, dark shadow behind all the flowery talk.

Finally, he got to the end of it. He turned and took Mary by the hand. Her hand was white and limp in his big red one.

“And so, Mary,” he says, his voice all thick with feeling, “on behalf of this entire town, a town that weeps with you, a town that holds you in its heart… we present you with this token of our esteem and our deepest, most profound sympathy.”

He picked up the money bag and held it out to her. The whole hall was dead quiet. Everybody was leaning forward. This was the big moment. This was the moment they’d all paid their dime for.

Mary looked at the bag. Then she looked up at Pettibone. Her face was pale as a sheet, but her eyes, for the first time, had a fire in them.

“I don’t want it,” she says.

Her voice warn’t loud, but in that silent hall it carried like a pistol shot.

Pettibone blinked. He give a little laugh, like he thought she was joking. “Now, now, child,” he says, all fatherly. “This is for you. For your future.”

“I don’t want it,” she said again, a little louder this time. She pulled her hand away from his. “I don’t want your money.”

A confused whisper went through the crowd. Pettibone’s face started to get red. The show was going wrong.

“Daughter,” the preacher says, standing up. “You are overwrought. These good people mean only to help.”

“Help?” she says, and she stood up from her chair. She was small, but right then she seemed to fill the whole stage. “You want to help? Where were you when he was yelling in the street? You laughed at him. You all did. You thought it was fun. Where were you when that man told him to be gone by one o’clock? You stood and listened. You did nothing.”

Her voice was shaking, but it was strong. “And when he shot my father down like a dog, where were you then? You formed a mob. Not for justice. For a spectacle. Because you are bored and you are cowards. And when he called you cowards, you ran. And now you bring me your spare change and you call it charity. You call it a great and compassionate community.”

She looked out over all their faces, and for the first time, I seen them look ashamed. Truly ashamed.

“This ain’t charity,” she says, her voice dropping to a fierce whisper. “This is the price of your guilt. And I won’t take it. I won’t let you buy your peace with my father’s blood.”

She turned her back on them, on all of them, and walked to the edge of the stage. She didn’t go back to her chair. She just stood there, facing the back wall, her little shoulders shaking.

The hall was so quiet you could have heard a pin drop on a feather bed. Pettibone stood there with the money bag in his hand, looking foolish. The preacher’s wife was fanning herself like her life depended on it. The whole town, for once, had nothing to say.

Then, from the back of the hall, a man stood up. It was Colonel Sherburn.

He’d been there the whole time, standing right by the door, just inside the shadows. Nobody had seen him come in. He hadn’t made a sound. He was dressed just as he always was, proud and clean, and he had his hat in his hand.

He walked down the center aisle. The people parted for him like the Red Sea. They didn’t just move out of his way, they shrank from him. He walked right up to the stage and stood at the bottom of the steps. He didn’t look at the crowd. He didn’t look at Pettibone. He looked only at the girl’s back.

“Miss Boggs,” he says. His voice was the same as ever, calm and low, but it had no mockery in it now. It was just a voice.

She turned around, slow. Her face was wet with tears, but her chin was up. She looked at the man who killed her father, and she didn’t flinch.

“I have no words of apology to offer you,” Sherburn said, looking her square in the eye. “What was done, was done. I will not say I am sorry, for I am not. Your father was a fool who courted death, and he found it.”

A gasp went through the crowd. It was a brutal thing to say, but it was honest. And in that hall full of lies, the honesty of it was like a cold wind.

“But,” he went on, “you are not your father. You have a spine, which is more than can be said for this entire town. You will not survive here. They will either smother you with their pity or poison you with their contempt. Both are a kind of death.”

He reached into his coat and took out a long envelope. “The nine o’clock packet for St. Louis leaves tomorrow morning. This envelope contains a ticket for passage. It also contains a letter of introduction to a headmistress of a ladies’ academy, where your tuition and board will be taken care of. And it contains a draft on a St. Louis bank for a sum of money sufficient that you will never need to accept the charity of cowards.”

He laid the envelope on the edge of the stage.

“The choice is yours,” he said. “You can stay here and be a monument to their hypocrisy. Or you can leave and make a life.”

He turned, and without a glance at anyone, walked back up the aisle and out the door, into the night.

For a long minute, nobody moved. Then Mary, the girl, walked to the edge of the stage. She bent down and picked up the envelope. She didn’t open it. She just held it. She looked out at the crowd one last time, at all the stunned, foolish faces. Then she turned and walked off the stage and out a side door, without a sound.

The show was over. People started to get up, quiet, not looking at each other. They shuffled out of the hall like they was leaving a church. The bag of money was still sitting on the table, looking heavy and worthless. Pettibone was just standing there, a big, empty man on a big, empty stage.

This whole idea of Southern bravery and Southern justice you’re talking about, it’s a lie. You want to lynch somebody? Then do it. Don’t stand there talking about it.”

He looked right at Buck, who was still frozen on his stump. “You, with the hat. You was the one doing the talking. Step forward. Let’s see you lead. Or do you need to borrow another man’s bottle before you can borrow his courage?”

Buck turned a pasty white. He looked around, like he was hoping somebody would say something, do something. But nobody did. Every man was looking at the ground, or at his neighbor, or at the back of the man in front of him. They wasn’t a mob no more. They was just a bunch of fellers standing in the street, looking ashamed of themselves.

Sherburn watched them for another long minute. Then he let out a short, ugly laugh. It was the most contemptuous sound I ever heard.

“The pitifulest thing out,” he said again, almost to himself. He cocked the squirrel rifle. Not to aim it, just the sound of it, a loud, final click that made every man there jump. “Now get. And don’t come back.”

He turned around, slow, and went back in the house, and shut the door. The lock turning was as loud as a gunshot in the quiet.

The mob just sort of melted. It didn’t break up and run; it just dissolved, like a clod of dirt in the river. Men started peeling off from the edges, walking away quiet, not looking at each other. It seemed like Sherburn had killed Boggs all over again, and this time he’d killed the whole town’s idea of itself right along with him.

I wandered back towards the main street, feeling kind of hollowed out. The excitement was all gone, and what was left behind felt sour and stale. The town looked the same, but it felt different. It was like seeing a pretty apple and then finding out it was rotten all the way to the core.

I was heading back towards the river, thinking I’d had about enough of towns for one lifetime, when I heard my name called. Not my real name, but the one I was using.

What could I do? I didn’t want a king and a duke on the raft, especially these two, but I didn’t want to get them mad, neither. They looked like they could be mean if they was crossed. So I just says, “This way,” and led them down to where I’d hid the canoe.

I knowed he was right. It was one thing for me to be helping a runaway slave. It was another thing altogether to have a runaway white girl on the same raft. The folks along the river, they wouldn’t stop to ask questions. They’d just see what they wanted to see, and it would be the end of us.

“So what’ll we do?” I says. “We can’t keep her here.”

“No. But we cain’t take her back to dat town, neither. Dat place is poison for her now. And we cain’t just put her off on the bank somewheres.” He looked over at the sleeping girl, and his face softened. “Look at her, Huck. She’s just a child. Lost everything she got. We got to help her.”

That was the thing about Jim. He was always thinking about other folks. Even with his own freedom on the line, he was worried about this girl he didn’t even know.

“She got any kin?” he asked.

“I don’t know. I didn’t ask.”

“Well, we got to ask. Come morning, we got to find out if she got an aunt or a grandma somewhere downriver. Somewhere safe. Dat’s our only play, Huck. We got to get her to her own folks.”

That made sense. It gave us a purpose. We warn’t just harboring her; we was transporting her. It still felt dangerous, but it was a plan.

We let the fire die down to embers and took turns on watch. The night was still and quiet, but it didn’t feel peaceful no more. It felt like we was sitting on a barrel of gunpowder, just waiting for a spark. Every time a log shifted on the shore, or a big fish jumped, I’d startle and look around, expecting to see a skiff full of men with guns.

When my watch was done, I laid down, but I couldn’t sleep for a long time. I kept seeing Colonel Sherburn’s face, all calm and proud, and poor old Boggs falling backwards in the street. And now his daughter was here, sleeping just a few feet away from me, her life all tangled up with mine and Jim’s. Pap always said that helping people was a mug’s game and only ever led to trouble. I was beginning to think that for once, the old man might have been right. But then I thought about her face when she talked about the river, about how she wanted it to be quiet under the water, and I knowed I couldn’t have done nothing different. Trouble or no, we was in it now, all three of us, floating down the middle of the big, dark river together.

The next morning, the girl, she told us her name was Mary, was still quiet, but the wild look was gone from her eyes. She looked tired and sad, but she looked like a person again. I give her some more coffee and a piece of fish Jim had caught on his trot-line.

I got around to asking her, same as Jim said I should. “Mary,” I says, “you got any folks anywhere? An aunt, or some cousins?”

She looked out at the water. “I got an aunt. My mother’s sister. Her name’s Polly, and her husband is Silas. They live in a town called New Madrid. It’s downriver. A long ways down.”

“New Madrid,” I says, trying the name out. I’d heard of it. It was a good ways, all right. Days of travel on a raft.

“You think they’d take you in?” Jim asked.

“I reckon so,” she says. “Aunt Polly always sent me things for my birthday. She’s a kind woman. But I ain’t seen her since I was little.”

That was it, then. That was the plan. We’d take her to New Madrid. It was a risky business, a powerful risky business, but it felt better than just drifting. It felt like we was doing something right.

We decided we’d have to travel mostly at night, same as before, only now we’d have to be ten times as careful. A girl on the raft was a thing that couldn’t be explained away. During the day, we’d have to tie up in the thickest willows we could find and keep quiet as ghosts.

The first day went by slow. We was tied up in a thicket of cottonwoods on the Missouri side, where the current had dug out a deep channel behind a towhead. You couldn’t see us from the river at all. Mary sat quiet most of the day. She asked me about the raft, and where I come from, and I told her a few things, but not the whole truth. You can’t ever tell nobody the whole truth. It’s too dangerous. I told her me and Jim was partners in a trading venture, going down to New Orleans. She seemed to believe it. It was easier than telling her I was a runaway boy and he was a runaway slave.

That evening, just as the sun was going down, we heard it. Chug-chug-chug-a-chug. A steamboat, coming upriver. That was normal enough. But then we heard another sound, the putter of a small gas engine. That was a patrol skiff. We all got down flat on the raft.

The skiff come around the head of the island, slow, two men in it with shotguns resting across their laps. They was looking over the banks real careful.

“See anything?” one of them called out.

“Nothin’ but snags and gators,” the other one called back. “Heard they was lookin’ for some runaway girl from over at Pokeville. Drowned herself, most likely.”

“Her old man was that Boggs feller, the one Sherburn shot. Good riddance to bad rubbish, I say. The whole family was trash.”

The men in the skiff laughed. I felt Mary flinch beside me, like she’d been struck. I wanted to stand up and holler something at them, something awful, but I held myself still. Jim’s hand was on my arm, holding me down. The patrol boat puttered on past our hiding spot and disappeared around the bend.

We was all quiet for a long time after. The cruelty of it just hung in the air.

Finally, Jim says, real low, “De world is a hard place, chil’ren. A powerful hard place.”

Mary didn’t cry this time. She just laid there, her face a pale blur in the twilight. When she finally spoke, her voice was cold and steady as a stone.

“I want to get to New Madrid,” she says. “I want to get to my Aunt Polly.”

That night, when we untied the raft and let the current take us, it felt different. It warn’t just me and Jim running away from our troubles no more. We was running for Mary, too. And it felt different from just running away. It felt like we was running *to* something, for a change. It was a heavy feeling, carrying all that hope and all that danger on our little raft, but it was a good feeling, too. It was the best feeling I’d had in a long, long time.

It seemed like being in a crowd just brought out the worst in a person. It made them mean and cowardly and righteous all at the same time. I decided right then and there that the river was the only place for a person to be, where you didn’t have to answer to nobody but yourself and the current.

The next few nights was all the same, and yet they was all different. We’d lay up in the willows all day, me and Jim and Mary, listening to the birds and the bugs and the river lapping against the shore. Jim, he was good to her. He’d fish off the edge of the raft and cook up a catfish for her, real careful, picking out all the bones. He’d talk to her in his low, soft voice, not about nothing in particular, just about the river, and the stars, and how you could tell the weather by the way the frogs was singing. He never asked her no questions about what happened. He just let her be, and was there for her when she needed it.

I tried to do my part too. I told her stories, funny ones, about the Widow Douglas and her snuff, and about how Tom Sawyer got the whole town to whitewash a fence for him. At first she didn’t do nothing but nod, but after a while I seen a little smile twitch at the corner of her mouth. It warn’t much, but it was a start.

One afternoon, when the sun was beating down something fierce and the air was so thick you could chew it, she finally started talking.

“He warn’t always like that, you know,” she says, her voice not much more than a whisper. “My pa.”

She learned how to bait a hook, and how to steer the raft with the big sweep oar. She was smart, and a quick learner. She even started to laugh sometimes, a real laugh, at some of Jim’s stories or my foolishness.

We seen more skiffs, and sometimes we’d hear voices calling out from the shore at night. We was running low on supplies, too. “We’s gwyne to have to go to a town, Huck,” Jim says one night, as we was floating along under a sky full of stars. “We needs flour, en salt, en some side-meat. En Miss Mary, she needs some proper vittles.”

I knew he was right, but the thought of it made my skin crawl. After what happened in Pokeville, I didn’t want to set foot in another town as long as I lived. But Mary was looking thin, and her dress was getting ragged.

We picked a town called New Par-is, a sleepy-looking little place on the Kentucky side. It looked peaceful enough from the river. He didn’t like it, and I didn’t like it neither, but it was too risky for him to go ashore.

“You be careful, Huck,” he says, his voice full of worry. “Folks is suspicious in dese little towns. You an’ Miss Mary, you act like you belongs.”

We decided on a story. We was brother and sister, come down from upriver to visit our sick grandma. It was a flimsy kind of story, but it was the best we could come up with. Mary had tied a scarf over her hair, and I had dirtied my face up a bit. We looked like any other pair of river rats.

We paddled the canoe up to the town landing just as the sun was coming up, painting the sky all pink and orange. The town was just starting to wake up. A few men was lounging on the porch of the general store, spitting tobacco juice into the dust. They watched us tie up the canoe, their eyes cold and curious.

“Mornin’,” I says, trying to sound cheerful.

One of them, a big feller with a greasy beard, just grunted.

There was a general store, a blacksmith, a saloon, and a little whitewashed church with a crooked steeple. The air smelled of mud and woodsmoke and something else, something sweet and cloying.

As we got closer to the center of town, we started to hear it: singing. A whole crowd of people, singing loud and off-key. We rounded a corner and seen where it was coming from. In a big clearing behind the church, there was a huge tent set up, and folks was pouring into it.

“What is it?” Mary whispered.

“Looks like a revival meeting,” I says. I’d seen them before. Preachers would travel from town to town, whipping people up into a frenzy about sin and salvation. It was another kind of show, just like the Duke and the King used to put on.

The general store was right next to the clearing, so we had to walk past the tent to get to it. A man was standing out front, a skinny feller in a black suit, with sweat stains under his arms. He was handing out pamphlets and exhorting people to come inside and be saved.

“Come, friends, come!” he hollered, his voice hoarse. “Come and hear the Reverend Gideon Blaylock cast out the demons of sin and sorrow! Are you burdened? Are you heavy-laden? The Reverend will lift your spirits and cleanse your soul!”

He tried to hand me a pamphlet, but I dodged him. We went into the store. It was dark and cool inside, and smelled of coffee and leather and pickled herrings. An old man with spectacles perched on the end of his nose looked up from his newspaper.

“Help ya?” he says.

“Yes, sir,” I says, pulling out the little bit of money we had. “We need some flour, and salt, and some bacon if you’ve got it. And maybe some coffee.”

He squinted at me. “That depends. You folks just passin’ through?”

And the sheriff from across the river sent word about some runaway girl. Daughter of that Boggs feller that got hisself shot. Sixteen years old, pale, sweet-lookin’. Says there’s a reward.”

I have been dispossessed.”

The young one, Bridgewater, he looked surprised. “Dispossessed of what, old gentleman?”

The old man wiped a tear from his eye with the back of his hand. “You see before you, young sirs,” he says, his voice trembling, “the wretched and pitiable remnant of a once-great line. You see before you the rightful King of France!”

Well, I just stared at him. Bridgewater, he stared too.

“The King of France?” Bridgewater says, kind of doubtful. “Which one?”

“The only one!” the old man says, getting a bit riled. “The late, lamented Dauphin! Louis the Seventeenth, son of Louis the Sixteenth and Marie Antoinette!” He sniffled. “They said I died in the temple, but it was a foul lie. I was spirited away, brought to this savage country, and left to languish in obscurity. For years I have wandered, a king in rags, my royal birthright denied me. In that last town, I was engaged in a temperance revival, collecting a small pittance for the missionaries, when some heartless ruffians accused me of drinking the collection money myself. The indignity of it! A king, accused of common theft!” He broke down and started to sob, a great blubbering noise.

The young one, Bridgewater, he looked at the old man, and then he looked at me, and he didn’t say nothing for a minute. Then he goes over to the old man and puts a hand on his shoulder.

“Your Majesty,” he says, his voice all soft and respectful. “I had no idea. To think that I should share my flight with royalty! It is an honor. Allow me to kiss your hand.”

And he done it, too. He bent down and kissed the old man’s dirty hand. The old man, he perked right up. He stopped crying and puffed out his chest.

“Rise, my good friend,” he says. “Your loyalty has not gone unnoticed. You shall be rewarded when I am restored to my throne. For now, you may call me Your Majesty. This young man here can be my valet.” He pointed at me.

Well, I didn’t know what to say. I knowed he warn’t no more a king than I was, and I figured the young one knowed it too. But the young one, he just bowed and says, “It would be an honor, Your Majesty. But might I suggest that I, with my worldly experience, would be better suited as your chief counsel and Duke? Perhaps the Duke of Bridgewater?”

The old man thought about it for a second. “Very well,” he says. “The Duke of Bridgewater it is. And what of the boy?”

“He can be our loyal retainer,” says the Duke. “He seems a good-hearted lad.”

And that’s how I ended up with a king and a duke on my raft. I seen right away they was both frauds, and I reckoned the duke only played along with the king so he could feel superior, him being a duke and all. I told all this to Jim later that night when they was asleep. Jim, he was quiet for a bit.

“Well, Huck,” he says, “dese kings is powerful strange. I never seen one before. But if he says he’s a king, maybe we ought to treat him like one. It don’t cost us nuffin’, and it might keep trouble away.”

I thought that was pretty good sense. Jim was often full of good sense. So I decided I’d let them have their way. It was easier than arguing.

But it sure did change things on the raft. The king and the duke, they took my bed, because they said it was the royal bedstead, and Jim and me had to sleep out in the rain when it rained. They made us do all the paddling and fishing, and they ate first at every meal. The duke would sit there and say, “Your Majesty, might I trouble you for another piece of that catfish?” and the king would say, “You may, my good duke, you may.” And they’d go on like that, all formal and polite, while they was eating up all the best parts.

After a couple of days of this, the duke, he got restless. “Your Majesty,” he says to the king, “this life of rustic simplicity is all well and good, but it is not filling our coffers. We are men of genius, wasting our talents on the barren air. We need a project.”

“What do you propose, my good duke?” says the king, who was laid up on his back, digesting his breakfast.

“A theatrical exhibition!” the duke says, his eyes gleaming. “We shall bring culture to these benighted river towns! Shakespeare! The immortal bard!”

The king, he looked doubtful. “Shakespeare?” he says. “I ain’t too familiar with his works. I done a little reclaiming of souls at camp-meetings, and a bit of doctoring with a patent medicine, and I can tell a fortune tolerably well, but I don’t know nothing about no Shakespeare.”

“Leave it to me!” the duke says. “I have trod the boards myself. I know all the best speeches. We shall put together a program of unparalleled dramatic intensity. The balcony scene from Romeo and Juliet, the sword fight from Richard the Third, and for the grand finale, the sublime soliloquy from Hamlet!”

He jumped up and struck a pose. “To be, or not to be; that is the bare bodkin,” he declaimed, waving his arms around. “Who would fardels bear, to grunt and sweat under a weary life, but that the dread of something after death puzzles the will, and makes us rather sling the arrows of outrageous fortune than fly to others that we know not of. It is a consummation devoutly to be wished!”

Well, it sounded grand, but it didn't sound quite right to me, from what little I'd heard of it. But the king, he was impressed. “By the godlings, duke!” he says. “That is talent! We shall make a fortune!”

So the duke set to work. He found an old bit of paper and a stub of a pencil, and he started making up a handbill. He’d walk up and down the raft, spouting lines of poetry and writing them down. He had the king practice the sword fight with a hickory stick, but the king was so clumsy and old he kept tripping over his own feet, so the duke decided he would play both Richard and Richmond himself. The king’s part was to be Juliet.

“But I’m a man,” the king whined. “And an old man at that.”

“Nonsense!” the duke says. “In Shakespeare’s time, all the female roles were played by boys. You shall wear a costume. It will be a triumph of the dramatic art.”

So for the next day or two, the raft was a regular play-acting place. The duke would be up on a box, being Romeo. “But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? It is the east, and Juliet is the sun! Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon!” And the king, he’d be hiding behind a blanket, and he’d squeak, in a high, cracked voice, “Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?” It was the ridiculousest thing I ever see.

When the handbill was done, the duke read it out to us. It was a masterpiece of foolishness. It said:

SHAKESPEAREAN REVIVAL!!!
WONDERFUL ATTRACTION!
FOR ONE NIGHT ONLY!
AT THE POKEVILLE COURTHOUSE!
The world renowned tragedians
DAVID GARRICK THE YOUNGER, of Drury Lane Theatre, London,
and
EDMUND KEAN THE ELDER, of the Royal Haymarket Theatre,
In their thrilling rendition of scenes from the immortal bard!
Including:
The Balcony Scene from ROMEO AND JULIET!
Romeo . . . . . . Mr. Garrick.
Juliet . . . . . . . Mr. Kean.
The thrilling Sword Fight from RICHARD III!
Richard . . . . . . Mr. Garrick.
Richmond . . . . Mr. Garrick.
And the heart-stopping, soul-searching Soliloquy from HAMLET!
By Mr. Garrick!
ADMISSION 25 CENTS. CHILDREN AND SERVANTS 10 CENTS.
LADIES AND CHILDREN NOT ADMITTED.

“There,” says the duke, mighty proud. “That ought to fetch them.”

“But why ain’t ladies and children admitted?” I says.

The duke winked. “That, my boy, is the secret to a successful enterprise. It suggests a certain… piquancy. A dash of the forbidden. It will make every man in the county desperate to see what it is they ain’t supposed to see.”

I thought it was a mean trick, but I didn’t say so. We was floating towards a little one-horse town that looked like it would be Pokeville, and the duke was all excited. I warn’t. I had a bad feeling about it. After what I seen in the last town, with Boggs and Sherburn and the mob, I was about ready to give up on people altogether. And now I was tied up with two of the biggest frauds on the whole Mississippi River, and we was headed right back into the thick of it. All I wanted was peace and quiet, and it looked like I warn’t gwyne to get it anytime soon.

so away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering, right down the middle of the street towards Sherburn’s house. I was right in amongst them, not because I wanted to be, but because the crowd was so thick you couldn’t do nothing but go where it went. It was like being a log in a river flood. Men that was hiding in doorways and behind cracker barrels when the shooting happened was now the loudest ones, shaking their fists and saying what they’d do to Sherburn. A big, fat man with a sweaty face, that I’d seen a-fanning himself under the awning of the store and not saying boo, was now at the front, his voice booming out like a foghorn. “We’ll string him up!” he yells. “We’ll show him what Arkansaw justice is!”

Everybody cheered at that, and some fellers that had their bottles with them took another pull, just to get their justice-makin’ courage up to the right point. The street was mostly mud and dust, and the running and stomping kicked up a cloud so thick you could hardly see the man in front of you. It was hot and close, and smelled of whisky and sweat and people that was too excited to be clean. I thought about slipping off down a side alley, but there was a kind of awful curiosity that kept my feet moving along with the rest of them. I wanted to see what a man like Colonel Sherburn would do when a whole town come for him.

His house warn’t far. It was at the end of the main street where the town started to give out into scrub trees and cotton fields. It was a good house, for that town; two stories high, and painted white, with a porch that run all the way around the front and green shutters on the windows. It had a nice picket fence, too, painted white like the house. The whole place looked calm and proud, like it didn’t have nothing to do with the ruckus that was boiling up to its front gate.

The mob come to a halt there, spilling out across the road. The fat man, he tried to keep his steam up. He rattled the gate, but he didn’t open it. “Come out, Sherburn!” he yells, but his voice didn’t sound quite so much like a foghorn now. It had a little tremble in it. “Come out and face the people!”

The whole crowd got quiet then, waiting. All you could hear was fellers breathing hard and shuffling their feet in the dust. The sun was beating down something awful. I seen the lanky man that had acted out the shooting; he’d pushed his big fur hat back on his head and was looking at the house like he was trying to figure out the best place to stand to get a good view. He warn’t saying nothing now, though. Nobody was. The whisky courage was starting to leak out of them. They’d run all the way here expecting to do something terrible, and now that they was here, nobody seemed to know what the next step was. They just stood there, a big, uneasy herd of men, staring at a closed door.

Then the door opened.

Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the porch. He didn’t have a coat on, just his white shirt and trousers, and he was holding a double-barreled shotgun, not aiming it, just holding it comfortable-like in the crook of his arm. He walked to the edge of the porch and stood there, looking down at the crowd. He warn’t in a hurry. He looked them all over, slow and deliberate, from one end of the crowd to the other. His face was just as ca’m as it was when he told Boggs he had till one o’clock. He looked like a man that was looking at a pack of stray dogs that had wandered into his yard.

Nobody moved. Nobody said a word. The fat man at the gate, he seemed to shrink down inside his clothes. You could feel the whole mob starting to wish they was somewhere else.

After a minute of the most dreadful quiet I ever felt, Sherburn spoke. His voice warn’t loud, but it was clear and cold, and it cut through the hot air like a razor.

“You can’t think,” he says, looking right over their heads like he was talking to somebody on a hill behind them. “You don’t know what you’re about. The idea of a lynch-mob is to work in the dark, and with masks on. It ain’t a daytime job, for real men. But you’re not real men. You’re the pitiful leavings of men.”

He let that sink in for a second. He looked down at the fat man, who was trying to hide behind the gatepost now.

“I know you,” he says. “I know every one of you. I see you every day. You borrow my money. You buy my goods. You elect my candidates. And you call yourselves men. You, Buck Harkness,” he says, pointing a finger, not the gun, at the lanky feller with the stovepipe hat. “You think you’re a man because you can get a crowd to follow you, so long as you’re all going the same way. You was brave enough to mark out the ground where a dead man fell. But would you have faced Boggs by yourself? No. You’d have run.”

The lanky man looked like he’d been struck. He tried to say something, but no words come out. He just kind of gulped and took a step back, bumping into the fellers behind him.

Sherburn went on, his voice filled with a kind of tired contempt. “A man stands up to a man. He looks him in the eye. He don’t hide behind a thousand other fellers and holler for blood. You want to lynch me. Why? Because I killed a man? The man I killed was a drunken fool who threatened my life in front of this whole town, and I gave him fair warning. I told him what I would do, and I did it. I’m a man. I live by my own lights.”

He rested the shotgun on the porch rail, still not aiming it, just resting it there. The two black holes of the barrels looked like eyes.

“But you,” he says, and his voice got a little louder. “You’re not men. You’re a mob. And a mob ain’t nothing but a coward. The bravest man in a mob is still a coward, because he’s got to have the mob with him. You came here to lynch me. So do it. Here I am. Step forward. Who’s first? Who will be the man to put the rope around my neck?”

He waited. The silence was so thick now you could have cut it with a knife and served it for dinner. Everybody looked at everybody else. Nobody wanted to be the first one. Everybody was waiting for somebody else to be the man Sherburn was talking about. The fat feller at the gate, he unlatched it, but not to go in. He opened it just enough to slip out, and he started walking away, quiet-like, trying not to make no noise.

Then another feller on the edge of the crowd remembered he had something important to do back at his store, and he peeled off. Then three or four more. They didn’t run. They just kind of melted away, back into the dust they’d kicked up.

Sherburn watched them go. A little smile, if you could call it that, played on his lips. It was a nasty-looking smile.

“That’s what I thought,” he says, his voice back to being quiet and cold. “You’re half-men, the lot of you. You didn’t come to lynch me. You came to see a lynching. You came to get all worked up and feel brave for five minutes, and then go home and tell your women how you run me out of town. Well, you’ve had your look. Now go home.”

He turned his back on them. He just turned his back on a whole mob that had come to kill him, and he walked back into his house and shut the door. He didn’t even slam it. It just clicked shut, soft and final.

Well, that was the end of it. The whole crowd just fell apart, like a rotten pumpkin. In two minutes, there warn’t twenty people left in the road. In five minutes, the street was empty, except for me and the dust, and the lanky man’s stovepipe hat, which had fell off and got trampled in the mud. He hadn’t even stopped to pick it up.

I stood there for a minute, looking at the white house with the green shutters. It was just a house again. I felt sickish. Not scared, just sick. Sick of the whole thing. The shooting was awful. And the crowd wanting to lynch Sherburn was awful. But somehow, the way Sherburn turned them all into scared children and sent them home, that felt awfulest of all. It made you feel ashamed of the whole human race.

I didn’t want to be in that town another minute. It felt like the air was poisoned. I slid out of there, keeping to the back ways and alleys, and I didn’t stop till I got to the edge of town where the woods started. I pushed through the brush and down the bank to where I’d left the raft tied up in a little hidden slough.

The river was there, big and brown and steady, and the raft was sitting on it, peaceful as you please. I untied the rope and shoved off into the current. The raft swung out into the middle of the river, and the town started to get smaller behind me. I laid down on the deck and let the sun bake the town-feeling out of me. The only sound was the gentle lapping of the water against the logs and the hum of the insects in the air. It was a powerful soothing sound.

After a while, Jim poked his head out from under the wigwam. He’d been hiding in there, like I told him, so nobody would see him.

“Is dey gone, Huck?” he says, his voice low and worried. “Is de noise all done?”

“It’s all done, Jim,” I says. “We’re clear of it.”

He come out and sat down on the edge of the raft, letting his feet dangle in the water. He looked back at the town, which was just a little smudge in the distance now.

“Dat was a pow’ful lot of shoutin’,” he says. “I was gettin’ mighty scared in here. I hear shootin’, and den I hear a whole mess of folks runnin’ and yellin’. I reckon sumfin’ bad happen.”

“Something bad happened, all right,” I says. I didn’t feel like telling him the whole story. It was too ugly to put into words right then. “A man got shot.”

Jim shook his head, slow and sad. “Always de same in dese towns, ain’t it? Folks gettin’ all het up and hurtin’ each other. A man ain’t safe on de shore. De river, she’s safe. She don’ git mad and try to lynch you.”

“No,” I says, looking up at the big empty sky. “The river don’t.”

We didn’t say nothing for a long time after that. We just floated. The sun got lower and turned the water all red and gold, and the air got cool, and the first stars started to come out. It was so peaceful it made the memory of that town and the mob and Sherburn’s cold voice feel like a bad dream. I thought about Boggs’s daughter, the poor thing, screaming over her father. I reckoned she was still back there, in that awful town, with all them awful people. And I was out here, on the river, free of it. It didn’t seem right, but it was the way it was.

“Huck,” Jim says after a while, his voice soft in the dusk. “Dat man dat got shot. Was he a bad man?”

I thought about it. I thought about what the feller said, that Boggs was the best-naturedest old fool in Arkansaw, and never hurt nobody. And I thought about him tearing through town on his horse, drunk and yelling that he was on the war-path.

“I don’t know, Jim,” I says. “I don’t rightly know. He was a fool when he was drunk. But I don’t reckon he was bad. Not clear through. He just made the wrong man mad.”

“An’ de other man?” Jim says. “De one dat shot him. Was he a bad man?”

That was a harder question. Colonel Sherburn was a murderer, plain and simple. But I’d seen him face down that whole mob with nothing but his own grit. He was brave, you had to give him that. But it was a cold, cruel kind of bravery.

“He’s… different, Jim,” I says. “He ain’t like anybody I ever seen. He’s got sand, a whole heap of it. But I think maybe his heart is made of the same stuff.”

Jim didn’t say nothing, he just nodded, like he understood. Maybe he did. Jim understood a lot of things that educated folks didn’t seem to have a grasp on.

We floated on into the night, leaving Arkansaw and its troubles behind us. The raft drifted silent, and the stars was thick as spatter-work. I felt mighty good to be on the water again, away from people. It seemed like the further we got from that town, the cleaner the air was to breathe. I reckoned I wouldn’t forget the sight of Boggs falling, or the sound of Sherburn’s voice, for a long time. But out here, on the big river, with the sky up above and the water down below, it seemed like you could wash all the dirt and foolishness of the world right off of you. And that’s what we did. We just let the river wash it all away.
