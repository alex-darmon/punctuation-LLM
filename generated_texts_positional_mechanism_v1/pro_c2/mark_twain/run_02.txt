It was one of them days in Flat Rock Landing when the dust don’t even care to move. The whole town was mostly just one long dirt street that run from the ferry landing up to the church, and most days you could shoot a cannon down it and not hit nothing but a stray dog or maybe old man Hemlock asleep on his porch. But this day was different, because the Widow Abernathy’s youngest boy, Jimmy, had got himself stuck down the old well again. He done it twice before, so it warn’t nothing new, but it was something to do. The whole town come out to watch.

Sheriff Miller, who was a considerable large man and sweated something awful, was in charge of the operation. He was pacing back and forth and looking mighty important, yelling orders that nobody paid much mind to. He says: “Now listen here, we need a long rope, the longest rope we got. And somebody fetch a lantern, it’s dark as a pocket down there.”

A feller from the saloon, name of Jed, he was leaning against a post and looking like he’d been drinking his breakfast. He pipes up and says: “Sheriff, that boy’s got a voice on him like a stepped-on cat. We don’t need a lantern to find him, we just need to follow the squallin’.”

A few folks laughed at that, and the sheriff he turned red and pointed a fat finger at Jed. He says: “You hush your mouth, Jedediah. This is a serious situation. A boy’s life is at stake.” Then he turned back to the well and hollered down: “You hang on, Jimmy! We’re a-comin’ for you!” And from way down in the dark we could hear a little voice crying about being hungry and wanting his ma.

By-and-by they got a rope and lowered a bucket down, thinking maybe they could get the boy to climb in, but he was too scared and just kept on wailing. His mother was there, the Widow Abernathy, and she was wringing her hands and crying, and two or three other women was fanning her and telling her it was all going to be fine, though they looked just as excited as everybody else. I seen a man taking bets on how long till they got him out. I reckoned it was good business, as there warn’t much else to bet on in Flat Rock Landing.

Then come Mr. Silas Blackwood, the blacksmith. He didn’t say nothing to nobody. He was a quiet man, with arms thick as hickory limbs and a face that looked like it was hammered out of iron itself. He just walked right through the crowd, carrying a mess of chains and a big hook he’d forged. Everybody got quiet when he come up. The sheriff puffed out his chest and says: “Now, Silas, we got this under control. Official town business.”

Mr. Blackwood, he just looked at the sheriff, then at the well, then back at the sheriff. He didn’t even blink. He says, real low and gravelly: “You’re wasting daylight.” Then he handed the end of the chain to three men and says: “Hold fast.” He spit on his hands and grabbed the chain and started going down into the well, hand over hand, his boots finding little holds in the rock wall.

The crowd didn’t make a sound, they just stretched their necks to see. You could hear the chain scraping on the stones and Mr. Blackwood breathing, and deep down you could hear the boy had stopped crying and was just sniffling. It felt like a powerful long time. The sun was beating down and I could feel my shirt sticking to my back. Pretty soon, we hear Mr. Blackwood’s voice, but it ain’t loud. It’s calm, like he was talking about the weather. He says: “All right, pull slow.”

The three men on the chain leaned back and started heaving, and the chain groaned and up it come, bit by bit. And then we seen Mr. Blackwood’s head, and then his shoulders, and he had Jimmy slung over one of them like a sack of meal. The boy had his arms wrapped tight around Mr. Blackwood’s neck and his face buried in his shoulder.

When they got to the top, Mr. Blackwood just set the boy down on the ground, and his ma run up and snatched him and started crying and kissing him all over. The crowd all started cheering and clapping for Mr. Blackwood. He just stood there, covered in dirt and well-slime, looking uncomfortable with all the noise. The sheriff come up and clapped him on the back and says: “A fine piece of work, Silas! A credit to the community!”

Mr. Blackwood, he just looked at the sheriff’s hand on his shoulder till the sheriff took it away. He didn’t say a word. He just picked up his chains and his hook, coiled them over his shoulder, and walked back towards his shop, the crowd parting for him like he was a king. Jed, the feller from the saloon, he raised his bottle and yelled out: “Three cheers for the blacksmith!” And everybody cheered again. I slid out of the crowd then. I figured the show was over, and I was right. In ten minutes, the street was empty again, and there was nothing to look at but the dust, which had finally decided to get moving.

chanting and yelling, a big river of people flooding out of the town square and turning down the street that led to Colonel Sherburn’s house. It warn’t a quiet river, neither; it was a roaring one, with men shaking their fists and waving sticks they’d picked up, and a few had old horse-pistols or squirrel guns. The long, lanky man that had acted out the shooting, he was somewhere in the front, his big white hat bobbing up and down like a cork in a flood. He was still carrying his cane, but now he was holding it like a general’s sword, pointing the way.

I got shoved along with them. I didn’t mean to, not exactly, but you couldn’t stand still if you wanted to. The crowd had a mind of its own, and it was a mean, stupid mind, and it pulled you in. I was curious, too, I reckon. I seen a hanging once, and I seen fights and all sorts of meanness, but I never seen a whole town try to lynch a man in broad daylight. It was something to see.

We swarmed down the street and fetched up in front of Sherburn’s house. It was a fine house, the best in town, like I said about his clothes. It was white, with two stories and green shutters, and a neat white picket fence running all around a yard full of flowers. It looked too clean and peaceful for what we was there for. The mob sort of spilled up against the fence and stopped, like a wave hitting a rock. They was all yelling and cursing, but nobody made a move to go through the gate. They just milled around, shaking their fists at the house and shouting for Sherburn to come out and take his medicine.

The lanky man, he clumb up on the fence post to make a speech. He wobbled a bit, and his hat nearly fell off, but he got his balance and held up his cane for quiet.

“Feller citizens!” he yells, his voice cracking a little. “We are here to see justice done! This here Sherburn, this high-and-mighty murderer, has shed the blood of a fellow Arkansawyer! He has made a widow of Boggs’s wife and orphans of his children!”

Somebody in the crowd yelled, “Boggs warn’t married!” and another one said, “And his daughter’s a grown girl!” which was true, but it didn’t seem to matter to the lanky man.

“He has brought shame upon our fair town!” he went on, getting warmed up. “Are we to stand by and let a killer walk our streets? Are we to be cowed by his fine clothes and his big house? I say no! I say we drag him out and serve him the same justice he served poor Boggs, only quicker!”

A big cheer went up at that, and the crowd surged forward again, and the picket fence groaned something awful. Two or three of the pickets snapped, and it looked like they was going to pour right into the yard. I scrambled up onto a woodpile next door so I could see over their heads and not get trampled.

Then the front door of the house opened. But Sherburn didn’t come out. A negro man did, an old feller with white hair. He looked scared to death, his eyes as big as dollars. He tried to say something, but the noise of the crowd swallowed it up. Then a window on the second floor slid open, and that’s when we seen him.

Colonel Sherburn stepped out onto the little roof over the front porch. He was holding a double-barreled shotgun, not aiming it, but just holding it easy in the crook of his arm. He hadn’t changed his clothes. He was still the best-dressed man in town, and he looked as ca’m as a summer’s morning. The whole crowd went dead quiet, so quiet you could hear a dog barking three streets over. All you could hear was a thousand men breathing.

Sherburn looked down at them. He didn’t look mad and he didn’t look scared. He looked, well, he looked like a man looks at a pile of garbage he’s about to have hauled away. He let his eyes travel over the whole crowd, slow and deliberate, from one end to the other. He didn’t miss a soul. I felt his eyes pass over me up on my woodpile, and I scrunched down a little, though he couldn’t have knowed me from Adam.

When he had looked his fill, he spoke. His voice warn’t loud, but it was clear and cold, and it cut through the silence like a razor.

“You can just listen to me,” he says. “And you’ll do it, too, because you’re cowards. Every last one of you.”

Nobody moved. Nobody said a thing. They just stood there with their mouths open, looking up at him.

“You’re not men,” he says, his voice getting a little stronger. “You’re the pitifulest imitation of men. I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average of man in all sections. The average man’s a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a day when he’ll have the courage to revolt. In the South one man, brave enough to face another man, will back down five thousand of his own kind, all full of vindictive talk and foolishness, and all they can do is just take it and swallow it.”

He shifted the gun a little in his arm. The lanky man on the fence post looked like he was froze there.

“You brought a rope to lynch me with,” Sherburn went on, and his lip curled just a little. “That’s right, ain’t it? And you want to burn my house, maybe. And you’re all brave enough to do it, ain’t you? As long as you’re in a mob, and it’s dark, and you’ve got your faces covered. But you ain’t got the grit to do it in the daytime. A man that’s a man faces his enemy in the light, one to one. I did it. I told Boggs I’d kill him, and I did. But you, you’d call yourselves a lynch-mob. A lynch-mob! Why, a real lynch-mob has got to have men in it. Real men.”

He took a step closer to the edge of the roof, and I seen some of the fellers in the front flinch back.

“Do you know what a man is?” he says, his voice like a whip now. “I’ll tell you. A man is Buck Harkness, that long-geared feller there on the fence post that you all listen to. He’s your leader, ain’t he? Oh, he’ll brag and he’ll bluster and he’ll talk about what he’s a-gwyne to do. But you put him out in front, by himself, and what does he do? He wilts. He whines. Look at him now.”

Everybody turned to look at the lanky man. He looked like he wanted the ground to open up and swallow him. His face was white and he was starting to tremble. He tried to say something, but only a squeak come out.

“You see?” Sherburn says. “You’re all Buck Harknesses. You didn’t want to come here. The idea of hanging a man in the daytime, face to face, it made you sick. You thought you’d get here and I’d be gone. You thought you’d yell a little and break a few windows and then go home and tell your wives what heroes you was. But you’re not heroes. You’re just a pack of curs that got called on your bluff.”

He raised the shotgun then, not to point it, but just to hold it up so they could all see it.

“Now get out of here,” he says, his voice flat and dead. “Go on. Take your half-a-man, your Buck Harkness, and crawl back into your holes. The show is over. Clear out.”

He turned his back on them. He just turned around, cool as you please, and stepped back through the window, pulling it shut behind him. He didn’t even look back to see if they was going.

For about half a minute, nobody moved. The whole crowd just stood there, stunned. It was like they’d all been slapped in the face at once. Then the lanky man, Buck Harkness, he slid down off the fence post real quiet and tried to melt into the crowd. But there was nowhere to melt. Everybody was looking at him. Somebody give a little laugh, a mean, nervous kind of laugh. Then another.

Pretty soon the whole crowd started to break up. It didn’t happen all at once. It just sort of dissolved, like sugar in water. Fellers started shuffling their feet and looking at the ground. They’d look at each other, and then look away quick, like they was ashamed. And they was. I felt mighty ashamed of them, and I hadn’t done nothing but watch. They didn’t talk loud no more. They just muttered to each other.

“Well, reckon he’s right,” one feller says, not to anybody in particular.

“Weren’t no sense gettin’ shot over it,” another one says, throwing his stick into the ditch.

“I got chores to do, anyway,” says a third, and he turned and walked away fast, without looking back.

In five minutes there was nobody left in front of Sherburn’s house but me, sitting on my woodpile, and a few kids kicking at the broken pickets on the fence. The street was empty. The big, brave lynch-mob was gone. There was nothing left but a busted fence and the smell of cowardice, which is a sour, dusty kind of smell.

I slid down off the woodpile and started wandering back towards the river. I’d had enough of town for one day. It seemed like the more people you got together in one place, the meaner and stupider they got. First they was all laughing at old Boggs, then they was all crying for him, then they was all wanting to hang the man that shot him, and then they all tucked their tails and run when the man talked mean to them. It was tiresome. A body couldn’t rely on nothing.

I was moseying along, thinking about all this, when I seen a stir down by the landing. It was the duke and the king. They was standing by a pile of their truck, and the king was arguing with a feller that run the wood-yard. I reckoned they hadn’t heard about all the excitement. I went up to them, and the duke, he seen me first.

“Ah, my boy!” he says, striking a pose with one hand on his hip. “Where have you been? We have been in dire need of your services. His majesty here is engaged in a most vexing fiscal negotiation with this plebeian merchant of timber.”

The king, he was red in the face and sweating. “It’s robbery, is what it is!” he says, loud enough for the wood-yard feller to hear. “To charge such a price for a bit of cordwood! An outrage against a man of the cloth, a poor missionary on his way to reform the pirates on the Spanish Main!”

The wood-yard feller just spit a wad of tobacco juice and says, “Price is the price, reverend. Pay it or get your boat away from my dock.”

I figured I’d tell them what they missed. “You should’ve been up in town,” I says. “There was a shooting. And then there was a lynch-mob.”

The duke’s eyes lit up. “A shooting! A tragedy of the most Shakespearian proportions, no doubt! Tell us, young man, what transpired? Was there blood? Was there high drama?”

So I told them the whole story, from Boggs riding into town drunk, to Sherburn shooting him down, to the crowd at the window of the drug store, and the long feller acting it all out, and then the mob and Sherburn on the porch roof. I told it all, just like I seen it.

When I was done, they was both quiet for a minute. The king was looking at me with his little piggy eyes, and the duke was stroking his chin, looking thoughtful.

“And this Sherburn,” the king says, finally. “He just... talked them down? With no help?”

“That’s what he done,” I says. “He called them all cowards and told them to go home, and they went.”

The king shook his head. “Well, I’ll be,” he says. “A whole town full of people, and not one of them had the sand to stand up to him. What a country.” He didn’t sound admiring, just kind of disappointed, like a good opportunity had been wasted.

But the duke, he had a different look on his face. He looked inspired. “By the boards, that’s a performance!” he says. “Magnificent! The lonely aristocrat, defying the maddened rabble! The power of a single, commanding voice against the unthinking herd! There is a play in that, my boy! A grand, soul-stirring spectacle!”

He started pacing back and forth, waving his arms. “We could call it ‘The Arkansas Colossus,’ or ‘The Taming of the Mob’! We could work it into our program. A recitation, perhaps, delivered by myself, portraying the noble Colonel Sherburn. We’d need a mob, of course. Your majesty, you could be the mob.”

“I ain’t playing no mob,” the king says, grumpily. “There’s no dignity in it. And besides, I don’t like the feel of this town no more. First the shooting, then this lynch business... it’s got an ugly temper. A town that can’t even pull off a proper lynching ain’t a town with no respect for tradition. It’s uncivilized. I say we get our wood and get gone before someone decides to look too close at our missionary work.”

The duke, he sighed a big, dramatic sigh. “Alas, for art to be so spurned for the sake of base commerce,” he says. “But perhaps your majesty is correct. The climate here has grown... inclement for the delicate flower of the theatre. The soil is too thin for genius to take root.”

So they paid the wood-yard feller his price, grumbling about it the whole time, and we loaded the wood onto the raft. It felt good to be back on the water, to feel the gentle pull of the current taking us away from that town. I laid on my back on the planks, watching the sky turn from blue to a kind of bruised purple, and I thought about Colonel Sherburn. I didn’t like him; he was a murderer, and a cold one at that. But I had to admit, there was something to him. He was a man, all right. A mean one, but a man. And the mob, they was just a thing. A big, scared, stupid thing. And I was glad to be floating away from all of them. The river didn’t judge you, and it didn’t talk back. It just carried you along. And right then, that was all I wanted.

and so away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering down the street towards Sherburn’s house, looking for all the world like they meant business this time. I didn’t have no more interest in seeing a lynching than I did in seeing the first part of the show, so I slid around a corner and made for the river bank. It warn’t long before I seen the whole pack of them coming back, looking powerful sheepish. They was quiet, and they warn’t looking at each other. They just sort of melted away, one to this street and one to that, till the whole big lynch mob was nothing but a dozen or so fellers standing around kicking at the dirt.

A man like that Sherburn, he was something to see. He warn’t a good man, I reckoned, not by a long shot. A man that’ll shoot another man down in the street, drunk or not, ain’t got much good in him. But the way he stood up to that whole town, with just his voice, and made them crawl, well, it was a thing to remember. It made you feel crawly yourself, just to think about it.

I found the king and the duke down by the wood-yard, arguing about the price of hickory. The king wanted to try and jew the feller down another ten cents a cord, but the duke said it warn’t dignified for royalty. I told them about Sherburn and the mob. It didn’t take long to tell.

They was both quiet for a minute. The king was looking at me with his little piggy eyes, and the duke was stroking his chin, looking thoughtful.

We floated on for two or three nights, mostly keeping to the middle of the river where we wouldn’t snag on nothing and nobody could see us too good. Jim and me, we’d take turns at the steering oar, and the king and the duke, they’d sleep most of the day and argue most of the night. The moon was getting fuller, and it laid a silver track on the water that was mighty pretty to look at. It was peaceful. Too peaceful for the king and duke.

On the third night, the duke, he couldn’t stand it no more. He’d been quiet, which warn’t his natural way. He’d been sitting by himself, staring at the black trees on the bank going by, and scribbling in a little book he had. All of a sudden he jumps up and strikes a pose, one hand tucked in his coat and the other one pointing at the sky.

“The average man’s a coward,” he says, his voice booming out over the water. It made me and Jim both jump. The king, who was trying to get to sleep on a pile of blankets, he sets up and says:

“What in the nation is it now, Bilgewater? Can’t a body get a moment’s repose from your folderol?”

The duke didn’t pay him no mind. He just kept on. “The pitifulest thing out is a mob; that’s what an army is, a mob; they don’t fight with courage that’s born in them, but with courage that’s borrowed from their mass, and from their officers.” He swelled his chest up and looked around at us like we was the town of Bricksville. “You didn’t want to go into the house of a brave man; but you had to be brave enough to go to church next Sunday, and look him in the face.”

He was doing Sherburn’s speech. He had it near about word for word, from what I told him. He had the sound of it, anyway, if not the coldness. The duke put too much music in it; Sherburn had just put ice.

“Splendid, ain’t it?” the duke says, turning to the king. “The raw material of high drama! I’ve been polishing it, your majesty. Refining the vernacular into pure, theatrical gold.”

“It’s noise,” the king says, laying back down. “And I’ve had my fill of it. I’ve had my fill of Arkansas, too. Every town is either dead as a doornail or boiling over with trouble. There ain’t no happy medium for a man of business.”

“Ah, but that is where you are mistaken, your majesty,” the duke says, getting that crafty look he got before a new swindle. “The trouble is the business! We shall not merely put on a play; we shall provide a public service. A moral lesson! We will show these benighted river folk the folly of mob rule.”

The king snorted. “They don’t pay for moral lessons.”

“They will for ours!” says the duke. “We shall bill it as ‘The Tragedy of Boggs the Drunkard, or, The Taming of the Mob.’ And I, in the monumental role of Colonel Sherburn, shall deliver the famous oration. Think of it! The tension! The pathos! The audience will be on the edge of their seats.”

“They’ll be on their way out the door to get their money back is what they’ll be,” the king says. “Who wants to see a play about a feller getting shot and then a lecture? It ain’t got no romance, it ain’t got no sword fights.”

“We can add a sword fight,” the duke says, not missing a beat. “Perhaps Sherburn is a veteran of the Mexican War. A flashback! No, wait. The power is in its simplicity. Man against the herd. We just need to present it right.” He started pacing again, which was a bad sign. It meant he was working himself up to something that would get us all in trouble.

“We get to the next town,” he says, “a town of sufficient size and ignorance. We put up bills. Not our usual, paltry bills, but grand pronouncements! ‘A Moral Spectacle! Witness the Dangers of Intemperance and Mob Hysteria! A Sobering Drama for Sober Times, Presented by the World Renowned Thespians, Dr. Armand de Montalban and Mr. Edmund Kean the Elder!’”

“I like that part,” the king says, sitting up a little. “That’s got a good sound to it.”

“Of course it does,” the duke says. “And for the climax, the great speech… we shall require a mob.”

“And I told you,” the king says, his voice going flat, “I ain’t playing no mob.”

“You misunderstand, your majesty. You are far too regal for such a role. You shall be… the conscience of the town! A respected elder who tries to calm the unfortunate Boggs. A small but pivotal part, full of dignity.” The duke was laying it on thick now, like molasses in January. “As for the mob… we shall use the audience itself.”

Me and Jim looked at each other. That sounded like the worst idea since trying to teach a cat to swim.

But the king, he was starting to warm to it. He liked the sound of being a ‘respected elder.’ He says, “The audience? How’s that to work?”

“Simple!” the duke says. “I shall direct my oration not to the empty stage, but to the very people in the seats! I shall look them in the eye, I shall call them cowards, I shall dare them to lynch me! It will be theatrical innovation of the highest order. They will be so stunned, so overwhelmed by the sheer power of the performance, that they will be putty in our hands. They will feel that they have truly lived through the event. And they will pay handsomely for the privilege.”

The king stroked his chin whiskers, which was his way of thinking. “So they give us their money to come in, and then you call them a pack of cowards, and they thank you for it?”

“Precisely!” the duke yells, slapping his thigh. “It is the very essence of art! It confronts, it provokes, it elevates!”

“It sounds to me like it’s the very essence of getting tarred and feathered,” the king says. But I could see the greed in his eyes was starting to win out over the common sense. “How much you reckon we could charge for this… moral spectacle?”

“Not less than fifty cents a head,” the duke says. “A dollar for the front rows, where they can feel the full force of my contempt.”

That settled it for the king. The idea of getting a dollar a head for letting the duke insult people was too good for him to pass up. So it was decided. The next town we came to, we was going to put on “The Taming of the Mob.” I had a powerful bad feeling about it. It seemed to me that trying to tame a mob was like trying to pet a rattlesnake; it might work once, but it was a fool’s game to try it for a living.

We floated another day, and the duke spent the whole time rehearsing. He’d stand on one end of the raft and holler Sherburn’s speech at the other end, while the king practiced looking dignified and me and Jim tried to stay out of the way. The duke made me tell him the story of the shooting over and over, to get all the details right.

“And what did Boggs’s daughter wear?” he’d ask. “Was it a calico dress? What shade of blue? These details are vital for emotional authenticity!”

I told him I didn’t rightly remember. He just sighed and said he’d have to invent it, and that the artist’s burden was a heavy one.

Late the next afternoon, we come in sight of a town. It was set on a high bluff, and it looked more prosperous than Bricksville. It had a two-story brick courthouse with a clock tower, and a steamboat landing with bales of cotton stacked up high. There was three or four church steeples sticking up.

“Pikesville,” the duke says, reading a sign on the shore. “An auspicious name. Pike, as in the fish, a creature known for its voracious and unthinking appetite. The perfect audience.”

So we tied up the raft in a little cove downriver and got ready to go into town. The duke put on his best clothes, which was a long black coat that was getting shiny at the elbows, and a tall hat that had been crushed more than once. The king wrapped a blue sash around his belly and called himself the Honorable Judge LaGrange, so he could practice being a respected elder. They told me and Jim to stay with the raft and keep out of sight. That suited me just fine.

They was gone for a good long while. Just before sundown, they come back, the duke walking on air and the king looking pleased with himself.

“It is done!” the duke says, waving a handful of papers. “The courthouse is rented for tomorrow night! The printer is running off the bills as we speak! The town, my friends, is buzzing with anticipation.”

“They got a temperance society here,” the king says, with a wink. “A big one. They was mighty interested in a play about the evils of drink. The head of it, a woman with a face like a hatchet, she bought twenty tickets for her flock on the spot.”

The duke beamed. “You see? We are not merely actors, we are public benefactors! We are bringing culture and morality to this benighted wilderness.”

All day, me and Jim heard talk from men that came down to the river to fish or loaf. They was all talking about the show. That night, they made me come with them to the courthouse to help take the tickets. The king stood by the door, looking solemn and important, and the duke was backstage, “preparing his instrument,” as he called it. The place was packed. It looked like every man in Pikesville was there, from the farmers in their dusty boots to the town swells in their white shirts. The air was thick with tobacco smoke and the smell of whisky. I took my seat in the back, where I could watch. The stage was bare except for a table and a chair. A feller in the front row, a big man with a red beard, he leans forward and says to his friend, “What’d he say?”

The crowd started murmuring. This warn’t what they paid their fifty cents for. They was expecting a play, not to get picked out and insulted.

By the time we got to the main street, he was doing a pretty fair imitation of old Boggs, though maybe not so loud. He was stumbling along and muttering to himself, and his old slouch hat was cocked over one eye.

The duke, he’d put on his best clothes, what was left of them, and was trying to look like a proud, rich man. He went into the biggest mercantile store on the street, just like Sherburn done, to wait.

The king started his performance. He staggered into the middle of the street and started yelling.

“Woe is me!” he shouts, waving his arms. “Woe to this town of sinners! I have come to call out the great transgressor, the scoundrel known as… as…” He forgot the name the duke had told him to use. He squinted at the store sign. “…the scoundrel known as Miller’s Mercantile!”

A few people stopped to look at him. He warn’t drawing a crowd like Boggs did. Boggs was an event; the king just looked like any other sorry drunk.

“Come out, Miller!” the king yells. “Come out and face the man you have wronged! You sold me a shod horse, and it went lame in a mile! I’m here for satisfaction, and my motto is, ‘Cash first, and apologies to top off on!’”

It was a poor sort of motto, and nobody seemed much impressed. A couple of farmers sitting on a bench just laughed. One of them says to the other, “Looks like old man Hemphill’s on a tear again.”

So they already had a town drunk, and they mistook the king for him. This warn’t going to plan at all. I was standing over by a water trough, feeling mighty conspicuous.

The king, he seen he was losing his audience. He took another pull from his bottle and decided to raise the stakes. He rode up an imaginary horse, slapping his own backside and yelling, “Cler the track! I’m on the waw-path, and the price of shod horses is a-gwyne to raise!”

This was so ridiculous that a few more people stopped to watch. He was getting a little crowd now, mostly boys and loafers. He was getting bolder. He started calling the store owner all kinds of names, and the crowd was snickering.

Right on cue, the duke steps out of the store. He looked tall and proud, and he had his face set just like he’d practiced. He was a heap the best-dressed man in that town, too, even if his coat was a little frayed at the cuffs. The little crowd of about twenty people went quiet.

The duke says, in a slow, cold voice: “I am not Mr. Miller. But I am a gentleman of substance, and I find your antics tiresome. I will endure this until the clock strikes three. Till three o’clock, mind, no longer. If you open your mouth against this establishment or its proprietor only once after that time, you can’t travel so far but I will find you.”

It was pretty good. He had the tone just right. He sounded dangerous. The little crowd looked sobered up, and they looked at the church clock, which said it was a quarter to three. Then the duke turned and went back in the store.

The king, he was supposed to keep up his cussing, but he seemed a little flustered. He hadn’t counted on the duke sounding so convincing. But he was a performer, and he knew his part. He started blackguarding the duke, calling him a high-toned swindler and a counterfeit gentleman.

“I ain’t afraid of you!” he yells, but his voice shook a little. “Three o’clock or four o’clock, it’s all the same to me! I’ll be here to collect what’s owed!”

Some of the men in the crowd started to edge closer. “You better clear out, Hemphill,” one of them says. “That feller ain’t from around here. He might just mean what he says.”

“He’s talking to the wrong man,” another one says, and I seen he was a big, rough-looking fellow with a mean face. “Old man Hemphill’s a harmless fool. He wouldn’t hurt a fly.”

This was bad. The whole idea was that the crowd was supposed to think Boggs was a nuisance who had it coming. But they thought the king was their own harmless town drunk, and the duke was some dangerous outsider. They was starting to get on the king’s side.

The king kept it up, though, raging up and down the street. It was getting close to three, and I could feel things getting tight. I was ready to start my lynching talk, but I had a bad feeling it would go the wrong way. They’d be more likely to lynch the duke for shooting their town fool than for him getting what he deserved.

Then the church clock started to strike. One… two… three.

The whole street went dead quiet. Everybody looked at the king. The king, he looked at the store. He opened his mouth, like he was gwyne to say something.

And just then, another man comes stumbling out of a saloon down the street. He was a big, red-faced man with wild hair, and he was even drunker than the king was pretending to be. He was yelling and waving a bottle.

“That’s him!” somebody in the crowd whispers. “That’s the real Hemphill!”

So the king had been mistaken for the town drunk, and now the real one showed up. It was getting complicated.

The real Hemphill comes weaving across the street. He sees the king, who was standing there frozen with his mouth open.

“Who’re you?” Hemphill yells. “You’re in my spot! This is my street for hollering! I’m the one that does the cussing of Miller in this town!”

He squared off in front of the king. The crowd loved it. They was laughing and slapping their knees. Two town drunks about to have a showdown.

The king, he didn’t know what to do. This warn’t in the script. He tried to wave Hemphill away. “Go on, now, good man,” he says, trying to sound dignified. “I am engaged in a matter of honor.”

“Honor?” Hemphill bellows. “I’ll give you honor!” And he swings his bottle at the king’s head.

The king yelped and jumped back, and the bottle just missed him. The crowd roared.

The door to the store flies open and out comes the duke. His face was white as a sheet. His whole tragedy was turning into a low comedy, and he was losing control of it.

“Unhand that man!” the duke shouts, pointing at Hemphill.

Hemphill turns to look at the duke. “And who’re you, Mr. Fancy-Pants?”

“I am the agent of justice!” the duke declaims, striking a pose.

Hemphill just laughed, a nasty, gurgling sound. “The only agent I see is an undertaker, and he’s a-gwyne to be busy with you!” He started towards the duke, who took a step back.

It was all going to pieces. The king was huddled behind a horse trough. The crowd was cheering for Hemphill. The duke was about to get beat up by a drunk. He looked around, desperate, and his eyes met mine. I just shook my head. I didn’t know what to do.

Then the duke remembered his part. He remembered the pistol. He reached into his coat and pulled out the big old rusty horse-pistol.

“Halt!” he cried. “Or you are a dead man!”

Hemphill stopped. He stared at the pistol. The whole crowd went silent again. This warn’t funny no more. This was real.

Hemphill squinted. “You wouldn’t dare,” he says, but he didn’t sound so sure.

“I am a man of my word,” the duke says, his voice trembling a little, but he held the pistol steady. It was pointed right at Hemphill’s chest.

This was the moment. But it was all wrong. He was supposed to shoot the king, who had the pig’s blood ready. He wasn’t supposed to shoot a real man. Even with a blank, it was a dangerous business.

The king, seeing his part of the show slipping away, decided to get back in it. He poked his head up from behind the trough.

“That’s right!” he pipes up. “He’s a-gwyne to shoot! I’m his first victim! He’s a killer!”

Hemphill, the real drunk, got confused. He looked from the duke to the king and back again. “What in tarnation is this?” he mumbled.

The duke saw his chance to get back to the script. He swung the pistol away from Hemphill and aimed it at the king.

“You!” he roars. “You were warned! The hour has struck!”

Bang!

The old pistol went off with a tremendous noise, and a huge cloud of gray smoke. It was a lot louder than I figured it would be.

The king let out a perfect screech. He smashed the flask of blood against his chest and staggered backwards, clawing at the air. “I am slain!” he hollered. “A martyr to shod horses!” Then he tumbled over backwards behind the water trough, out of sight. It was a pretty good performance, all things considered.

The crowd gasped. For a second, I thought it worked. They looked shocked. Hemphill, the real drunk, his eyes were as wide as saucers. The loud bang and the sight of the king going down seemed to sober him up some. He dropped his bottle and started to back away.

Then the duke made his mistake. Instead of looking proud and cold like Sherburn, he looked relieved. He lowered the pistol and a little smile played on his lips. He’d done it. He thought he’d pulled it off.

But a man in the crowd, the same rough-looking fellow from before, pointed. “Looky there,” he says. “That ain’t blood. That’s juice!”

I looked. The king had done his part too well. The flask had broke and the pig’s blood, or what was left of it, had splashed all over his shirt, but it was thin and dark, and it didn’t look right. And in his excitement, he’d staggered back and knocked over a basket of blackberries a farm woman had set by the trough. The dark purple juice was all over him, mixed in with the pig’s blood, and it was plain to see it was a fake. You could smell the berries.

“He ain’t hurt!” somebody else yells. “It’s a trick!”

Just then, the king sits up from behind the trough, wiping blackberry juice out of his eye. He seen the game was up.

The duke’s face fell. All the pride drained out of him, and he just looked like a scared old fraud with a rusty pistol. The crowd started to move. They wasn’t a lynch mob, not yet. They was just an angry mob, and they felt cheated. They started closing in on him.

“Swindlers!” the rough man yelled. “They come here to make fools of us! Let’s get ‘em!”

The duke didn’t wait to deliver no oration. He gave a squeak, turned, and ran. He ran right past the store and down an alley, his coattails flying. The king scrambled up and lit out after him, dripping purple juice and looking more ridiculous than ever.

The crowd took off after them, yelling and hollering. I didn’t waste no time. I slipped away in all the confusion and ran the other way, circling around the edge of town to get back to the willows where the raft was. I ran hard, because I was scared. It’s one thing to see a town get mad. It’s another thing to be the reason why.

I got to the raft and untied it, and shoved it out into the current, waiting. I was breathing hard and listening. Pretty soon, I heard them coming, crashing through the brush. It was the duke first, panting like a dog. He took a leap and landed on the raft, falling in a heap. A second later here comes the king, moaning and groaning, and he splashed aboard right after.

“Shove off, boy! Shove off!” the king wheezed.

“We’re already off,” I says.

We floated out into the middle of the river, and none too soon. We could see the mob come to the riverbank, shaking their fists. A couple of them had rocks, but we was already too far out. They yelled at us for a while, and then they give up.

We drifted down the river in the twilight. The king was trying to clean the blackberry and pig blood off his clothes, and complaining something awful.

“Ruined,” he says. “A perfectly good coat, ruined. And for what? Not a single solitary cent.”

The duke sat with his head in his hands. He looked terrible.

“It was the verisimilitude,” he moaned. “The verisimilitude was faulty. Blackberries. Who could have foreseen blackberries?”

“I could’ve,” I says. “They was right next to the trough.”

“The artist cannot be expected to account for stray fruit!” the duke snapped. “It was the town. A town with two drunks. It throws off the entire dynamic. It is an untenable dramatic situation.”

“And you shot at the wrong one,” the king says, dabbing at a purple stain. “You was supposed to shoot me. That was the plan. I was the star.”

“There was an interruption in the performance!” the duke said. “An unforeseen complication! I had to improvise!”

They went on squabbling like that for an hour, blaming each other, and the town, and the real drunk, and the blackberries. I didn’t say nothing. I just kept us in the current. I was glad, in a way. I was glad it went wrong. Nobody got hurt, not really, and we didn’t get no money. It seemed fair.

Play-acting being a king or a duke, that was one thing. Play-acting a murder was another. It felt like we’d got mighty close to the real thing back there, and I didn’t like the feel of it at all. The real Sherburn didn’t have to worry about blackberries or rival drunks. He just had a pistol and the will to use it. The duke only had the one, and when he tried to use it, the whole world turned into a circus.

It made me feel tired. I laid on my back and looked up at the stars starting to come out. They was cool and far away and didn’t have no part in any of it. The river just kept on moving, same as always, carrying a raft with a couple of fools and me away from the town of Napoleon’s Crown, and I was powerful glad to be going.

away they went, a-whooping and a-hollering for all the world like they was going to a picnic instead of a hanging. Most of the town went with them, all jammed together in a big solid lump of people, leaving the street where Boggs died all empty and quiet except for that long, lanky man. He was still there, a-practicing his staggering and falling, trying to get it just so. A couple of his friends was watching and telling him when he got it right, and when he done it perfect they’d clap him on the back and he’d take another drink from his bottle. I didn’t have no interest in seeing a lynching, which ain’t nothing but a lot of yelling and shoving and then a man choking at the end of a rope, so I slid away from the crowd and made for a little back alley.

I reckoned I’d just lay low till it all blowed over. It seemed to me that a town had more trouble in it than a body could shake a stick at. First it’s a drunk man getting shot for being a fool, and next it’s the whole town turning into fools themselves to go hang the man that shot him. It was all tiresome, and I wished I was back on the raft, with just the river and the sky. I found a pile of empty barrels behind the drug store and hunkered down between them, where nobody could see me. I could still hear the mob, but it was further off now, down by the far end of town where I reckoned Colonel Sherburn must live. The sound was like a big animal groaning and getting madder and madder.

I must have set there a good long while. The sun got lower, and the alley got darker, and the noise of the mob got louder and then it got quieter, and then it got all mixed up. Pretty soon I heard footsteps coming down the alley, and not the mob kind of footsteps, but just one person, walking steady and not in a hurry. I scrunched down smaller, but then I see it was just Colonel Sherburn himself.

He warn’t running, and he warn’t hiding. He was just walking along, as ca’m as if he was on his way to church. He had a shotgun in the crook of his arm now, not a pistol. He stopped not ten feet from my barrels and leaned against the wall of the drug store, and took out a plug of tobacco and bit off a chew. He just stood there chewing, and looking back the way the mob had gone.

Then here they come, not a-whooping anymore, but more of a rumble, and shuffling their feet. They come to the head of the alley and stopped. They seen him there, waiting. They filled up the whole mouth of the alley, a wall of faces, all pale and sweaty in the low light. The man in front, a big blacksmith by the looks of him, he says, “Sherburn, we’re here for you.” His voice was shaky. He was trying to sound brave, but he wasn’t.

Colonel Sherburn spit a brown stream of tobacco juice near his own feet. He didn’t raise the shotgun. He just looked at them, one to the other, slow and deliberate, like he was counting them.

“Well, now,” he says, and his voice warn’t loud, but it cut through the air like a razor. “So you’re the men that’s come to lynch me. I’m pleased to make your acquaintance.”

Nobody said nothing. The blacksmith shuffled his feet.

“I was born and raised in the South,” Sherburn says, “and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average man. The average man’s a coward. In the North he lets anybody walk over him that wants to, and goes home and prays for a humble spirit to bear it. In the South one man, all by himself, has got to go out in the middle of the street and watch a pathetic old drunk cuss him out and call him a swindler for ten minutes before he can shoot him. That’s the law of chivalry. But what does the _average_ man do? Why, he gets together with a thousand other cowards and they flock together in the dark and put on masks and go creeping up on a man that’s asleep. And they call themselves brave.”

He looked right at the blacksmith. “Why, I know you, Buck Harkness. You ain’t a man, you’re half a man. The idea of you lynching anybody! It’s laughable. The idea of you thinking you had pluck enough to lynch a man. But you don’t. Not a single one of you has got the guts to face a man in the daytime. You’re cowards, all of you.”

He took a step forward, and the whole front of the crowd flinched back.

“Your mistake,” he says, “is you brought your mob in the daytime. You didn’t bring your masks. You didn’t wait till midnight. You come to lynch a man, but you was afraid to come by yourselves. You had to bring the whole town. And now you’re here, and you’re scared. You’re scared of me, and you’re scared of what you come to do. So go on home. Go on, git. The show is over.”

He raised the shotgun then, not to aim it, but just holding it ready across his chest. The crowd looked at the gun, and they looked at his face, and his face was worse than the gun. They started to melt. First the ones in the back turned and slipped away, and then the ones in the middle, and pretty soon even Buck Harkness, he looked down at his own feet and turned and walked off. In about a minute, the alley was empty again. There warn’t nobody there but me behind the barrels, and Colonel Sherburn standing in the middle of the street, chewing his tobacco. He stood there for another minute, then he spit again, turned on his heel, and walked back towards his house, just as steady as before.

I let out the breath I’d been holding. It felt like I’d been holding it for an hour. It was a powerful strange thing to see. A whole town full of men, ready to kill somebody, and all it took to stop them was one man calling them cowards. It seemed to me that maybe Sherburn was right about the average man. I didn’t want to be around to see if he was right about me, too, so I slid out of the alley and made my way down to the river, keeping to the back ways.

I was glad to get back to the raft. The river was peaceful and wide, and the sun was setting, making everything all red and gold. It was a powerful relief after all the trouble and meanness in that town. I untied us and we slid out into the current, and I didn’t look back. I didn’t ever want to see that town again.

We drifted on for a couple of days after that, and it was easy living. The weather was fine and the river was good to us. But easy living didn’t suit the duke and the king for long. They had spent all the money they got from the last town on whisky and new hats, and now they was broke again, and getting ornery. The first day they just laid around and complained about their luck, and about how the world warn’t fair to men of genius. The second day they started to get that hungry look in their eyes, the one they got when they was cooking up a new scheme.

“Bilgewater,” the duke says, sitting up straight all of a sudden. “This lethargy will be the death of us. The artistic soul requires stimulus, and the mortal vessel requires sustenance. We are lacking in both.”

The king was over by the side of the raft, trying to catch a fish with a piece of string and a bent pin. He says, “What we’re lacking in is dollars, is what. I ain’t had a proper meal in two days, and my insides is commencing to complain most grievously.”

“Precisely,” the duke says. “And thus, a new enterprise is required. Something of a higher intellectual caliber than our previous endeavors. The Royal Nonesuch was profitable, but it catered to the baser instincts. It lacked… finesse.”

“It lacked a paying audience, is what it lacked,” the king grumbled, yanking his string out of the water. “That last town was a bust. All that work for nothing.”

“The fault was not in the art, but in the audience,” the duke says, waving his hand like he was brushing away a fly. “We must find a new venue, a new populace. One more attuned to the subtler sciences.” He got a gleam in his eye. “I have it,” he says. “Phrenology.”

I didn’t know what phrenology was, but it sounded like something you’d catch from bad water. The king, he looked just as puzzled as I felt. “Fren-what-now?”

“Phrenology!” the duke says, getting all excited. “The science of the mind! The study of the cranium! It is all the rage in the capitols of Europe. A trained practitioner can discern a man’s entire character, his virtues, his vices, his secret ambitions, merely by the palpation of his skull.”

The king’s eyes lit up a little. “You mean, feeling the bumps on his head?”

“A vulgar simplification, but essentially correct,” the duke says. “Each faculty of the mind, benevolence, acquisitiveness, combativeness, and so on, resides in a specific organ of the brain. The size of the organ is indicative of its power, and this is reflected in the topography of the skull. It is pure science, my dear Capet. And the public is ravenous for it.”

The king rubbed his chin. “And they’ll pay for this… head-feeling?”

“They will pay handsomely,” the duke says. “A quarter for a basic reading, a dollar for a full character analysis and written chart. It is a gold mine, and one that requires no props save for our own intellect and a bit of showmanship.”

So that was the new plan. For the next day, as we floated down the river, the duke set about preparing. He found an old piece of sailcloth and a chunk of charcoal, and he drawed a great big head on it. Then he divided the head up into about forty different sections with lines, and in each section he wrote a fancy word. I remember some of them. There was one over the ear called ‘Destructiveness,’ and one on the top called ‘Veneration,’ and a big one on the back of the neck he called ‘Amativeness.’ He was powerful proud of that chart. He’d stand back and look at it with his head cocked to one side, like a real artist.

“There,” he says. “Behold, the map of the human soul. Now, you, Dauphin, must commit these to memory.”

He tried to teach the king what all the words meant, but it warn’t no use. The king couldn’t get his tongue around half of them, and he couldn’t remember where they was supposed to be.

“Philoprogenitiveness,” the duke would say, pointing to a spot on the back of the drawing. “The love of one’s offspring. A prominent bump here indicates a doting parent.”

“Fiddlesticks,” the king says. “That’s too much to remember. We’ll just call it the ‘baby-loving bump.’ And this here one, ‘Acquisitiveness,’ that’s the ‘money-grubbing bump,’ ain’t it? And this ‘Combativeness’ is the ‘fighting bump.’ It’s simple.”

The duke throwed up his hands in despair. “You are a Vandal, sir! You would reduce the sublime science of Gall and Spurzheim to the crude jabberings of a carnival barker!”

“I’d reduce it to whatever gets the quarters in the hat,” the king says. “You handle the fancy talk, and I’ll handle the heads. I’ve got a natural feel for human nature, I have.”

They argued about it for a while, but that’s how they settled it. The duke would be the professor and give the lecture, and the king would be the ‘natural intuitive’ who did the readings.

The next day we come to a town that looked about right. It was called Pikesville, and it was a clean-looking place with a white church and a courthouse with a gold eagle on top. It looked prosperous, which the king said was the main thing. We tied up the raft in a little cove downriver and got ourselves ready. The duke put on his black suit that he used for tragedy, and the king put on a long coat and tried to look wise, which mostly just made him look like he had a stomach-ache. They rolled up the head-chart, and we walked into town.

They found a good spot right on the courthouse square, under a big elm tree. The duke unrolled his chart and hung it on the tree, and then he got up on a stump and started his speech.

“Ladies and gentlemen!” he yelled, and a few people that was loafing around turned to look. “Citizens of this fair metropolis! Do you know yourselves? Do you truly comprehend the mysterious workings of the brain-pan, the very seat of your immortal soul?”

It was a grand speech, full of words like ‘cerebral topography’ and ‘psychological revelation’ and ‘the indisputable march of science.’ He told them that for a limited time, he and his learned colleague, the world-renowned Doctor Armand de Montpensier of Paris, that was the king, would unlock the secrets of their skulls.

Pretty soon a little crowd had gathered. They was mostly just curious, but a few of them looked like they was taking it serious. The first customer was a lady with a bonnet all covered in fake flowers. She looked like she thought well of herself.

“My good woman,” the duke says, helping her up to the stump where the king was waiting. “Prepare for enlightenment.”

The king put on a solemn face and took off the lady’s bonnet. He run his fingers over her head, poking and prodding here and there, and humming to himself.

“Aha!” he says, stopping at a place above her temple. He looked over at the chart. “A most pronounced organ of… of…” He squinted at the chart again. “Ideality! Yes, Ideality! Ma’am, you have a soul that yearns for beauty and poetry. You are not meant for the humdrum and the mundane. Your spirit soars with the eagles.”

The lady just beamed. She fanned herself and said, “Oh, Doctor, it’s so true! I’ve always said my sensibilities were too fine for this rough country.” She give the king a quarter and went off looking pleased as punch.

After that, it was easy. The king told a farmer he had a powerful bump of ‘Alimentiveness,’ which meant he had a healthy love of food and drink, and the farmer laughed and said that was him to a T. He told a young man with slicked-down hair that his ‘Amativeness’ was so big it was a wonder it hadn’t busted right through his skull, and the young man blushed and give him fifty cents. He was just telling people what they already thought about themselves, or what they wanted to think. He’d find a bump, any bump, and then he’d look the person over and make up a story to fit. And the money kept clinking into the hat that I was holding.

Things was going along swimmingly. The crowd was getting bigger, and everybody was laughing and having a good time. The king was feeling his oats, getting more and more dramatic with his readings. Then a man pushed his way to the front of the crowd.

He was a tall, thin man, dressed all in black, with a long, sour face. He didn’t look like he was having a good time at all. He looked like he’d just bit into a bad lemon.

“This is a disgrace,” he says, in a dry, raspy voice. “An exhibition of charlatanism and folderol preying upon the ignorance of good people.”

The duke, he puffed out his chest. “Sir, you are addressing a man of science! This is not folderol, it is phrenology, the greatest discovery of the modern age!”

“It is bunkum,” the man says. “You are frauds and mountebanks. I am Silas Kane, the town undertaker, and I’ve handled more heads than you’ve had hot dinners. There is nothing in their shape to tell you if a man was a saint or a sinner. A skull is a skull.”

The crowd got quiet. They looked from the undertaker to the duke. This was getting interesting.

The king, he decided to take matters into his own hands. He stepped down from the stump and walked right up to Mr. Kane. “My good man,” he says, with a greasy smile. “Your skepticism is understandable. It is the refuge of the uninformed. Allow me to demonstrate the power of our science upon your own cranium, free of charge. A private consultation, to allay your doubts.”

Mr. Kane looked at the king with his cold, dead-looking eyes. “Very well,” he says. “But not here. In my place of business. I have a subject there, a perfect test case. A man whose character was known to all in this town, but who can no longer speak to it himself. If you can read his character from his head, I will concede your point. I will, in fact, give you twenty dollars in gold.”

The king’s eyes bugged out at the mention of twenty dollars. “Done!” he says, without even thinking.

The duke looked nervous. “Ahem, my dear Doctor,” he says, “perhaps a public demonstration would be more…”

“Nonsense,” the king says. “A scientific challenge has been issued! We accept!”

So Mr. Kane turned and started walking, and the duke and the king had no choice but to follow him. And the whole crowd, not wanting to miss the end of the show, followed right behind them. I tagged along, too, because I had a bad feeling and I didn’t want to be left alone with the hat full of money if things went sour.

Mr. Kane’s undertaking parlor was on a side street. It was a dark, gloomy-looking building with the curtains drawn, and it had a strange, sweet, chemical smell about it. He led us all inside. The front room was full of coffins, some plain pine and some fancy with silver handles. But he didn’t stop there. He led us through a door into a back room.

The room was cold, and the smell was stronger here. In the middle of the room was a long wooden table, and on the table was a man laid out, covered with a white sheet.

The crowd pushed in behind us, all trying to see. Mr. Kane walked over to the table. “Here is your subject, gentlemen,” he says, and with a flick of his wrist, he pulled back the sheet.

The man on the table was old, with white hair and a peaceful-looking face. He was dressed in a clean nightshirt. He was also very, very dead.

The king let out a little squeak and took a step back, right onto my foot. The duke went pale as the sheet.

“This,” Mr. Kane says, his voice as cold as the room, “was Judge Alistair Finch. He passed on two days ago. He lived in this town for fifty years. Now, tell us, ‘Doctor.’ What do the bumps on his head say about his character?”

The king was stuck. The whole town was watching him. He couldn't run. He looked at the duke, but the duke was busy trying to look scientific and not look like he was about to be sick.

So the king, he took a deep breath, and he shuffled over to the table. He was sweating something awful. He reached out a trembling hand and laid it on the dead man’s cold forehead. He closed his eyes for a second, like he was concentrating.

And all through it, there was that girl, who hadn’t done nothing to nobody, and now she was all alone with just a sticky piece of candy in her hand.

I walked through the town. It was getting dark for real now, and lamps was lit in the houses. People was on their porches, talking low. The excitement was over. It was just another hot night in Arkansaw now. Down the street I heard some fellers laughing. I went over to see what was the matter now.

It was the lanky man with the stovepipe hat again. He had a little crowd around him, in front of the saloon. But he warn’t acting out the shooting no more. He was acting out Sherburn on the porch. He stood up on a barrel, frowning and looking down his nose.

“You call yourselves men?” he squawks, trying to sound like Sherburn. The little crowd busted out laughing. “You’re cowards! The whole lot of you! You ain’t got the sand!”

Another feller in the crowd, a fat man with a red face, yells up, “That’s him! That’s just what he said! You got him perfect, Buck!”

So his name was Buck. He took a bow, all grand, and took a swig from a bottle somebody passed up to him. Then he looked down and seen me watching.

“Well now, here’s a witness!” he says, pointing at me with the bottle. “Ain’t that right, boy? Didn’t old Sherburn read us the riot act? Didn’t he have us all buffaloed?”

He was grinning, like it was the best joke in the world. And the other men was grinning, too. It was the strangest thing. A little while ago they was ready to tear a man to pieces, and now they was laughing about how he’d scared them off. It was like they was proud of it, in a way. Proud that their town had a man in it so hard and fearsome that he could face down a whole mob by hisself. They’d turned their own shame into a good story to tell.

“I reckon,” I says.

Buck laughed and offered me the bottle. “Have a snort, boy. Celebrate the day Arkansaw justice was stared down by one man with a shotgun!”

I shook my head and backed away. I’d had enough of them and their whisky and their stories. I’d seen the shooting, and I’d seen the dead man, and I’d seen the girl. It warn’t a story to me. It was real.

I turned my back on them and headed for the river. The sounds of their laughing followed me for a little ways, then faded out. Pretty soon all I could hear was the crickets starting up, and the deep, quiet hum of the river waiting for me. I couldn’t get to the raft fast enough. I wanted to be away from that town. I wanted to be out on the water, where things was clean and simple, and there warn’t no people to get drunk and kill each other and then laugh about it afterwards. The stars was coming out, thick and white. They looked cold and a long way off, and they didn’t seem to care one bit about the troubles down in Arkansaw. And I reckoned I couldn’t blame them.

When I got to the little patch of willows where we’d hid the raft, it was all quiet. The river was just a-gliding by, smooth and dark, with the stars swimming in it. I slipped through the bushes and there was Jim, sitting on the edge of the raft, watching the water. He didn’t say nothing when I clumb aboard. He just looked at me, and I could see his eyes shining in the dark.

“We got to go, Jim,” I says, soft-like. “We got to get out of here right now.”

He just nodded. “I hear de shoutin’, Huck. I hear de guns. I was pow’ful worried. Is you all right?”

“I’m all right,” I says. “But a man ain’t. A man’s dead, Jim.”

And so I told him all about it. I told him about Boggs coming to town drunk and cussing, and about Colonel Sherburn telling him to shut up by one o’clock, and how Boggs didn’t, and how Sherburn come out and shot him down right in the street. I told him about the daughter crying over him, and the big Bible they put on his chest, and how he died right there on the drug store floor. And I told him about the mob going to lynch Sherburn, and how Sherburn stood on his porch roof with a shotgun and talked them down and called them cowards till they all slunk away.

Jim listened to the whole thing, his chin in his hands. He didn’t say a word till I was all done.

“Dat man Sherburn,” he says, his voice low and rumbly like far-off thunder. “He got de devil in him, Huck. To shoot a man down like dat, in cold blood, and den to face down all dem other men… he ain’t a nat’ral man.”

“He ain’t no coward, that’s for certain,” I says, remembering how he stood there, so ca’m.

“No,” Jim says, shaking his head slow. “He ain’t no coward. But sometimes, Huck, de bravest-lookin’ thing is jes’ de cruelest. What become o’ de gal? De dead man’s daughter?”

I thought about her, how pale she was, and how she screamed. “They took her off somewhere. I don’t know. She was all tore up.”

Jim was quiet for a long time. “Dat’s de real hurtin’ part,” he says finally. “Dat’s de part dat don’t never heal. De men, dey’ll talk about de shootin’ and de lynchin’ for a week, and den dey’ll find somethin’ else to jaw about. But dat po’ gal, she got to live wid it ever’ day. She got to see her pappy shot down in her mind ever’ time she close her eyes.” He sighed a deep, heavy sigh. “Dis sho’ is a wicked world, Huck.”

“Let’s go, Jim,” I says again. I started to untie the rope from the willow root. “I don’t want to be nowheres near this town when the sun comes up.”

We was just about to shove off into the current when we heard a twig snap in the woods behind us. It was a sharp, loud crack in the dead quiet of the night. Jim and me both froze stiff. We was used to noises, critters and such, but this warn’t a critter noise. This was a man’s foot.

“Who’s dat?” Jim whispers, his voice all tight.

I didn’t answer. I just hunkered down low on the raft, trying to melt into the shadows. My heart was thumping up in my throat something awful. We listened, and pretty soon we heard more footsteps, coming slow and steady, not trying to be quiet at all. Whoever it was, they was coming right for the raft. I thought maybe it was somebody that had seen us land, and was coming to take the raft, or worse, coming for Jim. I felt for the nail on the steering oar, which was the nearest thing to a weapon we had.

The footsteps stopped right at the edge of the willows. A man’s shape stood there, black against the starlight. He was tall and stood up very straight. He didn’t move for a minute, just looked out at the raft. Then his voice come out of the dark, and it was a voice I’d heard before that day. It was ca’m and slow and cold as a gravestone.

“You on the raft,” he says. “I know you’re there. I saw the boy head this way.”

It was Colonel Sherburn.

My blood run cold. I couldn’t move, I couldn’t breathe. Jim was flat on the deck behind the little tent, trying to make hisself invisible. I knew what he was thinking. Here was a man who had just killed somebody for cussing him, and what would he do to a runaway slave?

Sherburn stepped out of the willows and onto the little muddy bank. He had the same shotgun he’d had on the porch roof, held easy in the crook of his arm. He warn’t pointing it at us, but it was there.

“I require passage downriver,” he says, same as if he was asking for a dipper of water. “I will pay for it.”

I found my voice, but it come out all shaky and thin. “We… we ain’t goin’ downriver, mister. We’re just… restin’ here.”

He made a little sound, not a laugh, just a puff of air out his nose. “Don’t lie to me, boy. It’s an insult to my intelligence, and you can’t afford to insult me. This raft isn’t tied up to stay. It’s tied up to go. You’re running from something. I don’t care what. I am, in a manner of speaking, doing the same. Our purposes align.”

He took another step, right to the edge of the raft. He looked it over, from the steering oar to the little shelter.

“My presence in town has become… inconvenient,” he says. “The fools will work up their courage again in the daylight, or a magistrate with more ambition than sense will feel compelled to act. I prefer to be elsewhere. I need to be in Napoleon by morning. Your vessel will suffice.”

Napoleon was a good fifty miles downriver. That would take us all night. All night on a little raft with this man. I didn’t know what to say. I couldn’t say no. I looked over my shoulder at where Jim was hiding. Sherburn’s eyes followed mine.

“You have a partner,” he states. It warn’t a question. “Is he black or white?”

The bottom fell out of my stomach. This was it. I had to say something. “He’s… he’s my pap’s,” I stammered. “He’s with me.”

“I see,” Sherburn says, and I couldn’t tell what he was thinking at all. “Well, tell your pap’s man to come out. Hiding is a sign of guilt, and I have no time for complications.”

Slowly, ever so slowly, Jim raised hisself up. He was trembling, I could see it even in the dark. He stood there by the shelter, tall and scared, his hands hanging loose at his sides.

Sherburn looked him up and down for a long moment. It was the most awful quiet I ever been in. The river just lapped at the logs, and the crickets seemed to have shut up.

“He looks strong enough,” Sherburn says finally, mostly to hisself. Then he looks at me. “Twenty dollars in gold. For silence and passage to Napoleon. You will take me there, drop me at the south landing, and then you will continue on your way and forget you ever saw me. Is that understood?”

“Yes, sir,” I whispered.

“Good.” Without another word, he stepped onto the raft. It dipped a little under his weight. He was a solid man. He walked to the middle of the raft and stood there, holding his shotgun. He didn’t look at us. He looked down the river, into the dark.

“Then let’s not waste any more of the night,” he says.

My hands was shaking so bad I could hardly get the rope untied. But I did it. I got it loose and I grabbed the oar and shoved us off the bank. The current caught us, and the raft swung out into the middle of the river. The dark water slid under us, and the town of Pokeville, with its murder and its laughing, began to fall away behind.

And there we was. Me and Jim, floating down the great big river on our raft, just like we wanted. Only now we had the devil aboard with us.

For the first hour, nobody said a word. I stayed at the steering oar, keeping us in the channel. Jim, he’d gone to the other oar, at the front, and was making out like he was busy, though there warn’t nothing to do but let her float. It was his way of staying as far from Colonel Sherburn as he could get.

Sherburn hisself, he just stood. He didn’t sit down. He stood straight and still near the middle of the raft, like a statue, the shotgun resting in his arm, watching the dark banks slide by. The starlight glinted off the gun barrels. He was dressed in his fine town clothes, his black coat and his white shirt. He looked strange and out of place on our rough old raft, like a hawk that had lit on a chicken coop.

The quiet was worse than yelling. My mind kept going over it. Here was a man that had killed another man just a few hours ago. He shot him twice, ca’m as you please, and watched him die. And now here he was, floating down the river with us, with that same ca’mness. I kept expecting him to do something, to turn on us, to just decide he didn’t need witnesses and send us to the bottom of the river with his shotgun. Every time the raft creaked, I jumped.

By-and-by, he moved. He walked over to the edge and looked down into the black water.

“The river,” he says, and his voice made me flinch. “It washes everything away. Towns, sins, memories. Or so they say.” He turned his head and looked at me. His face was mostly shadow, but I could feel his eyes on me. “What do you think, boy? Do you think a man can wash himself clean?”

I didn’t know how to answer that. It felt like a trap. “I… I reckon not, sir,” I says. “Not all the way clean.”

He was quiet for a second. “A practical answer. Perhaps you’re not as much of a fool as the rest of this state’s progeny.” He came over and stood nearer to me, and I had to fight to keep from shrinking away. He warn’t looking at me now, but at the shore. “They called me a murderer today. And they were right. I am. But what are they? A mob of whey-faced cowards who wanted to commit the same act, but lacked the courage to do it alone. They need the anonymity of a pack, the safety of numbers, to do their dirty work. They are lynchers. I am an executioner. There is a difference.”

He said it all so matter-of-fact, like he was explaining how to shoe a horse.

“Boggs was a nuisance. A dangerous fool who threatened the peace. For twenty years, he did this. And for twenty years, the town laughed and enabled him, treating his madness like a town festival. They are as responsible for his death as I am. I merely supplied the final, logical punctuation to a sentence they had all been writing for years.”

I didn’t say nothing. I just kept my eyes on the river ahead. I didn’t want to agree with him, but a part of what he said sounded true, and that scared me more than anything. The way them fellers was laughing about Boggs before he got shot, and then the way they was laughing about being scared by Sherburn afterwards… it was all mixed up and ugly.

“You saw it,” he went on, his voice still low and even. “You have clear eyes. You saw a man who had been warned. A man who was given a deadline. He chose to cross it. I am a man of my word. In a world of blustering idiots and liars, that is the only currency with any real value. My word.”

He turned then and walked over to our little pile of supplies. He picked up our tin coffee pot and looked at it, then set it down. He seemed to be seeing everything, judging everything. He looked over at Jim, who was trying to look like a part of the raft.

“Your man,” Sherburn says to me, but loud enough for Jim to hear plain. “Does he have a name?”

“Jim, sir,” I says.

“Jim,” Sherburn repeated the name, slow. “You’re a long way from home, Jim.”

“Yessuh,” Jim says, his voice barely a whisper.

Sherburn nodded. “We all are.” He looked up at the stars. “This is a fine way to travel. No schedules, no boiler explosions. A man can think out here.” He was quiet for a spell, and then he says, “There is a skiff tied up at the Napoleon south landing. A man will be waiting for me. He will ask no questions. We will go our separate ways, and this night will be a secret kept by three men and the river.” He looked hard at me, then at Jim. “The river is good at keeping secrets. I trust you will be, too.”

“Yes, sir,” I said. And Jim mumbled, “Yessuh.”

I felt a little better then. He had a plan. He wasn’t just a killer on the run; he was a man with a destination, and we was just the ferry. If we did what he said, we’d be rid of him.

But the night was long. The raft drifted on, a little world of its own, with the three of us on it. The moon come up, a pale, lopsided thing, and it made the river look like a road of silver. It made Colonel Sherburn’s face look like it was carved out of bone. He finally sat down, his back against our shelter, the shotgun across his knees. He didn’t seem to sleep. His eyes was always open, watching.

A couple of times, a steamboat would come churning up the river, all lit up and full of noise and music. It was a strange and lonely feeling, to be out on our dark little raft watching one of them big boats go by. They was part of a whole other world, a world of people who was sleeping in beds and didn’t know there was a raft floating past with a killer on it. Every time a steamboat passed, I’d see Sherburn’s hand tighten on his gun, but he never made to hide. He just sat there, watching it go, his face proud and hard.

It must have been three in the morning, or maybe four. The moon was down, and it was that black, thick dark that comes just before the dawn. I was getting sleepy, my head nodding, and Jim was dozing on his feet at the front oar. Sherburn was still awake, still sitting.

Then I heard it. A new sound. It was the dip of oars in the water, coming from upriver, behind us. It was a steady sound, a rowing sound. Somebody was in a skiff, and they was coming fast, trying to catch up.

I stiffened up, and I saw Sherburn’s head lift. He heard it too. He stood up, smooth and silent, and moved to the back of the raft with me. He peered into the darkness behind us.

“Who would be on the river at this hour?” he said, more to hisself than to me.

The sound got closer. It was two men rowing, by the rhythm of it. They was rowing hard. My first thought was, it’s the law. They’d got a posse together and was coming after him. We was going to be caught in the middle of it.

Sherburn laid his hand on my shoulder. It was a heavy, firm grip. It didn’t feel friendly. It felt like a warning. “Boy,” he says, his voice like ice. “No matter what happens, you say nothing. You know nothing. You are a simple raft-boy taking a passenger downriver. Do you understand me?”

“Yes, sir,” I breathed.

The skiff was close now. We could see the shape of it, a long black shadow coming at us. A voice hollered out across the water.

“Ahoy, the raft! Heave to!”

It warn’t a lawman’s voice. It was a country voice, rough and angry. Sherburn made a low sound in his throat. He brought the shotgun up, not to his shoulder, but holding it ready.

The skiff pulled up alongside us, bumping against the logs. There was three men in it. Two was at the oars, big hairy fellers in homespun clothes. The third man was standing in the bow, holding a lantern. He held it up high, and the light fell across our raft. He was a skinny man with a long, sorrowful face and a big, droopy mustache. He looked from me, to Jim, and then his eyes fixed on Colonel Sherburn.

His face went hard. “There you are, you murdering dog,” the man says, his voice shaking with hate. “We reckoned you’d take to the river.”

Sherburn didn’t flinch. He stood there in the lantern light, looking down at the man in the skiff. “You have me at a disadvantage, sir,” Sherburn says, his voice ca’m as ever. “You know my name.”

“I’m Abner Grimes,” the man says. “And this here is my brother, Jed. Boggs was our cousin. Our kin. We come to see you get what’s coming to you.”

“What is coming to me, Mr. Grimes?” Sherburn asks. “A trial? A jury? I doubt you’ve brought one in that little boat.”

“We’re your jury,” Abner Grimes snarled. He reached inside his coat. “And this here is the judge.” He started to pull out a pistol.

It all happened faster than I can tell it. Before Grimes’s pistol was even clear of his coat, Sherburn’s shotgun was at his shoulder. It didn’t seem to aim, it just was there. But he didn’t shoot.

“Stop,” Sherburn commanded. His voice cut through the night like a knife. “Think, Mr. Grimes. Your cousin was a fool who died because he would not think. Do you want to join him?”

Grimes froze, his hand on his pistol.

“You have a small-caliber revolver,” Sherburn said, his voice level and instructive. “I have a ten-gauge shotgun with two barrels of buckshot. At this range, it will cut you and your brother in half. It will also likely capsize your skiff. Your third man may or may not be able to swim. These are the facts. I invite you to consider them.”

The two men at the oars looked scared. They stared at the twin black holes of Sherburn’s shotgun, and they stopped rowing. Abner Grimes was breathing hard, his face all twisted up. The lantern light shook in his hand.

“You ain’t gonna get away,” he whispered.

“I am already away,” Sherburn corrected him. “You are the ones who are out of your depth. Your cousin died for his honor, as he saw it. A drunken, foolish honor, but his own. Are you here for his honor, or for simple revenge? Because revenge is a messy business, Mr. Grimes. It rarely ends with the person you aim it at.” He glanced at me and Jim. “Look at this boy. Look at his slave. Are you prepared to murder them, too? They will be witnesses.”

Grimes looked at me, and his face got a little confused. He hadn’t thought about us at all. We was just part of the scenery.

“Now,” Sherburn continued, pressing his advantage. “You will put your pistol away. You will turn your skiff around. And you will row back to whatever mud bank you crawled out of. You will tell the world that you searched the river and found nothing. It is the only way you and your brothers will see the sunrise.”

For a long, long minute, nobody moved. It was just the five of them, and me and Jim stuck in the middle. Abner Grimes’s face was a study. You could see the hate fighting with the fear. He wanted to kill Sherburn so bad he could taste it. But he was looking right into the face of death hisself, and he knew it.

Finally, slow, he let go of his pistol. His shoulders slumped. The hate went out of his face and left only a tired, bitter sorrow.

“Go to hell, Sherburn,” he mumbled.

“In my own time,” Sherburn said. “Not in yours.”

He kept the shotgun leveled. Abner Grimes sat down heavy in the skiff. “Turn her around,” he said to his brothers.

The two big men put their oars in the water and started to pull the skiff around. It scraped along the side of our raft and then it was pointing back upriver. They rowed away into the dark, not looking back. The lantern got smaller and smaller, and then it was just a little yellow speck, and then it was gone. The sound of their oars faded out.

We was alone again.

Sherburn lowered the shotgun. He let out a long breath. He turned to me, and in the first gray light of dawn that was starting to show in the east, I saw that his face was wet with sweat. He warn’t a statue after all.

“You see, boy,” he said, his voice a little rough now. “That is the difference between a man and a mob. A mob has no head, no thought. But a man… a man can be reasoned with. Even if the reason is a shotgun.”

He didn’t say no more. He went back to his spot and sat down, and this time he looked tired. The sky got lighter and lighter, and the river turned from black to gray to a kind of muddy silver. We saw the houses and wood-piles of Napoleon ahead.

When we got to the south landing, there was a little skiff tied up, just like he said. A slouch-hatted man was asleep in it. Sherburn stood up. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a gold coin. It shined in the morning sun. He tossed it to me, and I caught it.

“Our transaction is complete,” he says. He looked at me, and his eyes had that cold, hard look again. “We have never met.”

“Yes, sir,” I says.

He stepped off the raft and onto the bank without looking back. He walked over to the skiff and nudged the sleeping man with his foot. The man woke up with a start. Sherburn said something to him I couldn’t hear, and then he got into the skiff. He didn’t look back at us, not once. The man pushed them off, and they started rowing, not downriver, but across, toward the wild Arkansas shore on the other side.

Me and Jim just watched them go until they was too small to see. I was still holding the gold coin. It felt heavy and hot in my hand.

Jim come over and stood beside me. He looked at the coin.

“Dat’s blood money, Huck,” he says, soft.

“I know it, Jim,” I says.

I looked at it for a second more. Then I cocked my arm back and I throwed it as far as I could out into the river. It made a little plink sound when it hit the water, and then it was gone.

“Now we’re clean of him,” I says.

Jim nodded. “Yessuh. Now we is.”

The sun was up proper now, and the river was wide and shining. We was free of the town, and we was free of Colonel Sherburn. I took up the steering oar, and Jim got his. The raft felt lighter, somehow. It felt like our own raft again. We drifted on down the river, leaving Napoleon and its secrets behind us, and for the first time in a long time, the water felt clean again.
