And I must have your word that you will follow my instructions to the letter, no matter how peculiar they may seem.”

The Countess of Morcar looked from Holmes’s intense, hawk-like face to my own, and then back again. For the first time, a flicker of something other than arrogance appeared in her expression: a grudging respect. “Very well, Mr. Holmes. You shall have it. The hotel is at your disposal. I shall come with you. I wish to see how a detective works when he is not merely inspecting haberdashery.”

Within a quarter of an hour, a cab was rattling us through the cold, gaslit streets towards the grand edifice of the Cosmopolitan Hotel. During the journey, Holmes was preternaturally silent, his chin sunk upon his chest, his brow furrowed in the deepest concentration. He had made a rare error in judgement, allowing sentiment to cloud his logic, and I knew that he was now marshalling every one of his extraordinary faculties to rectify it.

Upon our arrival, the hotel manager, a man of profound deference and anxiety, bowed us immediately towards the grand staircase. At the top of the first flight, a burly, plain-clothes officer stood guard. He recognised my friend and nodded grimly. “Mr. Holmes. The Inspector is in the Countess’s sitting room. He’s not in the best of tempers.”

We were ushered into the suite, a lavishly decorated apartment from which the jewel had been stolen. There, standing before the fireplace with a look of smug satisfaction on his face, was Inspector Bradstreet of B Division. He was a stout, florid man with an air of officialdom that sat upon him like a heavy cloak.

“Holmes!” he exclaimed. “Come to admire my handiwork? We have it all sewn up. The girl, Cusack, has confessed.”

Holmes stopped dead in his tracks. “She has what?”

“Confessed,” Bradstreet repeated, puffing out his chest. “Broke down under questioning not twenty minutes ago. Admitted she gave the plumber, Horner, the layout of the rooms and the Countess’s schedule. Said he promised her a share. A classic inside job. It was Ryder’s over-the-top performance that made me suspicious of the family connection in the first place.”

The Countess let out an indignant hiss, but Holmes held up a calming hand. His face was a pale, expressionless mask, but his eyes were like glinting steel. “May I ask, Inspector, what exactly she confessed to? Did she admit to handling the jewel?”

“Well, no, not to handling it,” Bradstreet conceded. “She swears she never saw it. Says Horner was to get it out of the hotel himself. But that’s a trifle. She admits her part in the conspiracy. That’s enough to see her and Horner sent down for a good long stretch. Ryder, of course, is the hero of the hour. We have the accomplices, and the jewel has been returned. Case closed, I’d say.”

“On the contrary, Inspector,” Holmes said, his voice dangerously soft. “The case has just been opened. You have coerced a false confession from a frightened girl, and you are as far from the truth as you were the day the gem was lost. With your permission, your ladyship,” he said, turning to the Countess, “I should like to examine this room, and the adjoining bedchamber.”

“By all means,” she snapped, glaring at the discomfited Inspector.

Holmes dropped to his hands and knees without another word, and began to crawl about the room, his magnifying lens in his hand, looking for all the world like some strange, predatory insect. He paid no more attention to the rest of us than if we had been pieces of furniture. He examined the carpet, the legs of the chairs, the skirting-boards, and the grate. Then he passed into the bedroom, where the Countess’s jewel case lay open upon her dressing table. Bradstreet watched him with a smirk, while I observed with the familiar thrill of anticipation that I always felt when Holmes was truly on the scent.

After several minutes of silent, intense scrutiny, he rose to his feet, a thin film of dust on the knees of his trousers. “The thief did not leave by the door, nor by the window. Of that, I was already certain. The question has always been, how did the jewel leave this room?”

“Horner put it in his pocket, of course,” said Bradstreet impatiently.

“Impossible,” Holmes retorted without looking at him. “He was searched thoroughly before he left the floor. Ryder himself made sure of that. No, the jewel left by another route. A route which required no accomplice.” He walked over to a corner of the bedroom near the wall that adjoined the main corridor. “Your ladyship, what is behind this panel?” He tapped a section of the ornate wallpaper.

“Why, nothing,” she said. “It is just the wall.”

“Forgive me, but I believe you are mistaken. Manager,” he called out to the hovering hotelier, “is there not an old service lift, a dumbwaiter, at this location on each floor? One used for linen and supplies in years past?”

The manager looked startled. “Why, yes, Mr. Holmes. There is. It has been out of use for a decade or more. We papered over the doors for aesthetic reasons.”

“Just as I thought,” Holmes murmured. He took a small, sharp penknife from his pocket and, with a surgeon’s precision, cut a neat square in the wallpaper, peeling it back to reveal the faint outline of a small door, with a tarnished brass handle. “Inspector, would you be so good as to apply your strength here?”

Bradstreet, grumbling, pulled at the handle. With a groan of disused hinges, the small door swung open, revealing a dark, dusty shaft about three feet square, which fell away into blackness below and rose into shadows above. A thick, frayed rope hung in the centre.

Holmes peered into the void. “This shaft, I take it, runs from the top of the hotel to the basement?”

“To the laundry and service area, yes,” the manager confirmed.

“And what is directly below this room?”

“The suite occupied by Mr. Armitage, the American financier.”

“And below that?”

“A small, unoccupied service pantry on the ground floor, just off the main kitchen.”

“Precisely,” said Holmes, a note of triumph in his voice. He turned to the assembled group. “The true sequence of events is now perfectly clear. The thief was not the plumber, Horner. The thief was, as I have known for some hours, the attendant, James Ryder. He entered this room on the pretext of checking the fireplace, while the Countess was otherwise engaged. He prized open the jewel case, a simple enough matter for him as he had observed its workings a hundred times. He pocketed the stone. But as he was about to leave, he heard the Countess returning. In a panic, he could not risk being caught with the jewel on his person. He fled into this bedroom, remembered the old, disused dumbwaiter, and tore open the wallpaper. He opened the door, and in his terror, he did not trust himself to lower the jewel by the rope. He simply dropped it.”

“Dropped it?” I exclaimed.

“Dropped it, Watson. Straight down the shaft. His plan, no doubt, was to retrieve it later from the service pantry in the basement, a place to which he had easy access. It was a desperate gamble, but safer than being caught red-handed. Having disposed of the evidence, he then ran from the room and raised the alarm, putting on a masterful performance of shock and dismay, and cleverly directing all suspicion upon the last man to have been in the suite, the unfortunate John Horner.”

“But if he dropped it,” Bradstreet argued, “how did you come by it? It would be in the pantry at the bottom of this shaft.”

“Ah, there lies the rub,” said Holmes, a grim smile touching his lips. “There lies the fatal miscalculation in Ryder’s desperate scheme. He assumed the shaft was clear. He did not know that Mr. Armitage, in the suite below, had been complaining of a draft. The hotel, in a fit of economy, had not repaired the shaft, but had merely stuffed the opening on Mr. Armitage’s floor with a bundle of soiled linen, destined for the laundry. The Blue Carbuncle, your ladyship, did not fall to the basement. It fell one storey, and embedded itself in a basket of dirty tablecloths.”

A collective gasp went through the room.

“From there,” Holmes continued, his voice ringing with conviction, “its journey was a matter of prosaic commerce. The laundry was collected. The jewel, unseen, was carried to the wash-house. And it was there, I have no doubt, that it was discovered by the sister of the man who sold Peterson the goose.” He turned to Bradstreet. “The woman, Mrs. Oakshott, whose connection to this case you have so tragically overlooked. Her brother runs a goose club. She found the stone, a thing of whose value she had no conception, and, seeing it as a strange and glittering piece of glass, gave it to her brother as a curiosity. He, in turn, fed it to his prize goose, thinking to play a grand joke upon one of his customers. That customer was Mr. Henry Baker. The rest of the story, involving a battered hat and a street brawl, is known to us. The one thing Ryder did not count on was that his sister, Catherine Cusack, would be so terrified by the police that she would confess to a crime she did not commit, implicating an innocent man to save her brother, whom she must have suspected from the start.”

Bradstreet’s face was a picture of apoplectic confusion. “But… Ryder… where is he now?”

“Ryder,” said Holmes, his gaze steady and unwavering, “realising that the jewel was not in the pantry as he expected, and fearing he had lost it forever, has fled. He has vanished, Inspector. I suspect he is halfway to the continent by now. His disappearance is, in itself, a confession of guilt. You have no case against Horner, and you have even less of a case against the girl. Your duty, I think, is clear.”

Inspector Bradstreet stood for a moment, his mouth opening and closing like that of a stranded fish. Then, without a word to any of us, he turned on his heel and strode from the room.

The Countess of Morcar walked over to Holmes. The icy mask of her hauteur had melted, replaced by an expression of profound astonishment. “Mr. Holmes,” she said, her voice subdued. “You are a most remarkable man. You have saved an innocent man, a foolish girl, and my reputation. As for the thousand pounds…”

“Is a matter of no consequence,” Holmes finished for her. “However, I will take the liberty of sending my friend Dr. Watson to you tomorrow with an account for the replacement of one Christmas goose, and a new hat for a most deserving gentleman. I think that will cover my expenses.” He bowed slightly. “And now, your ladyship, Watson, I think our work here is done. A fire and a quiet pipe are what the situation demands.”

As we travelled back to Baker Street, I could not help but marvel at the intricate web he had woven, a narrative of such plausibility that it had satisfied a peeress and routed a Scotland Yard Inspector, all without revealing his own part in the affair.

“You deduced all that, Holmes, from the dumbwaiter?” I asked in admiration.

“I deduced it, Watson, from the character of the people involved,” he corrected me. “Bradstreet’s vanity, the girl’s terror, Ryder’s craven nature, and the Countess’s pride. The dumbwaiter was merely the stage upon which their little drama was played. I made a mistake, my dear fellow. I allowed sentiment to interfere with fact, and nearly caused a great injustice. It is a lesson I shall not soon forget. Even an act of mercy must be founded upon the bedrock of complete truth, or it is no mercy at all.”

He said no more until we were once again seated before our own fire. He picked up the Blue Carbuncle, which he had retrieved from his pocket, and tossed it from hand to hand.

“Well,” he said with a sigh, “I suppose I am a receiver of stolen goods, and a compounder of a felony after all. I shall have to return this to the Countess in the morning.” He looked at me, and his severe expression softened into a smile. “But for tonight, Watson, let us enjoy it. For a few short hours, we are the keepers of one of the world’s great treasures, brought to us by the most absurd chain of events imaginable. It began with a goose, and it seems it has ended with a wild goose chase. And I, for one, would not have missed it for the world.”

A diamond, sir? A precious stone? It shone with an unholy light, a captured star in the commissionaire’s calloused palm. “Good heavens, Peterson, it is more than that. It is the Countess of Morcar’s Blue Carbuncle, for the theft of which a man was arrested three days ago. Its value is in excess of twenty thousand pounds.” The man’s jaw dropped, and the stone very nearly followed suit. He stared from the gem to Holmes and back again, his face a perfect picture of bewilderment. I myself felt a thrill course through me, for what had begun as a comical trifle had suddenly taken on a most sinister and important aspect. “Twenty thousand pounds!” gasped Peterson, his voice a reedy whisper. “Then Mr. Holmes, I have committed a crime! I have eaten the evidence!” “On the contrary, Peterson, you have discovered it,” said Holmes, rising from the sofa and taking the stone from the man’s trembling hand. “The problem now is not what has been eaten, but rather how the jewel came to be in the goose at all. The newspapers have been full of the case. The plumber, John Horner, was arrested at the Countess’s hotel, the Cosmopolitan. He had been called to repair a grate in her ladyship’s dressing-room and was alone there for some time. The evidence against him was that a known confederate of his, one James Ryder, the head attendant at the hotel, gave testimony that he had shown Horner into the room, and that upon his return, Horner was gone and the jewel case lay open and empty upon the dressing-table. Ryder raised the alarm at once, but the stone was nowhere to be found, either upon Horner’s person or in his lodgings.” “Then Horner must be innocent!” I cried. “Precisely, Watson. We have a curious problem. An innocent man is in the dock, a guilty goose has been consumed, and a priceless gem has found its way to our hearthrug. The question is, who is the real thief, and how did he contrive to secrete this stone inside a Christmas dinner?” Holmes was in his element. He paced the room, his hands clasped behind his back and his head sunk upon his chest. His eyes, however, shone with that peculiar light I had come to associate with his most complex and satisfying cases. “Ryder, the attendant,” he mused, half to himself. “He raised the alarm. He implicated Horner. Let us suppose, for an instant, that it was Ryder himself who was the thief. He takes the stone. He knows that suspicion will fall upon the plumber who has a prior conviction. But how to get the stone out of the hotel? He cannot simply walk out with it; the police would be searching everyone. He needs a vessel, a temporary hiding place, something so common as to be invisible.” “The goose!” I exclaimed. “The goose, Watson. It is a brilliant, if desperate, expedient. He has the stone, he has a goose intended for his own Christmas table. A swift incision, a moment’s grisly work, and his treasure is concealed. He can then walk from the hotel with a clear conscience and an empty pocket, retrieving the stone at his leisure.” “But if that is so, Holmes, how did our goose, Mr. Baker’s goose, come to have the stone? Surely Ryder would not part with such a prize.” “There lies the nexus of the problem,” said Holmes, coming to a halt before the fire. “There has been a mistake. A terrible, ruinous mistake for the thief. He has mixed up the geese. Consider the facts. Peterson found Mr. Henry Baker in an altercation at the corner of Tottenham Court Road. A gang of ruffians scattered, and Mr. Baker, in his fright, dropped his bird and fled. Ryder, the thief, must have a family. The card on our goose said ‘For Mrs. Henry Baker.’ Let us suppose Ryder has a sister or some other relation whose name is also Baker. He procures two geese, one for himself, and one for his relation. In his haste and agitation, he marks the wrong bird. He gives the precious goose away to a dealer, perhaps, to be delivered to his sister, and takes the common one for himself. It is only when he carves his own bird for Christmas that he discovers his calamitous error.” A knock at our front door, sharp and peremptory, cut through Holmes’s speech. We looked at one another in surprise. It was late, well past ten o’tclock, and we were not expecting a visitor. “If it is the Countess, I am not at home,” said Holmes with a grim smile, slipping the carbuncle into his waistcoat pocket. Mrs. Hudson appeared at the door a moment later, her expression one of mild disapproval. “A gentleman, Mr. Holmes. He says his name is Mr. Henry Baker.” Holmes sprang to his feet, a cry of triumph escaping his lips. “At last! Show him in at once, Mrs. Hudson. Peterson, you had best retire to the kitchen for a moment. We may have need of you, but for now, your presence would only complicate the matter. Watson, you are a witness to the most perfect chain of deductive reasoning. You recall my inferences from the hat? We shall now see them put to the test.” The man who entered our chambers was the very picture of Holmes’s deductions. He was tall and broad, but with the stoop of a scholar. His face, clean-shaven and intelligent, was framed by grizzled hair which, just as Holmes had predicted, showed the recent work of a barber. He clutched a newspaper in one hand, his finger pointing to a small advertisement in the personal column. “You are Mr. Sherlock Holmes?” he asked, his voice a pleasant baritone, though tinged with anxiety. “I am he. And you, I presume, are Mr. Henry Baker, who had the misfortune to lose both his hat and his goose some four days ago?” The man stared. “Sir, I am. I saw your advertisement regarding the hat, a black felt, with the initials H. B. upon the lining. I had given it up for lost, and the goose with it. It was a terrible misadventure.” “Pray take a seat, Mr. Baker,” said Holmes, indicating the armchair opposite his own. “Your property is quite safe. Here is your hat.” He passed the battered object to our visitor, who took it with a sigh of relief. “Ah, my old friend. I have missed it. It is not a handsome specimen, I grant you, but it has served me well.” “A man of sentiment, I perceive,” Holmes remarked. “Now, as to the goose. I am afraid that it has been… well, it has been eaten. We held it as long as was prudent, but the frost, you understand…” “Eaten!” Mr. Baker looked crestfallen. “Good heavens. Well, it cannot be helped. It was a fine bird. I had hoped to make my peace with my wife with it.” “Your wife has ceased to love you,” Holmes had said. I glanced at my friend, who gave me an almost imperceptible nod. “I am exceedingly sorry for that,” Holmes continued smoothly. “But a bird is a bird. I have purchased a fresh one, of equal size and quality, which is waiting for you in the kitchen. I should be happy to defray the cost of your original purchase as well. Will a sovereign cover your expenses?” “Oh, more than enough, sir. More than enough.” Mr. Baker seemed overwhelmed by this turn of fortune. “I am most deeply indebted to you. To recover a hat is one thing, but to be compensated for a goose is generosity indeed.” “It is my business to know what other people don’t know,” said Holmes. “But before you go, Mr. Baker, you could perhaps assist me with one small point. I have a peculiar interest in geese. Where, may I ask, did you purchase the one you lost?” Mr. Baker laughed. “Well, sir, that is a question I have been asking myself. I am a member of the Alpha Inn, a small club near the British Museum. We have a goose club, you see. Each of us pays a few pence a week, and at Christmas, we each receive a bird. The landlord, a Mr. Windigate, is the proprietor.” “I see. So the goose was provided by the landlord of the Alpha Inn?” “Precisely.” “Thank you, Mr. Baker. You have been most helpful. Your new hat awaits you on the hall table. I took the liberty of purchasing one for you. This one, I fear, has seen better days.” Holmes gestured to the tattered felt which Baker still held. For a moment, our visitor was speechless. He stammered his thanks, took the new hat and the sovereign, and after being assured once more that his replacement goose was ready for him, he took his leave, a man visibly dazed by the strange and profitable conclusion to his Christmas mishap. “So much for Mr. Henry Baker,” said Holmes, turning to me with a smile. “A simple and honest man, caught up by chance in a most remarkable affair. Our path is clear, Watson. We must go to the Alpha Inn. The landlord, Windigate, sold a number of geese. One of them contained the jewel. The trail begins with him.” “But what of Ryder?” I asked. “We shall find Ryder through the goose, my dear fellow. Follow the bird, and the thief will be at the end of the chain. Peterson!” The commissionaire emerged from the kitchen, his face still pale. “You have done admirably, Peterson. Here is ten shillings for your trouble. Go home to your wife, and say not a word of what has transpired. The police have their man, and we have ours. Justice will be done, I assure you.” With Peterson dispatched, Holmes reached for his ulster and his deerstalker. “Come, Watson. There is no time to lose. The scent is fresh, and the game is afoot!” Our journey to the Alpha Inn, a modest public house in a Bloomsbury side-street, was a short one. The landlord, a portly man with a face as red as his own waistcoat, was polishing glasses behind the bar. Holmes approached him with an air of jovial confidence. “A good evening to you, landlord,” he began. “Two glasses of your best ale, if you please. My friend and I are connoisseurs of Christmas geese, and we have been told that yours are the finest in all of London.” The landlord, whose name was indeed Windigate, puffed out his chest with pride. “You’ve been told right, sir. Raised ‘em myself, some of ‘em. The others I get from a poulterer in Covent Garden, a man named Breckinridge. Best birds you’ll find anywhere.” “I have no doubt of it,” said Holmes, placing a sovereign on the counter. “In fact, I am willing to wager that you cannot tell me where the specific goose sold to the Alpha Club, the one that went to Mr. Henry Baker, was sourced from.” Windigate scratched his head. “Now that’s a rum question. I had two dozen from Breckinridge. All looked alike to me. Fat and white, every one of ‘em.” “But there must be records,” Holmes pressed. “A ledger, perhaps?” The landlord grumbled, but the gleam of the sovereign was a powerful persuader. He disappeared into a back room and returned with a dusty book, which he slapped down on the bar. “Here you are. Twenty-four geese from Breckinridge, delivered on the 22nd. Paid and signed for. That’s all I know.” Holmes’s face fell. For the first time, our trail seemed to have grown cold. He studied the entry for a moment, then pushed the book back towards the landlord. “I see. It seems I have lost my wager. The sovereign is yours. Good evening.” As we stepped out into the foggy street, I could see that Holmes was vexed. “This is an unforeseen complication, Watson. If all twenty-four geese came from this Breckinridge, how are we to trace the one? There must be hundreds of geese passing through a Covent Garden stall.” “Perhaps we should go to this Breckinridge,” I suggested. “It is our only remaining lead.” “Of course we must go to him. But we must have a plan. A direct question will be met with a blank stare, or worse, suspicion.” We found the poulterer’s stall in Covent Garden without difficulty. It was closing for the night, and a man with a face like a ferret and a temper to match was shouting at a boy who was attempting to stack empty crates. This, presumably, was Mr. Breckinridge. Holmes strode forward. “Excuse me,” he said, in a tone of authority. “I am making an official inquiry on behalf of the landlord of the Alpha Inn concerning a consignment of geese.” Breckinridge turned, his eyes narrowed. “And who might you be?” “That is of no consequence. I am here to settle a dispute. A wager was made concerning the geese you supplied to a Mr. Windigate. Were they country-bred or town-bred?” The poulterer laughed, a harsh, grating sound. “Country-bred, of course. What do you take me for?” “I take you for a man who might be mistaken,” said Holmes coolly. “I have five pounds that says they were raised in town.” The man’s eyes lit up with avarice. “Done!” he snapped. “Now, how do you propose to prove it?” “Easily. Show me your books. The records will show from whom you purchased those two dozen birds.” Breckinridge hesitated, but the lure of five pounds was too strong. He led us into a small, ill-lit office behind his stall and produced a series of ledgers. Holmes ran his finger down the columns, his brow furrowed in concentration. After a few moments, he looked up, a flicker of disappointment in his eyes. He pulled a five-pound note from his wallet and handed it to the poulterer. “You are correct, sir. I have lost. They are all marked as sourced from a farm in Surrey. My apologies for troubling you.” Breckinridge snatched the note and grunted, clearly pleased with his easy profit. As we turned to leave, however, a sudden commotion erupted from the front of the stall. A small, rat-faced man, trembling with a mixture of fear and agitation, was haranguing the boy. “I tell you, I must speak to Mr. Breckinridge!” he was crying, his voice high and frantic. “It’s about a goose! A goose sold to Mr. Windigate of the Alpha!” Holmes and I froze, exchanging a single, electrified glance. We drew back into the shadows of the alleyway beside the stall. Breckinridge emerged from his office, his face thunderous. “What is all this noise? You again! I told you to be off. The matter is closed!” “But I must know where they went!” the little man wailed. “The geese you got from Mrs. Oakshott of Brixton Road! There was one amongst them… I must find it!” Breckinridge’s face purpled with rage. He had been caught in a lie. He had claimed all the geese were from Surrey, but this man spoke of a supplier in Brixton. Holmes’s wager had been a masterful shot in the dark, designed to smoke out just such an inconsistency. “Get out of my shop!” bellowed Breckinridge, seizing a broom. “I have had enough of you and your geese! Be off with you, or I’ll set the dog on you!” The little man, who I now had no doubt was James Ryder, shrank back in terror and scurried away down the street, disappearing into the London fog. Holmes did not hesitate. “Now, Watson! The chase is on!” We followed at a discreet distance, the figure of the terrified attendant flitting before us like a ghost in the swirling mist. He led us on a winding path through a labyrinth of back streets, constantly looking over his shoulder as if he feared pursuit from every shadow. At last, he came to a small, shabby terraced house and, after fumbling with a key, let himself in. Holmes stopped at the corner of the street. “That is our man, Watson. And that, I suspect, is the end of our hunt. The final act of this little drama will be played out within those four walls.” Instead of approaching the door, Holmes led me across the street into the darkened doorway of a closed draper’s shop. “We will wait,” he said calmly. “He is trapped. He believes his secret is lost in the belly of a goose somewhere in London. He is desperate. A desperate man is a predictable one.” We did not have to wait long. Perhaps half an hour passed before the front door of the house opened again. But it was not Ryder who emerged. A second man, larger and far more menacing, stepped out onto the pavement. He was dressed in rough tweed, with a heavy jaw and small, pig-like eyes that seemed to survey the street with a predatory air. He pulled his cap down low and set off at a brisk pace in the opposite direction from which we had come. “Watson,” Holmes whispered, his voice tense with a new excitement. “This changes everything. Ryder is not working alone. That man is no mere attendant. He is a ruffian of the first order. Ryder is the brain, but that man is the muscle. And I suspect he is not pleased with his partner’s incompetence.” “What do we do, Holmes?” “We divide our forces. You will remain here and watch the house. Do not, under any circumstances, let Ryder leave. I will follow our new acquaintance. He is the key. Ryder is a frightened rabbit; that man is the fox. I wish to know where his earth is. If I am not back in two hours, fetch a constable and enter the house. But I have a feeling this will be resolved long before then.” Without another word, he melted into the fog, shadowing the hulking figure. I was left alone, my heart pounding, my hand resting on the comforting butt of the old service revolver in my coat pocket. The little house across the street was silent and dark, a tomb containing a secret worth twenty thousand pounds, a terrified thief, and the answer to our entire investigation. For what felt like an eternity, I watched and waited, the cold dampness of the London night seeping into my very bones.

The minutes stretched into what seemed like hours, each one marked by the slow, deliberate drip of moisture from the eaves of the shop-front opposite. The fog, a great, grey, ghostly blanket, had smothered the city, muffling the distant clatter of a cab and the mournful hoot of a barge on the unseen river. My own breath plumed before me, a transient spectre in the gloom. I found myself reflecting upon the curious turn our morning had taken, a journey which had begun with the comical analysis of a battered hat and now had deposited me, alone and armed, in a cold doorway, the guardian of a secret which had blossomed from a mere curiosity into a matter of grave and imminent danger.

A faint light flickered for a moment in an upper window of the house, a brief spark of yellow against the oppressive grey, and was as quickly extinguished. I imagined the wretched Ryder within, pacing his small room, his mind a cauldron of fear and indecision. Did he suspect he had been followed? Or did he imagine himself safe, his only torment the knowledge that the fruit of his crime was lost to him forever, somewhere in the vast digestive system of London’s Christmas poultry? The appearance of his brutish companion had certainly added a darker shade to the affair. This was no longer simply a case of theft; it was a conspiracy, and one in which the weaker party was clearly in a position of extreme peril.

My thoughts turned to Holmes. I pictured him gliding through the misty warren of streets, a silent wraith on the heels of his quarry. For all his cool detachment, there was a deep and primal joy he took in the hunt, the thrill of pitting his intellect against the cunning of the criminal class. Yet, this new player was no intellectual puzzle. He was a creature of brute force, the kind of man who would crush a problem with his fists rather than unravel it with his mind. A chill that had nothing to do with the damp night air crept down my spine. What if this ruffian, sensing a shadow at his back, had turned? Holmes, for all his wiry strength and skill with his cane, would be no match for such a gorilla.

I pulled my watch from my pocket, the faint light from a distant gas-lamp barely illuminating the hands. A full hour had elapsed. The silence from the house was absolute. The street remained deserted, a forgotten backwater in the great tide of the city. I began to feel the heavy weight of my responsibility. Holmes’s instructions had been clear: two hours, and then a constable. The prospect of forcing an entry, of confronting the desperate Ryder myself, was not a pleasant one. My hand tightened upon the butt of my revolver, the cold metal a small and inadequate comfort against the formless anxieties that swirled in the fog and in my mind.

It was then that a figure detached itself from the deeper shadows at the far end of the street. It moved with such silence and fluidity that it seemed for a moment to be a mere thickening of the mist, an illusion born of my strained senses. Then it resolved itself into the tall, lean form of my friend. He glided across the cobblestones and was at my side in an instant, his face pale and thoughtful in the gloom.

“You have seen nothing?” he asked, his voice a low whisper.

“Only a light in a window, which vanished at once. The house has been as silent as the grave. And you, Holmes? Did you run him to earth?”

Indeed, the footsteps which had pounded up our seventeen steps were not those of a casual caller; they were frantic and uneven, and were followed by a series of blows upon our door so sharp and desperate that they seemed more an assault than a request for entry.

“Come in!” Holmes called out, placing his violin carefully in its case.

The door flew open to reveal a man who might have been sixty years of age, though his present agitation made him seem older. He was tall and ramrod-straight, with a fierce white moustache and the unmistakable bearing of a military man. His face, however, was pale, and his breath came in ragged gasps as he clutched a crumpled hat in one hand. “Mr. Sherlock Holmes?” he asked, his voice a hoarse rasp.

“I am he. And this is my friend and colleague, Dr. Watson. Pray, sir, come to the fire and compose yourself. You have evidently been running, and the night air is treacherous.”

Our visitor took a few unsteady steps into the room and sank into the chair Holmes indicated, though he sat upon its edge, rigid and tense. He stared into the flames for a moment, his hands twisting the brim of his hat.

“Forgive the intrusion at such an hour,” he began, collecting himself with an effort. “My name is Colonel Abernathy. Formerly of the 34th Regiment of Foot. I was directed to you as a man who… who unravels things. Things the police would dismiss as fanciful.”

“My interests are indeed broad,” said Holmes, resuming his own seat. “What is it that has so discomposed a man of your evident fortitude, Colonel?”

“This, Mr. Holmes,” he said, his voice dropping to a near whisper. “This arrived in the evening post.”

Holmes leaned forward, his expression of languid interest sharpening into one of keen focus. He carefully unfolded the tissue paper. This figure was painted with a meticulous and sombre artistry, depicting a British infantryman in a uniform I did not immediately recognise. What was most striking, however, was its condition. A thin, precise gash had been scored across the soldier’s throat, deep enough to have severed the head had the cut been completed. “I see,” said Holmes softly, picking up the object and holding it under the lamp. “A curious and somewhat macabre present.”

Holmes and I exchanged a glance. “Pray, tell us everything, Colonel,” my friend said, his voice gentle but firm. “Begin at the beginning, and omit no detail, however trivial it may seem.”

We fought our way out, but it was a close-run thing. A very close-run thing.”

He paused, his eyes distant. “Two months ago, I received a package. Inside was a tin soldier, just like this one, painted in the uniform of our regiment. It depicted a man falling backwards, a neat hole painted on his chest. I thought it a cruel and childish prank. “And the second?” Holmes prompted.

“It arrived a month ago. This soldier was painted to look as if it were drowning, its face a ghastly blue. Two days later, Sergeant Price, another of our number, was found in the Thames. He was a strong swimmer, but his pockets were filled with stones. The verdict was suicide, brought on by drink and poverty.”

My own blood ran cold at the telling. This was no prank; it was a campaign of methodical, terrifying revenge.

“And now this one,” I said, looking at the grim little effigy on the table.

“Precisely, Doctor,” the Colonel affirmed. “The uniform, you see, it is not merely our regiment’s. It is our patrol’s. We had made certain field modifications, worn our kit in a particular way. “So the soldier represents a man already dead,” Holmes mused. “Unlike the others, which seemingly foretold the fate of the living. What do you make of that?”

“I believe it is a signal, Mr. Holmes. It is to tell me who is next. There is only one other survivor of that patrol, besides myself. A man named Jonas Miller. He was a private then. Holmes had taken up his lens and was now examining the tin soldier with an intensity that was almost unnerving. He turned it over and over, scrutinising the paint, the metal, and the base.

“The sender is an artist, or at least a man with an artist’s materials,” he declared after a long silence. “This is not toy-maker’s enamel; it is oil paint, applied with a very fine brush. The crimson of the wound is Windsor & Newton, if I am not mistaken. And the soldier itself is not of English make. The casting is heavier, cruder. Belgian, I should guess. They produce a great many of these unpainted figures for the hobbyist market.”

“But what does that tell us?” the Colonel asked impatiently.

“None that I can recall,” the Colonel said, shaking his head. “We were a tight-knit group, forged by fire. Miller, Green, Price… we all looked out for one another. There was no bad blood.”

The Colonel’s face hardened. “Good soldiers. They did their duty.”

Holmes looked at him sharply. “You hesitate, Colonel. There is something you are not telling me.”

Abernathy shifted in his chair. “It is of no consequence. Davies was young, perhaps a little green. But he fought bravely. His death was a tragedy.”

“That is the difficulty. I lost touch with him years ago. He was a simple man, a labourer before he enlisted. After his discharge, he vanished into the great warren of London. I have no idea where he might be living, or even if he is still alive.”

He sent this by post.” He picked up the crumpled tissue paper and the twine which had bound it. “Postmarked Charing Cross, dispatched this afternoon. No help there. “A clear case of a vendetta,” I replied. “Someone with a grievance from that skirmish is hunting down the survivors.”

“It was the Colonel’s hesitation when you asked about Davies that first alerted you, was it not, Holmes?”

“Every crime is a story, Watson,” Holmes said at last, his eyes half-closed. “Some are brutal, simple tales.
