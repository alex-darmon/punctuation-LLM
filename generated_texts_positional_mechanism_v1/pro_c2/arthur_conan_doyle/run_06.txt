In the days that followed, Mr. Finch held true to the strange pact. He would call upon Ashworth House two or three times a week, and I would often accompany him, for he seemed to feel my presence was a steadying influence. Our visits were models of propriety. The entire situation was now illuminated by a dreadful, glaring light. Blackwood was not his name; it was his claim. He was a Macleod, and he was living on the borders of the estate that he believed was rightfully his. His fury at Finch was not that of a protective guardian, but of a mortal enemy. He saw in my friend not a suitor for his daughter, but the heir of the man who had destroyed his family.

And yet, this did not explain everything. If his hatred was so profound, why had he agreed to the three month truce? Why allow the courtship at all? We were no longer simply observing a threat; we were now in a race against it. We had to expose him, and protect Finch, before he could put his deadly plan into action.

The cunning of the man was diabolical. He had acquired two of these terrible weapons. One he had allowed us to see, to lull us into a false sense of security, to make us believe we understood his timeline. And now he was shipping it away, a piece of theatrical misdirection, while the true instrument of death remained hidden, ready for its work. The three month delay was not a waiting period for a weapon to arrive; it was a waiting period for a duplicate to be manufactured and for his chosen victim to return to the killing ground.

The one man who could help me, Finch, was on his way to a deathtrap, and the law, in its majestic blindness, refused to see the danger. There was only one thing for it. If Stapleton intended to hunt, then I must become a hunter myself. I had to find the second rifle.

That evening, under the cloak of a moonless, star-pricked sky, I set out for Ashworth House. I was no spy, and the task was hateful to me, but the thought of Finch’s cheerful, trusting face, and the cold, reptilian gaze of the man who planned to murder him, drove me onwards. I did not approach the house itself, for I knew it would be watched. Instead, I made my way to the old quarry, the same dark hill from which I had witnessed that strange scene between the lovers months before. It was a place of broken ground and ancient stone workings, a perfect place to hide a thing, or to hide oneself. My theory was simple: Stapleton would not keep the weapon in his house. It was too risky. He would have secreted it somewhere nearby, somewhere he could retrieve it easily on the appointed day.

For hours I searched, my heart pounding at every rustle in the bracken, every hoot of a distant owl. I scrabbled amongst the rocks and peered into the black mouths of old mining shafts, my hands raw, my clothes torn. It was a fool’s errand, I knew, but I could not rest. As the first grey light of dawn began to seep into the eastern sky, I slumped down onto a flat rock, exhausted and defeated. I had failed.

It was then that I saw it. Not with my eyes, but with my mind. I saw Stapleton, the meticulous man of science, the collector. Where would such a man keep his most prized specimen? He would not simply dump it in a hole. He would protect it. He would house it. I remembered my walk earlier that day, the path that ran along the back of Stapleton’s property. I had noted, with idle curiosity, a small, stone-built shed, half-hidden by a stand of gorse. It was his tool shed, where he kept his gardening implements and, no doubt, the various poisons and preservatives for his collection. It was the one place on his property I had not considered.

A new surge of energy coursed through me. The sky was growing lighter by the minute; I had little time. I scrambled down from the quarry and made my way, staying in the deep shadows of the hedgerows, to the perimeter of Ashworth House. The shed was there, just as I remembered, a squat, windowless structure with a heavy wooden door secured by a large, rusty padlock.

The padlock was my first obstacle, and for a moment, it seemed an insurmountable one. But as I examined it, I saw that the wood of the hasp it was attached to was old and soft with rot. Taking a deep breath, I braced myself and threw my full weight against the door. There was a splintering crack, and the door flew inwards. I stumbled into the musty darkness, my heart hammering against my ribs.

The shed smelled of damp earth, oil, and chemicals. In the dim light, I could make out spades, forks, and coils of rope. On a high shelf were rows of glass jars containing formaldehyde and other noxious fluids. But I had no eyes for any of this. My gaze was fixed on a long, canvas-wrapped object propped in the darkest corner of the shed, almost invisible behind a stack of empty peat sacks.

With trembling hands, I pulled it out. The canvas was stiff and smelled of wax. I fumbled with the ties and peeled it back. And there it was. A long tube of polished steel, a complex mechanism of gears and levers, a finely crafted wooden stock, and a powerful telescopic sight. It was the twin, the exact replica, of the rifle we had discovered in the crate.

I had it. I had the proof. But as I stood there, clutching the cold, heavy weapon, a new and more terrible thought entered my mind. Finding the rifle was not enough. The constable had made that clear. He needed to catch the criminal in the act. My discovery, made by breaking and entering, would be thrown out of any court. Stapleton, with his devilish cunning, would simply deny everything. He would claim the rifle was a collector’s piece, or that I had planted it there myself.

No, I had to do more. I had to use Stapleton’s own trap against him. I had to let him walk into it, believing it was his own creation. A plan, as cold and ruthless as one of Finch’s own, began to form in my mind. I re-wrapped the rifle carefully, and tucked it back into its corner, making sure the peat sacks were arranged exactly as they had been before. Then, I took a small, sharp file from the tool bench. Working quickly, I located the firing pin within the rifle’s complex mechanism. I did not have the skill to remove it entirely without leaving obvious signs of tampering. Instead, I carefully, painstakingly, filed the tip down, just a fraction of a millimetre, not enough to be noticed by a cursory inspection, but enough, I prayed, to ensure that when the hammer fell, it would strike nothing but empty air. It would not fire.

I crept from the shed, pulling the broken door closed behind me so that the damage was not immediately obvious. The sun was now rising above the horizon, flooding the heath with golden light. The day of Finch’s return had begun. My telegram would not have reached him in time; he would be on the morning train, speeding towards his own execution. But now, the executioner’s weapon was a dud. All that remained was for me to play my part. I had to let Stapleton retrieve his useless rifle. I had to let him take up his position. I had to let him aim. And I had to be there, with a witness, when he pulled the trigger and met with nothing but a hollow click. I had to catch the murderer, not with the evidence of his weapon, but with the evidence of his intent, written on his face in the moment of his failure. The hunter had become the bait, and I, his humble friend, had become the trap.

He had yielded, but he had yielded like a general who gives up a minor outpost to prepare for a more decisive assault. This three month truce was no whim; it was a timetable. A chilling thought took root in my mind: he had not given up his murderous design, but had merely postponed it. He required time, but for what I could not imagine. The man was a spider, and he was content to let the fly buzz happily in the web for a time, so long as the final outcome was assured.

My nights became a torment of sleepless speculation. I watched Sir Henry, who was now in the highest of spirits, making plans and writing letters, his whole being illuminated by the promise of his future happiness. The contrast between his blissful ignorance and my own dreadful knowledge was a constant and heavy burden. I redoubled my vigilance, never letting him stray far, a course of action which he, in his lover’s preoccupation, seemed scarcely to notice. My one small comfort was my secret knowledge of the disabled rifle, a single flawed cog in the man’s infernal machine.

It was some ten days after Stapleton’s strange capitulation that a telegram arrived which threw our quiet household into a state of considerable excitement. It was from a Mr. Finch, a distant cousin of the Baskervilles who had been named in the old entail as the next in line should Sir Henry meet with a premature end. The man was an antiquarian of some repute, and he proposed to visit Baskerville Hall to consult certain ancient documents relating to the family history which were kept in the library.

Sir Henry was all for it. “The very thing, Watson!” he declared, waving the flimsy piece of paper. “Finch is a capital fellow, if a trifle dry. He can poke about amongst the dusty old books to his heart’s content while I am busy with the estate. It will be good to have another friendly face about the place.”

But to me, the name Finch struck a note of pure terror. If Stapleton’s aim was to inherit, then this man, this harmless bookworm, was another life that stood between him and the fortune. He was not just visiting the Hall; he was walking directly into the dragon’s den. Stapleton’s three-month deadline suddenly made a dreadful kind of sense. He had been waiting, perhaps, for all his obstacles to assemble in one place. I felt the web drawing tighter, its strands growing more complex and more deadly with every passing hour. I had to warn someone, but who would believe me? My suspicions were all conjecture, smoke without a fire that any court of law could recognise.

The day of Finch’s arrival was overcast and dreary, with a low grey sky that seemed to press down upon the moor, muffling all sound and draining the colour from the landscape. I found myself in a state of the most extreme nervous agitation, starting at every shadow and sound. Sir Henry, full of pleasant anticipation, had driven the trap to the station to meet his cousin, leaving me alone at the Hall. I could not bear the inaction. My mind raced, turning over the same dreadful possibilities again and again. Stapleton would not, I reasoned, make his move in the open. He was too cunning for that. His previous actions, and the very nature of the weapon I had discovered, pointed to a carefully planned assassination from a distance. He would need a pretext to be out upon the moor with his collecting gear, a clear line of sight, and a moment when his victim was isolated.

A sudden, desperate thought seized me. The rifle! I had disabled it, yes, but what if he had discovered my tampering? What if he had another? The man’s foresight was diabolical. I could not rest until I knew for certain. Slipping out of the house by a side door, I made my way through the drizzling mist towards Merripit House. My errand was one of the utmost folly, a trespass which could land me in the deepest trouble, yet I felt compelled by a force stronger than reason.

The house, when I came in sight of it, was dark and silent, with a single thread of smoke rising from one chimney into the damp air. I crept around to the back, my heart pounding a frantic rhythm against my ribs. The small outhouse where he kept his naturalist’s equipment stood as before. I thanked Providence that my previous entry had been a rough one, for the splintered wood around the lock, which I had done my best to disguise, would now serve to hide the fresh marks I was about to make. Using a stout penknife, I worked at the damaged frame until I could force the door open once more.

Inside, the familiar smell of chemicals and dry peat hung in the air. I made straight for the corner, my hands shaking so that I could barely grasp the stack of empty sacks. I threw them aside. The corner was empty. The long, canvas-wrapped object was gone.

A cold sweat broke out upon my brow. For a moment, the world seemed to tilt and swim before my eyes. He had it. He had the rifle. My carefully laid plan, my one secret advantage, had been for nothing. He was armed, and his quarry was at that very moment on his way to the slaughter. Whether he knew of my sabotage I could not tell, but it made no matter. The very fact that he had armed himself today, the day of Finch’s arrival, was proof enough of his intent.

I staggered out of the shed, my mind a blank turmoil of fear and despair. What was I to do? To race to the station would be useless; I would miss them on the road. To confront Stapleton now, empty-handed, would be to invite my own destruction. My clear duty, as ever, was to Sir Henry, and now to this poor, unsuspecting Mr. Finch as well. I had to get between them and the danger.

I ran, not towards the station road, but up the steep slope of the tor behind me, the same hill from which I had witnessed that strange interview between Sir Henry and Miss Stapleton. From its rocky crest, I could command a view of the entire district. The quarry-scarred slopes fell away below me, and the white ribbon of the road to the station was visible for a considerable distance. I scanned it frantically. There, in the distance, was the trap, a small dark shape moving slowly towards me. I could just make out two figures within it. They were safe, for the moment.

My eyes swept the moor, searching for any sign of the man I hunted. At first, I saw nothing but the desolate, rolling expanse of brown and green. Then, a flicker of movement caught my eye. It was down in the valley, near the treacherous ground of the Grimpen Mire. A man was moving through the reeds, his head low, his progress furtive and swift. Even at that distance, I recognised the lean figure and the distinctive tweed suit. It was Stapleton. He was not carrying his butterfly-net. In his hands was the long, canvas-wrapped parcel.

He was heading for a ridge of low, granite-strewn hills that overlooked the road from a different angle, a place where the track narrowed as it passed between two steep banks. It was a perfect sniper’s nest, offering a clear shot at the approaching carriage while keeping the rifleman entirely concealed from his targets. My blood ran cold. He had chosen his ground with a strategist’s eye. From my own vantage point, I could see both hunter and prey, but they could not see each other.

I had to act. I had to create a diversion, to change their path. I scrambled down the tor, my feet slipping on the wet rock, and ran along the moor path, my plan forming out of the sheer desperation of the moment. I burst out onto the road just as the trap came rattling around a bend.

“Sir Henry!” I cried, waving my arms frantically.

He pulled up the horse, his face a picture of astonishment. By his side sat a small, neat-looking man with a pale, scholarly face and spectacles, who regarded me with mild curiosity. This, I presumed, was Finch.

“Good heavens, Watson, what is it? You look as if you’ve seen the Hound itself!” Sir Henry exclaimed.

“A change of plan!” I panted, trying to keep my voice steady. “Do not take the main road back. I have just remembered, there is a path this way, across the moor. It is a little rough, but it passes by the prehistoric village. I thought Mr. Finch, with his interest in antiquities, might wish to see it. It is one of the most remarkable sites in the country.”

It was a flimsy pretext, and I saw a flicker of doubt in Sir Henry’s eyes. Mr. Finch, however, brightened at once.

“The neolithic settlement? Capital!” he said, his voice precise and reedy. “I have read a paper on it by Micklethwaite. I should be delighted to inspect the hut circles. An excellent suggestion, Doctor.”

Sir Henry shrugged, his good nature winning out over his surprise. “As you wish, then. If you think the trap can manage it, Watson. Lead the way.”

I breathed an inward sigh of relief that was almost a prayer. I had diverted them. I led them off the main road and onto a rough track that wound its way into the heart of the moor. We were now out of Stapleton’s line of sight, but I knew the danger was not past. He would realise his quarry had vanished, and he would come looking for them. My diversion had bought us time, nothing more. As we bumped and jolted along the path, with Mr. Finch chattering happily about beaker folk and flint tools, my mind was racing. My only hope was that my sabotage of the rifle had been successful. If it had, then Stapleton was armed with nothing more than a useless piece of metal. But if he had repaired it, or if this was another weapon entirely, then I had merely led my friends into a different corner of the same slaughterhouse.

The path grew steeper, and eventually Sir Henry pulled the horse to a halt. “We’ll have to proceed on foot from here,” he said. “The village is just over this rise.”

We dismounted, and I made sure to position myself as close to the two men as I could. The mist had begun to close in again, reducing our visibility to a few hundred yards. The grey, ghostly shapes of the ancient stone huts rose up around us. It was a place of profound and melancholy silence.

“Remarkable,” whispered Mr. Finch, peering at a low stone doorway. “Truly remarkable. To think that men lived and died here, thousands of years ago.”

As he spoke, my ear, preternaturally sharpened by the tension, caught a sound. It was the scrape of a boot on stone, from somewhere above us on the slope.

“Did you hear that?” I said, my voice low.

Sir Henry froze, his head cocked. “I heard nothing.”

“A sheep, perhaps,” suggested Mr. Finch, still engrossed by the ruins.

But I knew it was no sheep. I looked up towards the crest of the hill, a jagged line of granite tors breaking through the turf. A figure was standing there, silhouetted for a moment against the grey sky. It was Stapleton. The long shape of the rifle was clear in his hands. He had found us.

“Down!” I yelled, and threw myself at Sir Henry, tackling him to the ground with all my strength. We landed in a heap in the wet heather. Mr. Finch gave a squeak of alarm and stumbled back, his spectacles flying from his nose.

We lay there for a second that stretched into an eternity, waiting for the crack of the shot and the blow that would follow. The silence of the moor pressed in. Nothing. There was no report, no whistling bullet.

I raised my head cautiously. Stapleton was still there, a statue of black fury upon the hilltop. He was working frantically at the rifle, his movements jerky and enraged. I saw him break it open, then slam it shut. He raised it to his shoulder again, aiming directly at us.

Then I heard it. A faint, dry *click*.

My heart leaped with a wild, triumphant joy. It had worked! My desperate gamble in the shed had paid off. The firing pin was too short. The hammer was falling on an empty chamber. He could pull the trigger all day, but he could not harm us.

Stapleton let out a scream of pure, animalistic rage that echoed across the desolate landscape. He raised the rifle above his head and smashed it down upon a rock. The sound of splintering wood and tortured metal was shockingly loud in the stillness. He broke it again and again, like a madman, until it was nothing but a twisted wreck. Then he turned and fled, disappearing into the swirling mist like a phantom.

Slowly, shakily, we got to our feet. Sir Henry’s face was pale, but his eyes were blazing with a cold fury I had never seen in him before. Mr. Finch was scrabbling on the ground for his spectacles, his scholarly composure entirely shattered.

“Watson,” Sir Henry said, his voice trembling with emotion. “In God’s name, what was that? That was Stapleton!”

“Yes,” I said, my own legs feeling weak as water. “It was Stapleton. And he came here to murder you.”

There was no time for lengthy explanations. My first duty was to secure the evidence. “Stay here,” I commanded. “I am going to fetch the constable. Do not move from this spot.”

I left them standing amidst the ancient stones, two modern men stunned by the eruption of a savagery as old as the landscape itself, and I ran. I ran as I had never run in my life, my lungs burning, my mind alight with the single thought that the chase was not yet over. I had seen the direction in which Stapleton had fled: towards the Grimpen Mire. I knew the man’s expertise, his intimate knowledge of the safe paths across that treacherous bog. But in his blind rage and panic, he might make a mistake. He might forget the subtle landmarks, the particular tufts of grass that marked the way.

As I raced back towards the road where we had left the trap, I saw another figure hurrying towards me. It was the local constable, a stout and steady man to whom I had, on a wild hunch that morning, sent a lad with a cryptic note asking him to be near the road to the station at noon.

“Dr. Watson, sir!” he panted. “I got your message. What is happening? I heard a scream.”

“Attempted murder, Constable,” I gasped. “Stapleton. He’s fled into the Mire. We must get him.”

The two of us scrambled into the trap and I drove the horse mercilessly towards the edge of the great bog. We left the carriage and proceeded on foot, the constable now holding his service revolver in his hand. The mist was thicker here, a cold, wet blanket that clung to us and deadened all sound. We could see no more than twenty yards in any direction.

“He knows the paths, sir,” the constable said grimly. “We’ll never find him in this.”

“Listen!” I commanded.

We stood in silence, and for a moment there was nothing but the sighing of the wind through the reeds. And then we heard it. A cry. A terrible, high-pitched scream of utter terror, cut short horribly. It came from somewhere out in the white blankness ahead of us.

We plunged forward, following the sound. We came to one of the firm, green paths that Stapleton himself had once shown to me. There, on the path, was a single, muddy boot. It had been pulled clean off a foot. And beyond it, the surface of the bog was disturbed. A patch of viscous green and black ooze was still shuddering, and a few last, sickly bubbles rose to the surface and popped. And then there was nothing but the vast, implacable silence of the Mire.

We stood and stared, our own faces pale in the gloom. There could be no doubt as to the fate of the man who had worn that boot. In his blind flight, the master of the moor had made his first and last mistake. The bog, which he had planned to use as the final resting place for his victims, had claimed him instead. The darkness that had haunted the Baskerville family for so long had been swallowed up by a darkness far older and more profound. The naturalist had become one with his collection.

for a long minute the constable and I remained as if turned to stone, our eyes fixed upon that dreadful, simmering patch of bog. The silence that followed the scream was more terrible than the sound itself, a profound and final stillness that spoke of an end to all things. The chill of the moor seemed to penetrate to my very bones, a cold that had nothing to do with the clinging mist. It was the cold of a tomb.

The constable was the first to recover. He let out a long, shuddering breath and pocketed his revolver with a shaking hand. “God have mercy on his soul,” he muttered, though his tone suggested he thought the man unworthy of any such mercy.

“He has met the fate he designed for Sir Henry,” I said, my voice sounding hollow and strange in my own ears. “The moor has claimed its own. There is nothing more we can do here.”

We turned from that grim spot, leaving the single boot as a lonely, pathetic monument to the man’s demise. The journey back to the trap was a somber and silent one, each of us lost in our own thoughts. My own mind was a whirlwind of exhaustion and relief. The long vigil was over. The shadow that had hung over the house of Baskerville, a shadow that had darkened for generations, had at last been lifted. Yet the manner of its lifting left me with a deep sense of horror. To have witnessed a man’s end, even a villain’s, in so ghastly a fashion is not a thing one easily puts aside.

As we neared the great pile of Baskerville Hall, its windows now beginning to glow with lamplight against the gathering dusk, my thoughts turned to those within. Sir Henry, his face a mask of anxious fury after the assault on his person, and the lady, Mrs. Stapleton, or rather, as we now knew her to be, Mrs. Baskerville. What state must she be in? I had left her in a dead faint, her system finally succumbing to the years of terror and the acute shock of the final, brutal confrontation.

We left the trap with a stable boy and I led the constable through the great front doors. The scene within was one of hushed anxiety. Barrymore, his face pale and strained, met us in the hall.

“Dr. Watson, sir! Thank God you are returned. Is there any news?” he whispered.

“The danger is past, Barrymore,” I said grimly. “Stapleton will trouble no one again.”

At that moment, Sir Henry himself emerged from the dining room, a glass of brandy clutched in his hand. His face was flushed, but his eyes were clear and full of a burning question.

“Watson! What has happened? Where is he? Did you get the blackguard?”

“He fled into the Grimpen Mire, Sir Henry,” I said, choosing my words with care. “He knew the paths, but in his haste… he made an error. The Mire has… taken him.”

Sir Henry stared at me, the meaning of my words slowly dawning on his features. The anger in his face slowly drained away, replaced by a look of profound, solemn relief. He sank into a nearby chair and buried his face in his hands.

“Gone,” he murmured. “Then it is truly over.”

“It is over,” I confirmed. “And the lady? How is she?”

“Mrs. Barrymore has taken her to her room,” he said, looking up. “She recovered from her faint only to fall into a fit of weeping. The poor, dear girl. What she must have endured. “You could not have known, Sir Henry. None of us could. She acted with immense bravery.”

It was at that moment, as a sense of quiet finality began to settle upon the house, that the great oak door swung open once more. Framed in the doorway, silhouetted against the dark moor, stood a tall, lean figure. He was cloaked and capped, and the smoke from a briar pipe curled about his sharp, hawk-like features.

“An excellent summary, Watson, though you neglect the final, most pressing detail,” said the cool, incisive voice of Mr. Sherlock Holmes.

I sprang to my feet with a cry of astonishment. “Holmes! Where in heaven’s name have you come from? I thought you were in London!”

“A common misconception, which I encouraged,” he said, stepping into the hall and shaking the damp from his coat. “I have been occupying my leisure time in a small cottage in Coombe Tracey, taking a rather more direct interest in this fascinating little problem than you imagined. Sir Henry, utterly bewildered, could only stammer a greeting.

“But Holmes, how did you know to come here, now?” I demanded.

“I have been observing Merripit House for the better part of two days. I saw you and the constable give chase, Watson. A most commendable effort. When I saw Stapleton make for the Mire, I knew his race was run. He was a master of its secrets, but panic is a poor navigator. Rather than join you in your grim vigil, I made my way to the station at Princetown, to ensure no accomplice could make an escape by the last train. Finding all quiet, I presumed the matter had concluded as I expected, and came here to offer my congratulations. And,” he added, his eyes glinting, “to tie up one remaining loose end.”

“A loose end?” I asked, for I thought everything had been concluded in the most definite manner possible.

“Indeed, Watson. The instrument of the crime. The hound.”

A fresh chill ran through me. In the drama of the chase and Stapleton’s demise, I had completely forgotten the monstrous creature. “My God, Holmes, you are right! It must still be at Merripit House. Penned up in that outbuilding.”

“Precisely,” said Holmes, knocking the ash from his pipe. “And a starved beast of that size and ferocity is a danger to the entire moor. We cannot rest until it is dealt with. Sir Henry, I should be obliged if you would remain here and see to the welfare of the lady. Watson, the constable and I have one last piece of business to attend to. Constable, have you the stomach for it?”

The constable, who had been staring at my friend as if he were an apparition, straightened up and saluted. “Aye, sir. I’m with you.”

And so, within half an hour of our return, we were once more out on the dark road, this time heading for the lonely and now sinister precincts of Merripit House. Holmes had brought with him a powerful lantern, and its bright beam cut a tunnel through the oppressive darkness. The house itself was black and silent, a dead thing upon the moor.

“Stapleton kept it in the small stone outhouse behind the main building,” I explained in a low voice. “I saw him there once before.”

“Then that is where we shall go,” Holmes said calmly, and I marvelled, not for the first time, at the man’s iron nerve. He was armed only with his riding crop and the service revolver he had taken from his travelling bag, yet he walked towards the lair of a monster as if he were going to his morning breakfast.

We made our way around the silent house, our footsteps crunching on the gravel path. The outhouse was a small, grim-looking structure with a heavy, bolted door. A low sound from within, a deep, guttural whining, told us that our quarry was at home.

“He is hungry,” Holmes murmured. “That may make him careless, but it will also make him desperate. Constable, stand by the door. Watson, keep the lantern beam fixed upon the entrance. Do not move, whatever you see.”

With a steady hand, Holmes drew his revolver. Then, with a sudden, swift movement, he reached out and slid back the heavy iron bolt. He pulled the door open a few inches and peered into the darkness within.

For a moment, there was silence. Then came a scrabbling sound, and a low, menacing growl that vibrated through the very stones of the building.

“Come out, then,” Holmes said, his voice as calm as a physician’s. “Let us have a look at you.”

There was a terrific snarling bark, and then the creature burst forth. It was a sight to freeze the blood. Even in my memory, I cannot be sure of its full size, for it seemed to my terrified eyes to be as large as a small lioness. It was a cross, as Holmes later surmised, of a mastiff and a bloodhound, gaunt and skeletal from its confinement, but its very leanness made it look all the more terrifying. But it was the head that held the true horror. Around its muzzle and across its eyes was smeared a ghastly, shimmering substance. In the beam of the lantern, it glowed with a hellish, spectral light, a foul green-blue incandescence. The creature’s eyes burned like hot coals, and its great jaws, outlined in that devilish fire, dripped with slaver.

It gathered itself for a spring, its target clear. But it had not accounted for Sherlock Holmes. Before the beast could launch itself, there came two sharp cracks from Holmes’s revolver. The reports echoed like cannon fire in the night. The great hound gave a dreadful, agonized howl, leaped a few feet into the air, and then crashed to the ground, where it lay twitching and then, at last, was still.

We stood in silence for a moment, the acrid smell of cordite mixing with the damp night air. The glowing phosphorus on the dead hound’s head was already beginning to fade, and with it, the last vestige of the supernatural terror that had plagued this land.

“The hound of the Baskervilles,” Holmes said quietly, breaking the spell. He nudged the great, inert form with the toe of his boot. “A formidable beast. A pity to have to destroy it, but I see no other choice. That, gentlemen, is the end of the legend.”

With the final threat removed, we turned our attention to the house itself. Using a key that Holmes, with his typical foresight, had procured from Stapleton’s effects which had been left at the station, we let ourselves in. The interior was just as I remembered it: a curious and unsettling mixture of a naturalist’s laboratory and a comfortable gentleman’s residence. Butterfly cases lined the walls, and specimen jars sat beside fine china on the mantelpiece.

“Now,” said Holmes, his voice brisk and businesslike once more, “let us see what our late friend has left behind. The full story is always in the details, Watson.”

He led us to the small study at the back of the house. It was here that the true mind of the man was laid bare. Upon a large desk, held down by a fossil ammonite, was a genealogical chart. Holmes held it up to the light.

“As I suspected,” he said. “Behold, the motive. See here, Rodger Baskerville, the younger brother of Sir Hugo, who fled to South America. It was thought he died childless. He did not. He married, had a son. This son, in turn, had a son, who took the name Vandeleur, and then Stapleton. Our naturalist was a Baskerville, and with Sir Charles and Sir Henry out of the way, he would have had a clear, if complicated, claim to the entire estate.”

Beside the chart was a tin box. Holmes prised it open with the tip of a penknife. Inside was a sticky, luminous paste.

“And here, the method,” he continued, holding it up. “A preparation of phosphorus. Not toxic, but fearsome to behold on a dark night. The cunning of the man was sublime. He used the legend to his own ends, creating a spectral hound that would frighten a man with a weak heart to his death, and which, even if seen by others, would be dismissed as a local superstition.”

He moved about the room, his eyes darting everywhere, missing nothing. He pulled a shipping schedule from a drawer. “A ticket booked for two, from Liverpool to Costa Rica, for the end of the month. He intended to dispose of Sir Henry, claim his bride, and then, after securing the fortune, vanish back to the land from which he came.”

“But his wife, Holmes?” I asked. “She was his prisoner, but she would surely have exposed him.”

“Would she, Watson? Consider her position. She was a beautiful woman, but a foreigner with no friends or family in England, save for the man who was both her husband and her tormentor. He had complete power over her. He had convinced the world she was his sister. Had his plan succeeded, he would have forced her to go along with him. Her choice would have been between a new life of immense wealth, albeit with a murderer, or being implicated in the crime herself and ending her days in a prison or an asylum. It is a terrible choice to force upon anyone.”

We made a final tour of the silent, grim house, and then, locking the door on its dark secrets, we returned to Baskerville Hall.

The following day brought with it a sense of profound peace. The sun shone, and the moor, stripped of its shadows, looked almost benign. An inquest was held, at which Holmes provided a masterful, though carefully edited, account of events, attributing Stapleton’s madness and subsequent demise to the pressures of his lonely life and obsessive studies. The legend of the hound was quietly laid to rest as a product of local superstition, cleverly exploited by a disturbed mind.

The lady of the house, Beryl Baskerville, remained in her rooms for several days, attended by Mrs. Barrymore. Sir Henry, his natural ebullience slowly returning, treated her with a gentle, brotherly reverence that was deeply touching to see. He had offered to see that she was safely returned to her family in Costa Rica, with a generous portion of the fortune her husband had tried to steal, to ensure her future comfort.

On our final evening at the Hall, Holmes and I sat with the baronet before a roaring fire, much as we did in our rooms in Baker Street.

“I can never repay you, Mr. Holmes,” Sir Henry said, his voice thick with emotion. “You and Watson have saved not only my life, but my family’s honour. The name of Baskerville can now stand in the sunlight again.”

“Your gratitude is payment enough, Sir Henry,” Holmes replied. “As for Watson, he has the material for another of his sensational tales. I suspect the public will be most pleased. Just try, my dear fellow, to stick to the facts as much as your romantic nature will allow.”

I smiled, for I knew this was as close to a compliment as I was ever likely to receive. The case was over. The darkness had been confronted and vanquished. And as I looked from the steadfast face of Sir Henry to the sharp, animated profile of my friend, I felt a deep sense of satisfaction. We had walked into the heart of a very old and very dark legend, and we had emerged, once more, into the light.

“So you are not to see her again?” I asked, after Sir Henry had relayed to us this most singular of resolutions.

“That is the heart of the matter,” he replied, his face a mask of frustration. “He has given his consent, if you can call it that, but upon a condition of such a nature that it is no consent at all. He claims it is to allow her to recover from the shock of her arrival in a new country and to give me time to consider my own heart. A more preposterous arrangement I have never heard!”

“And what was your reply?” asked Holmes, who had been listening with the most profound attention, his long fingers steepled before him.

“What could I say? The man held all the cards. I told him that if those were his terms, I had no choice but to accept them, though I made it clear I did so under protest. He spoke of his deep affection for his sister and his fears for her in a strange land. It was all very plausible, Watson, and yet I have the strongest feeling that I am being played for a fool.”

“I should not be at all surprised,” Holmes remarked quietly, his grey eyes fixed upon the glowing embers in the hearth. “There is a deep game being played upon this moor, Sir Henry, and we are yet only upon its fringes. This three month stipulation is a very curious thing. It is not the action of a protective brother; it is a strategic delay. He needs time. The question is, for what?”

Holmes’s words, which at the time seemed merely a characteristic expression of his own suspicious nature, were to prove more prophetic than any of us could have imagined. The days that followed were marked by a strange and uneasy calm. Sir Henry, true to his word, made no attempt to contact Miss Stapleton. He threw himself into the business of his estate, poring over maps and ledgers with a feverish energy that I knew was designed only to distract him from the ache in his heart. For my own part, I continued my walks upon the moor, yet its grim face now seemed to hold a more personal and immediate menace. Every tor and every mire seemed to watch me, and I could not shake the feeling that Stapleton’s butterfly-net was but a cover for some darker pursuit.

It was upon the fourth day after the agreement that the placid surface of our lives was once more disturbed. We were at breakfast when Barrymore entered with a telegram upon his salver. It was for Sir Henry, who tore it open with a look of mild curiosity that quickly contorted into one of utter astonishment.

“Good heavens!” he cried, dropping the flimsy paper upon the table. “What new devilry is this?”

Holmes picked it up and read it aloud, his voice devoid of emotion. “‘Arriving on the morrow to collect what is mine. Do not interfere. Garcia.’”

“Garcia?” I echoed. “Who is this Garcia?”

Sir Henry shook his head, his face pale. “I have no idea. The name means nothing to me. But the tone of it! ‘To collect what is mine.’ It sounds like a threat, Holmes.”

“It is more than a threat, Sir Henry; it is a claim,” said Holmes, his eyes gleaming with a light I had come to recognise. The hunt was on once more. “This is a new player, and one who does not feel the need for subtlety. The telegram was sent from Plymouth this morning. He will be here by tomorrow. The question we must ask ourselves is not only who he is, but what, or whom, he has come to claim.”

My own thoughts flew at once to the Stapletons. Could this be some associate from their past? Before I could voice my conjecture, Holmes was on his feet.

“There is no time to waste in speculation, Watson. We must have facts. I shall require your assistance. We will pay a visit to the telegraph office in Grimpen. The clerk there, a man named Abernathy, owes me a small favour. We must learn everything we can about the sender of this message.”

Within the hour we were rattling along in a pony-cart, the crisp moorland air doing little to cool the fire of Holmes’s intellectual curiosity. Mr Abernathy, a small, nervous man with spectacles perched on his nose, was indeed most accommodating when he saw my companion. After a brief, quiet word from Holmes, he became a fount of information.

“The gentleman, sir? Oh yes, I remember him,” the clerk chirped. “A foreign sort of fellow. Very dark, with a black moustache and the most piercing eyes. He spoke good English, but with a Spanish lilt to it. He was most particular about the wording, and he paid in gold sovereigns, which is a rarity these days.”

“Did you observe anything else about him, Abernathy? His manner? His dress?” Holmes pressed.

“He seemed impatient, sir. Agitated, you might say. Kept looking over his shoulder as if he were expecting someone. As for his dress, it was good quality, but looked as though it had been slept in. He carried a small, leather valise, and I noted a scar, a thin white line, running down his left cheek from his temple to his jaw.”

“Excellent, Abernathy. You have a good eye for detail,” said Holmes, slipping a coin into the man’s hand. “You have been most helpful.”

As we drove back towards Baskerville Hall, Holmes was uncharacteristically silent, his brow furrowed in thought.

“Well, Holmes?” I ventured at last. “What do you make of it?”

“I make of it, my dear Watson, that a vulture is circling. This Garcia, as he calls himself, is no stranger to the Stapletons. The description, the impatience, the Spanish connection, it all points to South America, the very place from which Beryl Stapleton and her supposed brother have come. He has come to claim something, and I would wager my reputation that it is not a piece of lost luggage.”

His theory was to be tested sooner than we expected. Upon our return to the Hall, we found a scene of some commotion in the great entrance hall. Sir Henry stood near the fireplace, his fists clenched, while Mrs Barrymore was attempting to soothe a figure who sat huddled in a large armchair, her face buried in her hands. It was Beryl Stapleton.

“Miss Stapleton!” I exclaimed, rushing forward.

She looked up at the sound of my voice, and I was shocked by her appearance. Her face was deathly white, her eyes wide with a terror that went far beyond her previous anxieties.

“He is coming,” she whispered, her voice a dry rasp. “Mateo is coming.”

“Mateo Garcia?” Holmes asked, his voice gentle but firm as he stepped to her side.

She flinched at the name and gave a single, sharp nod.

“Who is he to you, Miss Stapleton?” Sir Henry demanded, his voice rough with a mixture of concern and jealousy. “Is he a relative? A former lover?”

“He is a devil,” she said, and a shudder ran through her entire frame. “He was my husband’s partner. In Costa Rica. Before… before we came here. My husband, Stapleton, he was known as Vandeleur then. They were involved in many things, many dark enterprises. When my husband decided to flee, he took with him not only his own share but Garcia’s as well. He left him for dead, or so he thought. Mateo swore that if he ever found him, he would have his revenge, and his money.”

A terrible understanding began to dawn upon me. “And now he has read of Stapleton’s death in the papers,” I said.

“And of the wealthy baronet who was the object of his schemes,” Holmes finished grimly. “He has put two and two together and has come, as he says, to collect what is his. He believes Stapleton’s fortune is now in your possession, madam.”

“But there is no fortune!” she cried, her voice rising in desperation. “There was never any great sum. Only enough to live, to carry on the deception. What little there was, is gone. But he will not believe me! Mateo is a man of terrible violence. He will think I am hiding it, that Sir Henry has it. He will stop at nothing!”

At that moment, the great oak door of the Hall was pushed open, and Barrymore appeared, his face ashen.

“Sir Henry,” the butler stammered. “There is a gentleman to see you. A Mr Garcia.”

A collective gasp went through the room. We had thought we had a day’s grace, but our man had wasted no time.

“Show him in, Barrymore,” said Holmes, taking command of the situation with a quiet authority. He turned to Beryl. “Madam, it is imperative that you compose yourself. Your fear will only confirm his suspicions. Go with Mrs Barrymore to your room and remain there. Watson, stand by the bell-pull. Sir Henry, I must ask you to allow me to handle this interview.”

Sir Henry, though clearly chafing at being sidelined, gave a curt nod of agreement.

The man who entered was precisely as the telegraph clerk had described him. He was of medium height, but with a wiry strength that was apparent in his every movement. His face was lean and tanned, dominated by a pair of restless, obsidian eyes and a neatly trimmed black moustache. The white scar on his cheek was a livid mark against his dark skin. He held a wide-brimmed hat in his hands, and his gaze swept the room, taking in each of us before it settled upon the empty chair where Beryl had sat.

“I was told I would find a lady here,” he said, his voice a low, musical baritone with the promised Spanish accent. “The widow of my late associate, Mr Vandeleur.”

“The lady is indisposed,” said Holmes, his voice cool and level. “And you, I take it, are Mr Garcia.”

“I am,” the man replied, his eyes narrowing as he studied Holmes. “And you are?”

“My name is Sherlock Holmes. This is my friend, Dr Watson, and this is Sir Henry Baskerville, the master of this house. May we inquire as to the nature of your business?”

A slow, un-prepossessing smile spread across Garcia’s lips, a smile that did not reach his eyes. “My business is simple. I have travelled a very long way. My partner, who called himself Stapleton here, I believe, owed me a considerable sum of money. He met an unfortunate end before he could repay his debt. I have come to collect it from his widow.”

“I am afraid you are misinformed,” said Sir Henry, unable to contain himself any longer. “Mrs Stapleton has no money. Her husband left her destitute.”

Garcia’s smile vanished. “Do not play me for a fool, baronet. Vandeleur was not a man to be destitute. He fled Costa Rica with enough to make him a king. He came here to steal your fortune as well. I have read the accounts. He is dead, and the law says his property passes to his wife. So, I will ask again. Where is the money?”

“There is no money,” Holmes repeated calmly. “Stapleton, or Vandeleur, was a man of grandiose schemes and little actual substance. He lived upon credit and the terror he inspired. The entire enterprise was a hollow shell. Your journey has been for nothing.”

Garcia took a step forward, his hand clenching the brim of his hat until his knuckles were white. “A hollow shell? Do you think I am a child? Vandeleur would not cross the street without a profit! He had plans, always plans. He spoke of a hiding place, a place for his treasures where no one could find them. The lady will know.”

He took a menacing step towards the door through which Beryl had departed. Sir Henry moved instantly to block his path.

“You will not go near her,” the baronet growled, his whole body tense.

For a moment I thought violence was inevitable. The two men stood face to face, the bluff English nobleman and the sinewy South American predator. Garcia’s eyes blazed with a dangerous fire, and I saw his hand twitch, as if reaching for a weapon that was no longer there.

It was Holmes who broke the spell. “This is getting us nowhere,” he said, stepping between them. “Mr Garcia, let us be reasonable. You believe there is a hidden treasure. We assure you there is not. There is a simple way to resolve this.” He turned his gaze upon the newcomer, and his voice dropped to a conspiratorial whisper. “I happen to know something of Stapleton’s affairs. I know, for instance, that he was obsessed with the history of this place, with the legends of the old family.”

Garcia’s eyes flickered with interest. “What of it?”

“I believe,” Holmes continued, choosing his words with care, “that if such a treasure exists, it is not in this house. Stapleton was too clever for that. He would have hidden it where he felt most secure, in the place where he conducted his true work. He would have hidden it upon the moor.”

A silence fell upon the room. The idea was so audacious, and yet so perfectly in keeping with what we knew of Stap.eton’s cunning, that it held a certain mad logic. I saw Sir Henry’s expression change from anger to confusion, and even Garcia seemed to be considering the possibility.

“The moor?” Garcia said slowly. “It is a vast place.”

“But not without its landmarks,” Holmes countered smoothly. “Stapleton had his favourite haunts. There was the black mire, of course, where he kept his hound. And there was the old quarry, a place of stone huts where the ancient folk lived. A man who trusts no one might well trust the earth to keep his secrets.”

I watched Holmes’s face, trying to discern his purpose. Was this some elaborate bluff to get Garcia out of the house? Or did he truly believe what he was saying?

Garcia stood for a long moment, his dark eyes shifting from Holmes to Sir Henry and back again. He was a man cornered, and Holmes had offered him a new, tantalising path.

“Very well,” he said at last. “I will look. But do not think this is over. If I find nothing, I will return. And when I do,” he fixed his gaze on Sir Henry, “I will not be so polite. The lady will tell me what I wish to know, one way or another.”

With that final threat hanging in the air, he turned on his heel and strode out of the hall, slamming the great door behind him.

The moment he was gone, Sir Henry turned on Holmes. “What in God’s name have you done, Holmes? You’ve sent him out onto the moor! You’ve given him reason to believe this fantasy! He will tear the place apart and then he will be back, more angry than before!”

“Precisely,” said Holmes, with a grim satisfaction that I found utterly chilling. “And this time, we will be ready for him. Watson, I fancy another walk upon the moor is in order. But this time, I suggest you bring your service revolver. I have a strong suspicion that Mr Garcia did not come to England merely to dig for treasure, and that his business is not yet concluded.”

Sir Henry, however, was not so easily convinced. He paced the length of the great hall, his boots echoing upon the flagstones like the tolling of a sullen bell.

“Ready for him?” he cried, throwing his hands up in exasperation. “Holmes, the man is a brute and a desperado! He threatened my wife! And you have sent him off to brood upon his grievances and work himself into a greater fury. When he returns, empty handed and cheated, he will be like a rabid wolf, and it is we who have barred the door to his den!”

“That is a most apt, if dramatic, simile,” Holmes replied, unperturbed. He had moved to the great fireplace and was tamping fresh tobacco into his well worn briar pipe. The action was so calm, so methodical, that it seemed a deliberate counterpoint to the baronet’s agitation. “A wolf, you say? Very well. Then we shall not bar the door; we shall, on the contrary, leave it invitingly ajar and place a fine joint of meat just inside. The wolf will enter, intent upon his prize, and that is when the steel jaws of the trap will close.”

“I wish you would speak plainly,” Sir Henry grumbled, though some of the heat had left his voice. He stopped his pacing and looked to my friend, his brow still furrowed with anxiety.

“The plainest speech, Sir Henry, is that Garcia is not after treasure,” Holmes said, striking a match and holding it to his pipe, his sharp features illuminated in the brief flare. “Or rather, the treasure he seeks is not one of silver or gold. Think, man. Stapleton was a cunning and meticulous villain, but his obsession was singular: this estate and the title that accompanies it. He was not a man to indulge in side ventures. Had he possessed a great fortune, he would have used it to escape England and begin a new life, not lingered here to pursue his bloody scheme. No, Garcia is a fool, but he is a dangerous fool, and he has come for something else entirely.”

I confess I was as much in the dark as Sir Henry. “But what else could there be, Holmes?” I asked. “What could Stapleton have possessed that would bring this man across the world with threats and violence?”

Holmes drew on his pipe, his eyes half closed in that familiar way which so often preceded a startling revelation. “You will recall, Watson, that Stapleton had another life before he came to Devonshire. He was the head of a school in the north. He was also a man of some scientific repute, a recognised authority on entomology. He travelled. He corresponded with learned societies. Such a life, my dear fellow, provides a magnificent cover.”

“A cover for what?” Sir Henry demanded.

“For the collection and dissemination of secrets of a rather more sensitive nature than the mating habits of the Cyclopides,” Holmes said softly. “I have had my suspicions for some time, and I made certain enquiries by telegraph whilst you and Watson were enjoying your breakfast this morning. The replies were most illuminating. It appears our Mr Vandeleur, whom we knew as Stapleton, was suspected by certain parties in Whitehall of being in the pay of a foreign government. His sudden disappearance from his school coincided with the theft of some rather important naval schematics.”

I stared at him, aghast. The sheer audacity of the idea, the image of that butterfly-chasing naturalist as a master spy, was almost too much to credit. And yet, knowing the man’s deep and serpentine cunning, it had the undeniable ring of truth.

“Good heavens, Holmes!” I exclaimed.

“So you believe Garcia is his foreign contact?” Sir Henry asked, his voice low with dawning comprehension.

“I do. And I believe Stapleton, ever the parsimonious rogue, double crossed his employers. He took their money and kept the schematics, likely intending to sell them again to the highest bidder once he was secure in his new identity as master of Baskerville Hall. He hid them. And now Garcia, his erstwhile confederate, has come to collect. He has heard the local legends, perhaps from Stapleton himself, and has conflated this secret package with the mythical Baskerville treasure. He is a greedy, violent man, and his mind runs only on gold.”

“Then the trap…” I began.

“Is for a spy, not a treasure hunter,” Holmes finished. “We will let him search the moor. We will allow his frustration to build. And when he returns to this house, convinced that the lady holds the key to his fortune, we will be there to greet him. Not to fight him on his own brutish terms, but to expose him for what he is. An agent of a foreign power, trespassing on British soil with felonious intent. A matter for Scotland Yard, and, I should think, a very long term of penal servitude.”

Holmes’s plan, now fully illuminated, was a thing of cold and beautiful logic. He had turned Garcia’s own misconception into the bait. Sir Henry, his anger replaced by a grim resolve, nodded his assent.

“What must we do?” he asked.

“First,” said Holmes, turning towards the drawing room, “I must have a word with Lady Baskerville. Alone.”

Sir Henry hesitated, a protective instinct causing him to frown. “She has been through a great deal, Holmes. She is fragile.”

“Her fragility is a matter of perception,” Holmes said, not unkindly. “She was Stapleton’s confederate, however unwillingly, for years. She possesses a resilience you have not yet seen. It is that resilience, and the knowledge she holds, that I must now access. Do not fear, Sir Henry. I am a doctor of the mind, just as Watson is of the body. I shall be gentle.”

With that, he disappeared into the drawing room, leaving me with the baronet. We stood in silence for a time, the weight of this new and sinister development pressing down upon us. The grim legend of the hound seemed almost a simple country tale compared to this dark web of international intrigue that had been spun around Baskerville Hall.

After a quarter of an hour, Holmes emerged. His face was grave, but there was a spark of triumph in his eyes. He was followed by Lady Baskerville, who looked pale, but whose expression was no longer one of fear, but of a quiet, steely determination. She had been a pawn in her supposed brother’s games for too long, and I saw in her a woman who had finally resolved to play a hand of her own.

“Lady Baskerville has confirmed my theory,” Holmes announced. “Stapleton often spoke of his ‘real treasure,’ and how he had hidden it in ‘the heart of the old world.’ He was a theatrical villain to the last. She has also provided a most crucial detail. Garcia is not his name. It is a pseudonym. His real name is Colonel Sebastian Moran.”

The name struck me like a physical blow. Moran! The second most dangerous man in London, the confederate of the late Professor Moriarty, the man whose nerve of steel and tigerish ferocity were the stuff of whispered legend at Scotland Yard. The man Holmes himself had brought to justice years ago.

“Moran?” I gasped. “But he was imprisoned!”

“He was released some eighteen months ago for good behaviour,” Holmes said with a wry smile. “A testament to his skills as an actor. It appears he has wasted no time in returning to his old trade. This is a far more dangerous game than I had anticipated. Moran is not a man to be trifled with. He is a dead shot and utterly without conscience.”

“All the more reason to be prepared,” Sir Henry said, his hand straying unconsciously to a cabinet where I knew he kept a pair of hunting rifles.

“Indeed,” Holmes agreed. “Watson, your revolver will be essential. Sir Henry, you will arm yourself as you see fit. We will not wait for him here. The stage must be set. He believes the secret lies upon the moor; therefore, it is upon the moor that we shall have our final act. The old stone huts, in the quarry you mentioned, Watson. They will provide the cover we need. Moran will seek them out. Stapleton’s clue, ‘the heart of the old world,’ points to them directly. We will be there before him.”

An hour later, as the last of the sullen daylight bled from the sky, the three of us set out from Baskerville Hall. We were cloaked and armed, three grim figures moving into the growing dusk. The air was cold and carried the damp, earthy scent of the moor. A thin mist was already rising from the hollows, wreathing the tors in ghostly shrouds and promising a night of poor visibility.

Our progress was slow and cautious. The path was treacherous in the fading light, and the silence of the great wilderness was broken only by the squelch of our boots in the damp peat and the mournful cry of a curlew. I could not help but feel a prickle of the old terror that this land inspired. It was a place of shadows and secrets, a fitting theatre for the dark drama that was about to unfold.

We reached the quarry as the last vestiges of twilight gave way to a profound and starless dark. The jagged black scar in the hillside gaped before us, and within its basin stood the low, circular ruins of the Neolithic huts. It was here, amongst the dwellings of men who had lived and died thousands of years ago, that Holmes had chosen to lay his trap for one of the most modern and sophisticated criminals of our age. The irony was not lost on me.

“He will come to the largest of the huts,” Holmes whispered, his voice barely disturbing the profound silence. “It is the most central, the most obvious. Sir Henry, you and Watson will conceal yourselves in the ruin directly opposite. There is a broken wall that will afford you a clear line of sight. Do not, under any circumstances, reveal yourselves or fire unless I give the signal. Is that clear?”

Sir Henry nodded, his face a pale mask in the gloom. I gripped my service revolver, its cold, hard weight a small comfort against the creeping dread in my heart.

“And you, Holmes?” I asked. “Where will you be?”

“I,” he said, a hint of theatricality in his tone, “shall be the joint of meat in the trap. I will be inside the main hut, examining the ground with a lantern when he arrives. He will believe I am another searcher, a rival perhaps. His arrogance and his temper will do the rest.”

“Holmes, it is madness!” I protested. “To put yourself alone with that man! He will shoot you down without a second thought!”

“That is a risk I must take,” he replied calmly. “He must be caught in the act, Watson. We need his confession, or at least an action so unambiguously criminal that no court could deny it. A dead spy is a mere diplomatic incident; a captured one is a triumph for British justice. Now, take your positions. The overture is complete. The curtain is about to rise.”

He gave my shoulder a reassuring squeeze and melted away into the darkness, a shadow among shadows. Sir Henry and I found our assigned position, crouching behind the damp, moss covered stones of a collapsed wall. From our hiding place, we had a perfect, if unnerving, view of the entrance to the largest hut, some thirty yards distant.

And then, the waiting began. It was the longest and most trying vigil of my life. The cold of the stone seeped into my bones, and the mist grew thicker, swirling around us in dense, spectral waves. Every rustle of the wind in the heather, every distant night cry of a moorland creature, sent a jolt through my nerves. My imagination peopled the fog with a dozen phantom Morans, and I found myself starting at shadows, my finger tightening on the trigger of my revolver. Beside me, Sir Henry was as still and silent as the ancient stones themselves, a statue of grim expectation.

I do not know how long we waited. It may have been one hour or it may have been three. Time on that accursed moor seemed to stretch and distort, each minute an eternity of suspense. Then, through the shifting veils of fog, we saw it: a tiny, flickering point of light. It was a lantern, moving cautiously across the broken ground at the far side of the quarry.

“He is here,” Sir Henry breathed, his voice a harsh rasp.

The light advanced steadily, bobbing and weaving as its carrier negotiated the treacherous terrain. It was heading directly for the cluster of huts. My heart hammered against my ribs. In a few moments, Moran would walk into Holmes’s trap. The light grew larger, brighter, and I could just make out the dark shape of a man behind it. He moved with a kind of predatory confidence that spoke of long experience in such nocturnal ventures.

He reached the central hut and paused, holding his lantern high. The yellow light cast a circle upon the ancient stones, revealing the interior: a floor of bare earth and walls of rough hewn granite. And there, in the centre of the light, kneeling upon the ground as if searching for something, was the tall, thin figure of my friend, Sherlock Holmes.

From the darkness beyond the lantern’s glow, a voice spoke, a voice as cold and sharp as splintered ice.

“You are energetic in your search. Have you found our little nest egg?”

Holmes rose slowly to his feet, turning to face the newcomer. He shielded his eyes from the glare of the other man’s lantern. “I fear you have the advantage of me, sir,” he said, his voice calm and even.

“I have the advantage of most men,” the voice sneered. And the figure stepped into the light.

He was a tall, powerfully built man with a sunburnt face and a bristling, iron grey moustache. But it was his eyes that held me transfixed, even from that distance. They were pale, cold, and utterly devoid of mercy, the eyes of a hunter who has cornered his prey. It was, without a doubt, Colonel Sebastian Moran. In his right hand, he held a large calibre pistol, and it was levelled squarely at Sherlock Holmes’s chest.

“I know you,” Moran said slowly, a look of dawning, murderous recognition spreading across his features. “I know you. Holmes. The meddling detective. So, you survived the Reichenbach Falls after all. I should have made sure of the matter myself.”

“A common failing amongst the criminal classes, Colonel,” Holmes replied, his tone conversational, as if they were discussing the weather in a Pall Mall club. “A distinct lack of thoroughness. Now, if you will be so good as to lower that firearm, we might discuss the matter of Stapleton’s legacy like civilised gentlemen.”

“Civilised?” Moran let out a short, brutal laugh. “There is nothing civilised about what I am about to do. I have come a very long way for those schematics. Stapleton cheated me, and now you stand in my way. The world believes you dead once already. This time, I shall make it a reality.”

My own pistol was raised, its sights fixed on the dark shape of the Colonel. My finger was taut on the trigger. Beside me, I heard the click of the hammer on Sir Henry’s rifle. The signal, I prayed, let Holmes give the signal!

But Holmes made no move. He simply stood there, a faint, mocking smile on his lips. “You are mistaken, Colonel,” he said. “I am not standing in your way. In fact, I am the one who can give you exactly what you desire.”

Moran hesitated, his predatory eyes narrowing. “What devil’s game are you playing now, Holmes?”

“No game,” said Holmes. “A simple transaction. The schematics are not here. Stapleton was a man of immense ego. He would not hide a prize of such value under a common stone. He converted them. Transformed them into a thing of beauty, something he could admire in plain sight.”

He reached into the deep pocket of his greatcoat. Moran’s pistol jerked upwards. “No tricks, Holmes!”

“Only a demonstration,” my friend said smoothly, and his hand emerged. It did not hold a weapon. It held a small, black, velvet lined box of the sort used by lepidopterists to display their finest specimens. He opened the lid.

Even from my hiding place, I could see what lay within. Pinned to the velvet was a butterfly of extraordinary size and beauty. Its wings were a dazzling pattern of iridescent blue and black, a creature of almost supernatural loveliness.

“The Gloria Scott, I believe it is called,” Holmes said, his voice ringing with a strange note of triumph. “A unique subspecies discovered by Stapleton in the jungles of South America. For a long second, there was an absolute silence, broken only by the sigh of the wind. Moran took a step forward, his pistol lowering slightly as he stared, mesmerised, at the impossible object in Holmes’s hand. Greed, astonishment, and suspicion warred on his face.

And in that instant of hesitation, Holmes gave the signal. It was not a word, but a sharp, sudden whistle.

At once, the night erupted. From behind another of the stone huts, the dark figures of two, no, three, uniformed constables rose up, lanterns flashing, their voices crying out, “In the name of the law!”

Moran spun around, snarling like a cornered tiger. He fired his pistol, not at Holmes, but into the darkness where the policemen had appeared. A flash of orange, a deafening report that echoed across the quarry. Then he turned to flee.

But there was nowhere to run. Sir Henry and I were on our feet, our own weapons covering his retreat. Holmes, with a speed I would not have thought possible, had snapped the specimen box shut and lunged forward, not at Moran himself, but at his feet. The Colonel, caught off balance, stumbled over a loose stone and went crashing to the ground.

Before he could recover, the three of us were upon him. The struggle was brief but ferocious. Moran fought with the silent, desperate strength of a wild animal, but he was outnumbered. Within moments, he was subdued, his hands securely bound behind his back by one of the constables who had, blessedly, been untouched by his wild shot.

As they hauled the furious and cursing Colonel to his feet, Sherlock Holmes calmly retrieved the specimen box from the ground where it had fallen. He brushed a speck of dirt from its lid and turned to me, his eyes twinkling in the lantern light.

“You see, Watson,” he said, tucking the box safely back into his pocket. “A well executed bluff is a more potent weapon than any revolver. Our friend the Colonel was so fixated on his prize that he failed to notice the audience had already gathered for the final curtain.” He gestured to the surrounding darkness, from which Inspector Lestrade of Scotland Yard now emerged, looking immensely pleased with himself.

“A brilliant capture, Mr Holmes,” Lestrade declared, clapping Moran on the shoulder with unnecessary force. “Brilliant! When your wire arrived, I came down on the first train. To bag the Colonel himself… they’ll be talking about this at the Yard for years!”

I looked from the captured spy to the triumphant Inspector, and then to my friend. Holmes had risked everything on his knowledge of a criminal’s mind, on the belief that greed and arrogance would blind a man to the most obvious trap. Two days later, however, the problem became less fanciful. A second farmer, a tenant of Sir Henry’s named Elias Thorne, came to the Hall in a state of high agitation. He was a sturdy, weather-beaten man, not given to flights of fancy, but his face was grey with a fear that was very real indeed.

I approached with the utmost caution, keeping to the shadows of the rocks. The place was a scene of utter desolation, a jumble of granite boulders and pools of stagnant, black water. It was a device for exsanguination, a devilish piece of machinery designed for the cold, efficient draining of blood. The full, monstrous scope of the plot was laid bare between us in the silent, lamp-lit room. The family legend, the spectral hound, was to be made real. Stapleton, with his scientific knowledge and his diabolical ingenuity, was preparing to stage the ultimate, terrifying murder, a murder that would be blamed on a supernatural curse.

“We must get word to Holmes,” I said, my voice hoarse.

“There is no time for letters,” Sir Henry said grimly. “You said yourself that your next communication would be a telegram. The time has come, my friend. Send it now. Tell him to come at once. But until he arrives, the battle is ours. Staplelon knows his quarry is suspicious. He knows his time is running out. He will strike soon. But this time, Watson, we shall be ready for him.”

The die was cast, and a strange calm descended upon us, the calm which follows the acceptance of a terrible and unavoidable truth. Our course of action was decided upon with the swift and silent understanding that exists between men who have faced peril together. The telegram to Holmes was the first and most vital step.

“You must go, Watson,” Sir Henry said, his voice level and hard. “I am the bait in this trap, and I must not leave the hook. Barrymore can see you to the path, but the journey to Grimpen is yours alone.”

I looked out at the window, where the fog pressed against the panes like a living, breathing thing, a blind, white monster that had swallowed the world. To venture into its depths seemed a form of madness. The moor was treacherous enough in broad daylight; in this swirling chaos, a man might wander for hours only to find himself back where he started, or worse, to stumble into one of the mire-pits which dotted the landscape.

“I shall manage it, Sir Henry,” I said, though my heart was not so confident as my words. “I will take the lantern and follow the road. So long as I do not stray from the cart-tracks, I should be safe enough.”

“Be armed, Watson,” he insisted, going to a locked cabinet in the corner of the study. He produced a small, heavy revolver, which he handed to me. “This is no time for half-measures. Stapleton knows you have found his lair. He must suspect that his secret is out. He will not scruple to remove you from his path if he sees the chance.”

I took the weapon and checked its mechanism, the cold weight of it a grim but reassuring presence in my pocket. We summoned Barrymore and laid the whole dreadful business before him. The stout butler and his wife listened with faces of blank horror, Mrs. Barrymore stifling a sob with her apron, while her husband’s florid complexion turned to a pasty grey. He was a man of nerve, however, and the shock soon gave way to a stolid determination.

“My God, Sir Henry,” he stammered. “A machine? To do… such a thing? The man is a devil from hell itself.”

“He is a man, Barrymore, and a very clever and dangerous one,” Sir Henry corrected him. “While Dr. Watson is gone, we must secure the house. Every door, every window on the ground floor must be barred. No one enters or leaves. You and I shall keep watch.”

“Aye, sir. You may count on me,” the butler said, his jaw set. “He’ll not get past us, not while I have breath in my body.”

And so, with my revolver in my coat and a powerful lantern in my hand, I prepared to set out. Barrymore accompanied me to the great door, which he unbolted with a grating clang that echoed in the heavy silence. The moment it swung open, the fog billowed in, cold and damp, smelling of wet earth and decay. It was like stepping from a ship’s cabin into a spectral sea.

“God speed, Doctor,” Barrymore whispered, his voice hushed with awe. “Keep to the road, sir. Do not let the sounds of the moor lead you astray.”

I nodded, gave a final glance back into the warm, lighted hall where Sir Henry stood watching, a lonely and defiant figure, and then plunged into the white abyss.

The world vanished instantly. The great, dark mass of Baskerville Hall was swallowed up behind me in two paces. The lantern cast a circle of luminous yellow vapour about ten feet in diameter, and beyond that lay utter blackness. I felt as though I were the last man on earth, adrift in a formless, silent universe. I trod carefully, my boots crunching on the gravel of the drive, my senses strained to the utmost. The silence was the most unnerving part; it was a dead, muffled quiet, where even the sound of my own footsteps seemed unnaturally loud.

I found the main road by feel more than by sight, the ruts of the cart-tracks guiding my feet. My progress was painfully slow. I held the lantern low, its beam cutting a swath through the base of the fog, and shuffled forward, my eyes fixed upon that small patch of illuminated ground. To look up was to be blinded by the swirling whiteness, to become instantly disoriented. Every instinct screamed at me to turn back to the safety of the Hall, but the thought of Holmes, of his clear, incisive mind being brought to bear upon this monstrous puzzle, drove me onwards.

After what seemed an age, a sound did reach me, a sound which froze the very blood in my veins. It was a low, dreadful moaning, which seemed to come from far out on the moor to my right. It rose and fell in a mournful cadence, a cry of utter despair and agony. It was not the cry of any animal I knew, and my mind leaped at once to the spectral hound of the legend. I stood frozen, my hand gripping the revolver in my pocket, my heart hammering against my ribs. The sound came again, closer this time, and it was joined by a series of frantic, splashing noises. A pony, I realised with a surge of relief that left me weak, a pony caught in the mire. It was one of the saddest sounds of the moor, the death-cry of a trapped beast, but at that moment it was more welcome to my ears than a symphony, for it was a sound of this earth, a natural tragedy, and not the baying of a supernatural fiend.

Shaken, I hurried on, my pace quickened by a fresh spur of fear. Was it only a pony? Or was Stapleton out there in the fog, moving about his secret business, perhaps testing some new horror? The thought made the hairs on my neck prickle. Every shadow thrown by the lantern seemed to take on a menacing shape, and the gorse bushes on the roadside loomed up like crouching figures. I imagined I heard footsteps behind me, a soft, stealthy padding, and I spun around more than once, my revolver in my hand, only to see the fog swirling silently in the beam of my lamp.

It was with a gasp of profound relief that I at last saw a new light ahead, a faint, blurred orange glow that slowly resolved itself into the window of a cottage. I had reached the outskirts of Grimpen. A few minutes more of stumbling along the muddy track, and I was in the little village street, standing before the post office. The contrast between the ghastly, fog-bound moor and this small pocket of mundane reality was so jarring that I felt a moment of dizziness.

I pushed open the door and entered. The postmaster, a small, bespectacled man, looked up in surprise at my sudden, wild-eyed appearance.

“A telegram, man, a telegram!” I said, my voice hoarse. “It must go out at once. It is a matter of life and death.”

I scribbled the message upon the form he pushed towards me, my hand shaking so much that the words were barely legible. It was addressed to Sherlock Holmes, at his Baker Street lodgings.

COME AT ONCE. OUR MAN IS STAPLETON. HOUND IS REAL BUT NOT SPECTRAL. A DEVICE OF DIABOLICAL INGENUITY. LIFE IN GRAVEST DANGER. PREPARING TO LAY TRAP BUT NEED YOU. COME TO GRIMPEN. WATSON.

I paid the fee, impressing upon the bewildered postmaster the absolute necessity of its immediate transmission, and then, my duty done, I turned to face the ordeal of the return journey. The thought of it filled me with dread, but the image of Sir Henry waiting alone in that beleaguered house was a more powerful spur than any fear for my own safety.

My journey back was a repetition of the horrors of my journey out, yet with a subtle and more terrible difference. I now knew for a certainty that a human enemy of supreme cunning and malice was at large upon that moor, an enemy who might at any moment emerge from the white wall of fog that surrounded me. The cry of the pony had shaken my nerves, and I found myself starting at every whisper of the wind, every drip of water from the bracken.

It seemed an eternity before I saw the light of Baskerville Hall once more. It was not the single beam of a lantern this time, but the warm, steady glow from the upper windows, a beacon of hope in the smothering darkness. I broke into a run, stumbling on the gravel of the drive, and hammered upon the great oak door.

It was swung open almost at once by Sir Henry himself. His face was pale and drawn in the lamplight, and he held a revolver in his hand.

“Thank God, Watson!” he exclaimed, pulling me inside as Barrymore shot the heavy bolts behind me. “I have been half-mad with worry. Did you send it?”

“It is on its way to Baker Street now,” I panted, leaning against the wall to catch my breath. “With any luck, Holmes could be here by tomorrow evening.”

“Tomorrow may be too late,” he said in a low voice, and his expression was so grim that a fresh chill ran down my spine. “An hour ago, Watson, while you were gone, we heard it.”

“Heard it?” I asked, though I knew what he meant. “The hound?”

“Aye. It was not the cry of a trapped pony this time. It was a baying, Watson. A deep, blood-curdling baying that echoed across the moor. It came from the direction of the mire, from the heart of the Grimpen Mire. It was a sound that would curdle the blood of the bravest man. Barrymore heard it too. It was the Hound of the Baskervilles, and it is hunting.”

We stood in silence for a moment, the full import of his words sinking in. Stapleton had unleashed his beast. Perhaps it was a trial run, or perhaps a form of psychological warfare, a means of unnerving his quarry before the final attack.

“He is making his move,” I said at last.

“Let him,” Sir Henry said, and his eyes glinted with a hard, cold fire. “We know his plan now. He intends for me to be found on the moor, my heart burst from terror, the victim of the family curse. He will arrange it so that no mark is found upon me, save for the footprints of a gigantic hound. He will inherit the fortune, and no one would ever have been the wiser, had it not been for you and for Holmes.”

Our vigil that night was long and trying. We sat up in the study, Sir Henry and I, our revolvers on the table between us, listening. Every creak of the old house, every sigh of wind in the chimney, sent a jolt through our strained nerves. But the night passed without further incident. The fog remained, a thick, impenetrable blanket, but the dreadful baying was not repeated.

The morning brought no relief. The world was still shrouded, and a sense of gloom and foreboding hung heavy within the walls of the Hall. We were prisoners, waiting for our enemy to strike. The tension was becoming unbearable when, around noon, the expected development occurred. A boy from the village arrived with a note. It was from Stapleton.

“My dear Sir Henry,” it read. “I trust the deplorable weather has not dampened your spirits. My sister and I would be so delighted if you would give us the pleasure of your company for supper this evening. Let us say eight o'clock. I feel I must make further amends for my unpardonable behaviour the other day, and Beryl is most anxious to see you. I should be glad to send a servant with a lantern to guide you across the path, should the fog persist. Yours in anticipation, Jack Stapleton.”

Sir Henry read it aloud and then tossed it onto the table. “The invitation,” he said, his voice tight. “The fly is invited into the spider’s parlour.”

“This is it, Sir Henry,” I said, my own heart beginning to beat faster. “This is the night he means to do it. He will ply you with wine, and then send you out, alone and unsuspecting, onto the moor path. And somewhere, in the darkness and the fog, his creature will be waiting.”

“Precisely,” Sir Henry said, a grim smile touching his lips. “And I shall accept his invitation. I shall walk into his trap with my eyes wide open.”

“But you will not walk back alone,” I declared. “I will be there. We must station ourselves somewhere along the path between Merripit House and the Hall. When he unleashes his hound, we shall be ready for it.”

He looked at me, his eyes full of a desperate gratitude. “I was hoping you would say that, Watson. The thought of facing that thing alone, even knowing what it is… well, it is not a pleasant one. But are we enough? Two men against this fiend and his monster?”

“We must be,” I said firmly. “If Holmes and Lestrade arrive tomorrow, they will be too late to save you, though they will surely avenge you. The battle must be fought tonight, by us. We have the advantage of surprise. Stapleton thinks we are fools; he believes you to be a lovelorn suitor and me a bumbling doctor. He does not know that we have uncovered his entire plot. He expects a terrified victim, not an armed and waiting hunting party.”

Our plan was quickly formed. Sir Henry would write a note of acceptance and walk over to Merripit House as arranged, playing the part of the eager guest. I, meanwhile, would leave earlier, under the cloak of the ever-present fog, and conceal myself beside the path at a point where the hound was most likely to be released, close to the boundary of the mire. We knew the layout of the land from our previous excursions; there was a stretch of rocky ground, a series of low tors, which would provide excellent cover. I would wait there for Sir Henry to pass on his way home.

The afternoon was spent in preparation. We cleaned and loaded our revolvers, stuffing our pockets with spare cartridges. Barrymore, who had been informed of the plan, looked upon us as men marching to their doom, but he did his part, providing us with a flask of brandy and a silent, heartfelt handshake.

As evening drew in, the fog seemed to grow even denser, pressing against the house with a silent, inexorable force. It was the perfect stage for the terrible drama that was about to unfold. Sir Henry dressed for dinner, and though he affected a casual air, I could see the immense strain in the tight set of his jaw and the pallor of his skin beneath its tan. He was a brave man, but he was going to offer himself as bait to a monster, and he knew that the slightest mistake could mean a dreadful and lonely death out on the moor.

“Well, Watson,” he said, forcing a smile as he stood ready to depart. “The curtain is going up. Wish me luck.”

“I will do more than that,” I replied, gripping his hand. “I will be there. Keep your nerve, Sir Henry. Walk steadily. Do not run. Let it come right up to you. We must have a clear shot.”

“I’ll not run,” he said, his voice low and fierce. “After what this man has put me through, and what he intended for me, I should rather like to meet his pet. Just be sure your aim is true, old chap.”

He turned and, with a final nod, walked out of the study. I heard his footsteps cross the hall, the grating of the bolts, and the soft thud of the great door closing behind him. He was gone. He had begun his lonely walk towards the house of his would-be murderer. And now, my own part in the night’s terrible work was about to begin.

I gave the room one last survey, taking in the familiar comfort of the leather chairs and the reassuring presence of the books which lined the walls, and I must confess that a profound sense of foreboding settled upon my spirit. I was to leave this sanctuary of warmth and light for the black, fog-bound heart of the moor, an uncertain ally in a battle against an unknown, diabolical foe. I checked my revolver a final time; its cold, heavy weight in my pocket was a small comfort, a tangible link to the world of reason in a place that seemed governed by superstition and fear. Then, without a further backward glance, I slipped out of the side door, which Barrymore had left unbolted for me, and was at once swallowed by the night.

The fog was thicker than I had ever known it. It was a tangible entity, a cold, wet blanket that smothered every sense. It clung to my clothes with damp fingers, and its raw, earthy scent filled my nostrils. The great house of Baskerville vanished behind me after only a dozen paces, its lighted windows fading to faint, blurry smudges before disappearing entirely, leaving me in a world of swirling grey vapour. Sound was deadened, distorted. My own footfalls on the gravel path were soft, furtive thuds, and the sighing of the wind in the bracken was a low, mournful whisper that seemed to come from all directions at once. It was an environment perfectly suited to ambush and sudden death, and I felt a fresh stab of anxiety for Sir Henry, who was at that very moment walking into the lion’s den.

My path lay not directly towards Merripit House, but at an angle to it, towards that grim stretch of the moor where the ground grew treacherous and the great Grimpen Mire began. It was here, amongst a cluster of low, granite tors, that we had determined the attack was most likely to come. The ground was broken and uneven, a labyrinth of stone and heather which would afford me the cover I required. I moved with as much haste as I dared, my eyes straining to pierce the impenetrable gloom. The moor is a difficult place to navigate in the full light of day; at night, in such a fog, it becomes a death trap for the unwary. I relied upon my memory of the landscape, keeping the faint slope of the ground to my left and praying that my sense of direction would not fail me. More than once I stumbled, my foot sinking into a soft patch of sphagnum or turning upon a loose stone, and each time my heart leaped into my throat as I imagined a pair of burning eyes fixed upon me from the darkness.

After what seemed an age of blind, stumbling progress, I felt the ground rise more sharply beneath my feet and the rough, gritty surface of granite. I had reached the tors. I groped my way forward, my outstretched hands brushing against rock that was cold and slick with moisture, until I found a suitable niche, a hollow between two great, weathered boulders that commanded a view of the faint track which Sir Henry must take on his return. Here I crouched, making myself as small as possible, and began my vigil. The silence was now almost absolute, broken only by the drip of water from the rocks above me and the frantic beating of my own heart. I was utterly alone in the vast, dark emptiness of the moor.

To say that my nerves were strained would be a gross understatement. Every faculty was stretched to its utmost limit. My ears ached with the effort of listening for the slightest sound, a footstep, a growl, anything to break the dreadful stillness. My eyes scanned the shifting wall of grey before me, seeking to resolve the nebulous shapes of the fog into some recognisable form. Time seemed to lose all meaning. Minutes stretched into eternities. I thought of my friend Holmes, and wished for the hundredth time for his cool, incisive mind and unflappable courage. He would be sitting comfortably in his carriage, I imagined, speeding through the sleeping countryside, while I was crouched here like a hunted animal, my life and that of my friend hanging upon the steadiness of my hand.

I pictured the scene at Merripit House: the lamp-lit dinner, the polite conversation, the veneer of civilisation masking a murderous heart. I saw Stapleton, with his thin lips and pale eyes, playing the genial host, perhaps discussing his latest lepidopteral findings, while all the time he was listening, waiting for the moment to unleash his monstrous agent. And Sir Henry, our poor, brave Sir Henry, forced to eat this man’s salt, to smile at his jests, knowing that the man opposite him had already signed his death warrant. The sheer cold-blooded villainy of it sent a fresh wave of anger through me, steeling my resolve. Let the beast come; I was ready for it.

Suddenly, a sound, faint and distant, cut through the fog. It was the sharp, metallic clang of a closing door. My head snapped up, every muscle in my body tensing. It had to be from Merripit House. The terrible charade was over. Sir Henry was on his way. I drew my revolver from my pocket and thumbed back the hammer, the quiet click sounding like a gunshot in the profound silence. I peered down the path, my gaze fixed on the spot where I expected him to appear. The fog eddied and swirled, a ghostly curtain that might at any moment part to reveal the actors in our tragedy.

Then I heard it.

It began as a low, deep-throated sound, a guttural rumble that seemed to vibrate through the very ground beneath me. It was not the bark of any dog I had ever heard. There was a quality of profound malignancy in it, a slobbering, baying cry that rose in volume until it filled the night, a hellish chorus that spoke of death and ancient evil. It was the sound that had haunted the dreams of the Baskervilles for generations, the legendary baying of the hound. Even I, a man of science and reason, felt a primal chill of terror grip my soul. For a moment, the old stories seemed not tales but truths, and I felt as if we were not fighting a mere man, but a supernatural force, a demon loosed from the pit.

Through the dreadful chorus of sound, I could now distinguish another noise: the hurried, pounding rhythm of running feet. It was Sir Henry. He had appeared as a dark, fleeting shape at the edge of my vision, a silhouette against the grey. All our carefully laid plans, our injunctions to walk steadily and not to run, had been shattered by the sheer, unearthly terror of that cry. He was running for his life, and behind him, gaining at every stride, was the creature.

And then I saw it. The fog parted for a brief, terrible instant, as if a curtain had been drawn back upon a scene from hell. It was not a dog, or not what I could call a dog. It was a vast, coal-black shape, larger than any hound I had ever seen, a creature of nightmare. But it was not its size, nor its speed, nor the slavering jaws that struck the heart with icy horror. It was the fact that the thing glowed. A flickering, spectral fire seemed to play about its muzzle and its eyes, which burned with a smouldering, hellish light. A faint, phosphorescent shimmer outlined its massive form, giving it the appearance of some ghastly apparition. The hound of the Baskervilles lived, and it was as dreadful as the legend had ever painted it.

It was upon him. I saw the great beast gather itself for a spring, a silent, deadly launch that would tear the throat from our friend. There was no more time for waiting, no time for a clear shot. Sir Henry screamed, a high, choking cry of ultimate terror, and threw himself to one side, falling heavily to the ground. In that same instant, I fired.

The roar of my Webley was deafening, a sharp crack that momentarily overcame the hound’s baying. I saw the creature flinch, and a terrible, high-pitched yelp of pain and fury tore through the night. The supernatural spell was broken! The thing was mortal; it could be hurt, and it could be killed. Heartened, I leaped from my hiding place and ran down the slope, firing again as I went. The second bullet struck home, for the hound staggered, its head dropping. It was still a fearsome sight, a great, wounded beast, its ghastly luminescence flickering, its eyes blazing with fury. It had forgotten Sir Henry now; its attention was entirely upon me, the new source of its pain. It turned, snarling, its dripping, fiery jaws agape, and charged.

I stood my ground, my heart hammering against my ribs. The words of our plan echoed in my mind: “Let it come right up to you.” This was the moment. The colossal shape loomed out of the mist, a charging spectre of flame and fury. I could smell its foul, hot breath. I saw the insane rage in its burning eyes. Waiting until it was no more than ten yards from me, I took careful aim at the centre of the dark mass and squeezed the trigger. I fired again, and then a fifth time, emptying my revolver into the advancing monster.

For a moment, it seemed that my shots had no effect. The creature’s momentum carried it forward, but then it faltered. Its front legs buckled, and with a final, choking howl that ended in a rattling gasp, it crashed to the ground almost at my feet. It lay there, its great black flanks heaving, the ghastly light around its muzzle slowly fading as its life ebbed away. In the sudden, ringing silence, I could hear nothing but my own ragged breathing and the whimpering of Sir Henry, who lay prone upon the path.

I hurried to his side, my empty revolver still smoking in my hand. “Sir Henry! Are you hurt?” I asked, my voice hoarse.

He rolled over, his face a pale, ghostly mask in the gloom. He was uninjured, but his nerves were utterly shattered. He stared at the vast, still shape on the ground, his eyes wide with a horror that was slow to fade. “My God, Watson,” he whispered, his voice trembling uncontrollably. “What was it? What, in God’s name, was it?”

I knelt beside the dead creature. The unholy light had now vanished completely. In the faint, ambient luminescence of the fog, I could see it for what it was: an immense hound, indeed, a cross of mastiff and bloodhound, gaunt, savage, and as large as a small lion. But it was its head that drew my attention. I touched its muzzle, and my fingers came away sticky and luminous. “Phosphorus,” I said, a wave of revulsion and triumph washing over me. “The villain coated it with a preparation of phosphorus. A cunning devil, to make his accomplice seem supernatural.”

We stood there for a minute in silence, two shaken men beside the corpse of the monster that had so nearly claimed a life. The fog swirled around us, muffling the world, and the moor was once more silent and brooding. We had faced the hound and we had won. But as I looked from the dead beast to the pale, shaken baronet, I knew that our work was not yet finished. The agent of the crime lay dead, but its master, the cunning, venomous human brain behind it all, was still at large.

“Stapleton,” I said, my voice hard.

Sir Henry scrambled to his feet, a new fire beginning to replace the terror in his eyes. He looked towards the faint, distant location of Merripit House. “The brute,” he growled, his voice thick with rage. “He sent that thing to kill me. Watson, we must go after him.”

“Indeed,” I replied, reloading my revolver with cartridges from my pocket. “He believes his plan has succeeded. He believes you are lying dead upon the moor. We still have the element of surprise.” I handed Sir Henry his own pistol, which he had dropped in his flight. He took it, his hand now steady. The hunter had become the hunted. Our terrible night on the moor was not over. It had merely entered its final, most desperate act. With a shared glance of grim understanding, we turned and began to walk, side by side, through the fog and the darkness, towards the house of the murderer.

Our vigil on the path had been a cold and trying one, and the subsequent rush of adrenaline had left us both weary to the bone. Yet, the grim task ahead lent a terrible energy to our limbs. We trod the path to Merripit House not as guests or neighbours, but as executioners of a justice long overdue. The fog, a damp and clinging shroud, had thinned in places, only to thicken in others, creating a disorienting labyrinth of grey walls and fleeting corridors; yet through it all, the pale glimmer of a light, a single point of yellow in the universal monochrome, served as our unerring beacon. It was a light in an upper window of the naturalist’s house.

“He is there, Watson,” Sir Henry muttered, his voice a low growl. The terror that had so recently gripped him had been burned away by the fires of indignation, leaving behind a cold, hard resolve that was more formidable than any simple courage. “He is waiting, perchance, for news of his devil’s work.”

“Let him,” the baronet said grimly. “I ask for nothing more than to meet him face to face.”

We left the path and struck out across the damp ground, our boots squelching softly in the peaty soil. Our approach was made in silence, each of us lost in our own dark thoughts. The house loomed up before us, a blacker shape in the surrounding gloom, that one square of lighted window staring out into the night like the baleful eye of some brooding creature. As we drew nearer, we crouched low, moving under the cover of a line of gorse bushes until we were directly beneath the walls of the house itself.

From this vantage point we could see that the lighted window was that of a bedroom on the first floor. The window was open a few inches at the top, and from it, we could hear a sound that made my blood run cold. It was the sound of a woman weeping, a low, despairing sobbing that was intermittently broken by what sounded like the sharp crack of a whip, or perhaps a cane, striking some heavy piece of furniture. A man’s voice, tight with fury, could be heard in a low monotone, but his words were indistinct.

“My God, Watson, he is beating her,” Sir Henry whispered, his knuckles white as he gripped his pistol. “The cowardly brute!”

My own heart was pounding with a mixture of pity and rage. It was clear that Mrs. Stapleton was not a willing accomplice in her husband’s villainy, but a terrified and abused prisoner. The entire scene on the moor, the strange warnings, the brother’s supposed disapproval, it all fell into a new and more terrible context.

We crept along the wall to the front of the house. The door was shut and bolted, as we had expected. We tried the windows on the ground floor, one by one, and found them all to be securely fastened. It was the dining room window which finally yielded. The latch was old and loose, and with the aid of my pocketknife, I was able to jimmy it back until the window swung silently inward. Sir Henry went through first, as lithe and silent as a cat, and I followed immediately after, closing the window behind me.

We stood for a moment in the darkness of the dining room, our ears straining for any sound from above. The weeping had ceased, but we could hear footsteps moving about hurriedly, and the shutting of drawers. The house was filled with the faint, cloying scent of ether, and another, sharper chemical smell which I could not immediately place. The air was heavy with a palpable sense of evil.

With a nod to Sir Henry, I led the way out into the central hall. A lamp was burning low on a table, casting long, dancing shadows up the walls and onto the staircase. To our left was the door to the sitting room, where we had first met Stapleton. It was from there that the chemical smell seemed to be strongest. Pushing the door open, I found the room just as I remembered it, with its many glass cases of mounted insects. But on a central table, under the light of a powerful oil lamp, was a scene of a more sinister nature. A large tin of phosphorus paste, the same kind I had seen used for luminous paint, stood open, its contents smeared across a porcelain dish. Beside it lay several brushes and a large, empty kennel chain. There could be no doubt; this was the very workshop where the infernal hound had been prepared for its ghastly role.

A sudden, loud crash from upstairs brought our investigation to an abrupt end. We rushed back into the hall and took the stairs two at a time, our pistols held ready. The sounds had come from the room with the lighted window. The door was locked.

“Stand back, Watson!” Sir Henry cried, and without a moment’s hesitation, he threw his powerful shoulder against the door. The wood splintered, but the lock held. He drew back and lunged again, this time with a roar of fury. The lock burst from its housing with a crack like a pistol shot, and the door flew open.

The scene within was one of utter chaos and desperation. The room was in disarray, with clothes and personal effects strewn across the floor. In the centre of it all, bound tightly to an upright chair with coils of thick rope, was Mrs. Stapleton. A silk scarf had been forced into her mouth as a gag, and her eyes, wide and terrified, were fixed upon us. Her face was pale and marked with a long, red weal upon her cheek, a testament to her husband’s cruelty. There was, however, no sign of Stapleton himself.

Sir Henry let out a cry of pity and anger as he rushed to the poor woman’s side, while I kept my watch on the door and the open window. With his knife, the baronet quickly severed the ropes, and I gently removed the gag from her mouth. She collapsed forward, gasping and sobbing, into Sir Henry’s arms.

“He is gone,” she whispered, her voice hoarse and broken. “He heard the shots. He knew his plan had failed. He came up here in a rage. He told me I had betrayed him, that he would see to it that I shared his fate. Then he heard your approach and fled.”

“Which way did he go?” I asked urgently, helping her to a seat. “Did you see?”

She pointed a trembling finger towards the open window. “Out there,” she said. “Into the mire. It was always his escape. He has a path marked, a secret way through the bog to the old mine workings on the central island. He has food and water stored there. He boasted that no one could ever follow him.”

I strode to the window and peered out into the impenetrable grey soup of the fog. To flee into the heart of the Grimpen Mire on such a night was an act of supreme desperation. Even for one who knew its secrets, the risks were enormous.

“He was fastening the window when he heard you at the door below,” she continued, her strength seeming to return with the release from her bonds. “He told me he would be back for me, that I was not to think I could escape him. He has kept me a prisoner for years, Watson. I am not his sister. I am his wife. My name is Beryl Garcia, and I came with him from Costa Rica, where my family was of good standing. He is not Stapleton, but Rodger Baskerville, the son of Sir Charles’s younger brother, the one who was thought to have died unmarried in South America. He discovered his claim to the inheritance, and it turned his heart to stone. He used me, my story, my very presence, to lure Sir Henry into his trap, all the while promising me freedom if I would only obey.”

Her confession poured out in a torrent, a dam of fear and oppression bursting at last. She confirmed what my friend Holmes had already suspected, filling in the terrible details of a plot so diabolical in its cunning that it took my breath away. Stapleton, or rather Baskerville, had purchased the hound, the most savage he could find, and kept it hidden in the old Neolithic hut out on the moor. He had used the legend to his advantage, and the phosphorus to give his agent a supernatural terror. His supposed passion for entomology was merely a cover for his frequent excursions into the mire, where he tended his hound and perfected his escape route.

“The path,” I said, turning back to her. “How is it marked?”

“With white wands,” she replied, her eyes gleaming with a sudden, fierce hope. “Slender sticks of peeled willow, which he drove into the ground at intervals. They are all that stands between solid earth and a dreadful death. He took his collecting tin and his net, to maintain his disguise should he be seen.”

“Then we may still catch him,” cried Sir Henry, his face a grim mask. “Watson, we must not delay.”

“Indeed,” I agreed. “Mrs. Baskerville, I must ask you to remain here. Barricade this door and do not open it for anyone but us. We will return.”

The lady nodded, her eyes filled with a mixture of terror and gratitude. We left her there, two grim-faced hunters, and plunged back out into the night. The fog had grown thicker still, and it was only with the aid of our pocket lamps that we were able to find the faint track leading from the back of Merripit House into the mire itself. After a few hundred yards, just as the ground was becoming soft and treacherous underfoot, Sir Henry let out a low exclamation. In the beam of his lamp, a slender white stick was visible, poking up a foot or so from a clump of reeds.

The chase had begun. We moved with as much haste as we dared, our lamps sweeping from side to side, searching for the next marker. The mire was a place of dreadful and alien beauty. Great clumps of strange, luridly-coloured mosses clung to the black, peaty soil, and the air was filled with the murmur of unseen water and the sighing of the wind through the reeds. Pools of black, stagnant water lay on every side of us, their surfaces gleaming like polished jet in our lights. It was on the banks of one of these that I saw it, a single, dark object half-submerged in the slime.

It was a man’s boot, of a familiar tweed pattern. I had no doubt that it was the one that had been stolen from Sir Henry at his hotel in London, the one Stapleton had used to give the hound its scent. He must have dropped it in his panicked flight.

We pushed on, our hearts beating with a savage excitement. The wands led us deeper and deeper into the heart of the great morass. Twice, Sir Henry sank up to his knees in the treacherous ground, and it was only with my help that he was able to pull himself free. The world had shrunk to the small circles of our lamplight and the desperate search for the next white stick.

It was I who found the final piece of evidence. Some fifty yards past the boot, lying on a patch of relatively firm ground, was the butterfly net, its green gauze a ghostly wisp in the fog. But beyond it, the line of white wands came to an abrupt end. Before us lay a vast, quaking bog of unimaginable horror, a place where the very ground seemed to heave and bubble as if with a foul life of its own. Streaks of phosphorescent marsh gas, the infamous will-o’-the-wisps, drifted like lost souls over its surface. The last wand stood at the very edge of this abyss, a final, silent signpost to oblivion.

We stood there for a long time, peering into the dreadful quagmire, listening. But there was no sound save for the gurgle of the bog and the whisper of the night wind. We called his name, our voices sounding small and absurd in the vast emptiness. There was no reply. Somewhere out there, in the clinging, bottomless slime, the last of the evil Baskervilles had met his end. The great Grimpen Mire, which he had used as his accomplice and his refuge, had claimed him for its own. In his haste, blinded by fog and terror, he must have missed a footing, taken one false step on his own secret path. There could be no more fitting a grave.

Slowly and wearily, we made our way back along the trail of wands, two exhausted men whose hunt was finally over. The light was beginning to break as we reached Merripit House, the fog turning from black to a pearly grey. Mrs. Baskerville was waiting for us, her face pale but composed. We told her what we had found, or rather, what we had not found. She received the news in silence, her expression unreadable, and I knew that her future, though free from her tyrant, would be a difficult and uncertain one.

As the sun rose over the moor, casting a golden light over the landscape that had so recently been a place of terror, I looked at Sir Henry. He was safe. The curse was broken. The shadow that had haunted his family for generations was lifted at last. Our work was done. And though my friend Holmes had laid the foundations, I could not but feel a certain satisfaction that it was I, his humble friend and colleague, who had been there at the last, to face the hound and to see the final, dreadful justice of the mire. The case of the Hound of the Baskervilles was, at long last, closed.

We slept, if such a broken and troubled state could be called sleep, for a few short hours upon our return. The grey, unforgiving light of a new day was already filtering through the windows of Merripit House, painting the dreary room in shades of ash and bone. Sir Henry had collapsed into a chair by the cold hearth, his face smudged with dirt and fatigue, his eyes staring into the middle distance. He looked a man ten years older than the hearty baronet who had arrived at Waterloo Station so full of life and vigour. As for myself, every bone in my body ached with a weariness so profound that it felt as if I had marched across the whole of Devonshire and back again.

Mrs. Baskerville, as we must now call her, had retired to her room without a word, a silent, wraithlike figure who had accepted the news of her husband’s monstrous end with a stillness that was more unnerving than any outburst of grief. Her silence was a heavy blanket upon the house, and I found my thoughts turning to her with a mixture of pity and professional curiosity. What was to become of her now? She was a victim, yes, but she was also the widow of a would be murderer and the keeper of his terrible secrets. The law, I feared, might not be so quick to draw the distinction.

“Watson,” said Sir Henry at last, his voice a dry rasp. “What a black and bloody business this has been.”

“It is over now, Sir Henry,” I said, rising and placing a hand upon his shoulder. “You are safe. The curse is broken.”

“Safe,” he repeated, and gave a short, bitter laugh that had no mirth in it. “I feel as if I shall never be safe again. To think that I walked with him, talked with him, shared a cigar with him. All the while he was plotting to set that foul beast upon me, to see me torn to pieces upon the moor which he knew I had come to love.” He shuddered, a violent tremor that shook his entire frame. “And the lady, Watson. Beryl. To think she was his wife all along. By thunder, what a snake he was, to use her so, to make her his lure. And yet, she tried to warn me. I see it all now. Her strange words, her fear. I thought it was maidenly hesitation, but it was terror. Terror of him.”

“She showed great courage,” I agreed. “It was her message, the warning cut from the Times, that first put my friend Holmes upon the right track.”

“And to what end?” he cried, springing to his feet and beginning to pace the room. “She is free of the brute, but what is she now? A widow, alone in a strange country, with the shadow of his crimes upon her. We cannot simply abandon her, Watson. It would not be the act of a gentleman.”

“Indeed not,” I said, my heart warming to the baronet’s innate decency, even in the midst of his own trauma. “But first, we must consider our own position. There is a dead convict upon the moor, a gigantic dog shot and lying in a gully, and a man missing, presumed drowned, in the Grimpen Mire. The authorities must be notified, and we must have a story for them that is both truthful and, if you will forgive me, believable.”

It was this practical consideration which finally broke through Sir Henry’s daze. He stopped his pacing and looked at me, a glimmer of his old self returning to his eyes. “You are right, of course, old fellow. Always the clear head. What do you propose we do?”

Before I could answer, there was a light tap at the door, and Mrs. Baskerville entered. She was dressed in a simple dark gown and had combed her hair, but her face was as pale as marble. She carried a small tray upon which sat a pot of coffee and two cups.

“I thought you might have need of this,” she said, her voice low and carrying a trace of that exotic accent which I now knew to be Costa Rican. “You have been up all the night.”

Sir Henry stared at her, his expression a conflict of pity, admiration, and a deep, wounded confusion. “Mrs. Baskerville,” he began, “I… we are deeply sorry for your loss.”

It was a conventional thing to say, and yet in the circumstances, it sounded utterly absurd. A faint, sad smile touched her lips. “Please,” she said. “Do not be sorry. You have set me free. The man I lost died many years ago; the creature in the mire was but his monstrous shell. My name is Beryl Garcia. I should be glad if you would call me so.”

She set the tray down upon a dusty table, and her composure, which had been so rigidly maintained, seemed to suddenly falter. Her hands began to tremble, and her eyes filled with tears. Sir Henry, acting upon a noble instinct, stepped forward and gently guided her to a chair.

“You are amongst friends here, Miss Garcia,” he said softly. “You need not fear anything now.”

At this simple kindness, her fortitude broke completely, and she buried her face in her hands, her shoulders shaking with silent sobs. Sir Henry stood over her, a helpless and distressed expression upon his face, looking to me for guidance. I gestured for him to sit, and poured a cup of the steaming coffee, placing it before her. For some time, no one spoke. The only sounds were the woman’s quiet weeping and the distant cry of a curlew from the brightening moor.

It was I who finally broke the silence. “Miss Garcia,” I said, as gently as I could, “I am afraid that we must trouble you. The police will have to be informed of what has occurred. For them to understand the true position, and to ensure that no blame falls upon you, we will need to hear your story. Everything that you can bear to tell us.”

She looked up, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. “I will tell you everything,” she said, her voice gaining a measure of strength. “He cannot hurt me now.”

And so, in the cold morning light, with the smell of stale smoke and damp decay in our nostrils, we listened as the terrible story of her life with Stapleton unfolded. She told us of their meeting in South America, of the handsome, charming naturalist who had swept her off her feet, and of the slow, horrifying revelation of the cruel, scheming devil who lay beneath that placid exterior. He had squandered her fortune, isolated her from her friends, and dragged her to England on the pretext of his scientific pursuits, only to reveal his mad, ambitious plot to claim the Baskerville estate. She had been his prisoner, bound to him by fear and by the twisted remnants of a love she had once felt. Her attempts to warn Sir Henry had been desperate gambles, carried out under the constant threat of his discovery and his terrifying rage.

“He saw me as a tool,” she said, her voice bitter with memory. “When he brought me here, he insisted I pose as his sister. He believed that Sir Henry might be more easily lured to his doom if he thought he was courting a free woman. But then… then he became jealous. The night you saw us on the moor path, I was trying to warn Sir Henry to leave, to flee for his life. Stapleton saw us, he saw Sir Henry’s arm around me, and he went mad. He did not care for me, you understand, not as a person. But I was his property. The thought that another man might touch his property drove him into a fury.”

Her narrative laid bare the entire squalid enterprise, from the purchase of the hound, a cross between a mastiff and a bloodhound, to the theft of Sir Henry’s boot at the hotel, to the use of the phosphorus to create the illusion of a supernatural beast. It was a story of such calculated, cold blooded wickedness that it left us speechless.

By the time she had finished, the sun was fully risen. I knew what must be done. I left Sir Henry to offer what comfort he could to the distraught woman, and I made my way on foot to the village of Grimpen to fetch the local constabulary. I found Inspector Davies, the head of the local force, at his breakfast. He was a stout, sensible looking man with a ruddy face and shrewd grey eyes. My tale, which I delivered with as much sobriety and factual detail as I could muster, was met with an expression of profound astonishment. A spectral hound, a fiendish plot for an inheritance, a naturalist who was a secret baronet; it was a great deal for a country policeman to digest along with his toast and marmalade.

“Dr. Watson,” he said, when I had finished, wiping his mouth with a napkin. “I know you to be a man of some standing, and a friend of Mr. Sherlock Holmes, whose name is not unknown to us even down here. But this is the most extraordinary narrative I have ever heard.”

“I am aware of that, Inspector,” I replied. “Which is why I suggest you come with me at once to Merripit House. The evidence you will find there will leave you in no doubt as to the truth of my words.”

And so it proved. Davies, accompanied by two of his constables, returned with me to the house on the moor. The sight of Beryl Garcia, her haunted beauty and her evident distress, seemed to impress him favourably. But it was the tour of the house itself that truly convinced him. In an upstairs room, we found Stapleton’s makeshift laboratory: jars of chemicals, including a large container of phosphorus, and books on heredity and local folklore. More damning still, in a locked outbuilding, we found the place where the hound had been kept. A system of chains and pulleys spoke of a creature of immense strength, and the ground was littered with the enormous bones of sheep and, to our horror, the skeletal remains of several smaller dogs. Davies, a dog lover himself, looked upon the scene with a grim expression.

“He starved them, sir,” he said to me in a low voice. “Then likely fed the weaker ones to his monster. There is a special place in hell for a man who would do that.”

The rest of the day was a blur of official activity. The bog was searched, though all knew it was a futile endeavour. The Grimpen Mire does not give up its secrets, or its victims. The corpse of the great hound was located and photographed, a grotesque monument to Stapleton’s evil. The body of Selden was formally identified. And through it all, statements were taken, notes were made, and the slow, ponderous machinery of the law began to turn.

That evening, as Sir Henry and I sat in the great dining hall of Baskerville Hall, the ordeal finally over, a telegram was delivered. It was from London. I opened it and read it aloud.

“WATSON, MERIPIT HOUSE. HAVE FOLLOWED YOUR REPORTS WITH INTEREST. DO NOT ALLOW SIR HENRY TO BECOME TOO ATTACHED TO THE WIDOW. REBOUNDS CAN BE MORE DANGEROUS THAN HOUNDS. EXPECT YOU BOTH IN BAKER STREET AT YOUR EARLIEST CONVENIENCE. HOLMES.”

Sir Henry listened, a weary smile touching his lips. “Your friend does not miss a trick, does he, Watson? ‘More dangerous than hounds’. I dare say he is right.” He looked into the fire, his face thoughtful. “Dr. Mortimer has been suggesting for some time that I take a long sea voyage, to put some distance between myself and all this. I believe the time has come to take his advice.”

“An excellent notion,” I agreed heartily.

“And Miss Garcia,” he continued, his brow furrowed. “I have spoken with my solicitors. A portion of the Baskerville fortune was, by rights, her husband’s, had he been the legitimate heir. It seems only right that she should not be left destitute on his account. They will see to it that she is provided for, and arrangements will be made for her safe passage back to her own people, should she so wish.”

I looked at my friend, and a great sense of admiration filled me. He had been through a trial of fire and brimstone, and had emerged not broken, but tempered. He had lost the woman he thought he loved, but had retained his honour and his compassion.

Two days later, we were on the train, speeding away from the rolling hills of Devon and towards the familiar fogs of London. As I looked out of the window, I saw the great, wild expanse of the moor for the last time, its tors black against the skyline. It was a place of stark beauty, but it held too many dark memories. I was glad to be leaving it behind.

Our arrival at 221B Baker Street was a return to the centre of my universe. The rooms were as untidy as ever, the air thick with the pungent aroma of shag tobacco, the mantelpiece littered with letters, test tubes, and a jackknife pinning a new set of unanswered correspondence to the woodwork. And there, in his mouse coloured dressing gown, was the man himself, Sherlock Holmes, looking up from a complex chemical experiment with a welcoming gleam in his deep set eyes.

“Watson! Sir Henry! Excellent,” he said, as if we had just returned from a stroll in the park rather than a life and death struggle with the forces of darkness. “You are just in time. I believe I am on the verge of developing a new method for detecting bloodstains upon tweed. You see, the lanolin in the wool presents a unique challenge to the standard guaiacum test. However, if one introduces a solution of… but I digress. You have had a most trying experience. Pray, be seated. Watson, you will recount for me the final act. Your telegrams, while concise, lacked the dramatic flair that is your particular specialty.”

And so, by the cheerful blaze of the Baker Street fire, with the rattle of hansom cabs in the street below, I told him everything: the vigil on the moor, the terrifying appearance of the hound, the chase, the discovery of Stapleton’s lair, and the final, dreadful justice of the Grimpen Mire. He listened in his usual fashion, with his eyes closed and his fingertips pressed together, a faint smile playing on his thin lips.

When I had finished, he remained silent for a long moment. Then he opened his eyes and fixed me with a gaze that was both sharp and, to my surprise, surprisingly warm.

“You have done well, Watson,” he said simply. “Exceedingly well. You have conducted yourself with courage and with no small amount of ingenuity. You may not have been the light, but you were an excellent conductor of the light.”

Coming from Holmes, this was high praise indeed. I felt my cheeks flush with a pleasure that no medal or official commendation could ever have provided.

“It was a near run thing, Holmes,” I confessed. “Had you not deduced the truth about Stapleton’s wife, I fear I should have been completely at sea.”

“Nonsense,” he said, with a dismissive wave of his hand. “You saw the truth of the situation; you simply lacked the final piece of the puzzle. Stapleton made two fatal errors. The first was his arrogance, in believing his intellect superior to all others. The second, and more fatal, was in underestimating the bonds of friendship. He could not conceive that I would send my best man to watch over my client. It is a deficiency of the criminal mind; it cannot comprehend loyalty, and so it is always, in the end, taken by surprise.”

He rose and went to the window, looking down at the bustling stream of London life.

“The world is full of obvious things which nobody by any chance ever observes,” he murmured, more to himself than to us. “Stapleton saw Sir Henry as an obstacle, the hound as a weapon, and the moor as a tool. He failed to see the man, the friend, and the spirit of the place itself. And in the end, they all conspired to bring about his ruin.”

He turned back to us, his face animated once more. “And now, Sir Henry, your world tour! An admirable plan. And Watson, a new case has just arrived which may be of interest. It involves a stolen Stradivarius, a club footed man, and the curious affair of the vanishing omnibus. The game, my dear friend, is afoot once more.”

As I settled into my old chair, the comforting familiarity of Baker Street wrapping around me like a warm cloak, I knew that the shadow of the hound was finally and truly behind us. The moors of Devon seemed a world away, a dark dream from which we had at last awakened.
