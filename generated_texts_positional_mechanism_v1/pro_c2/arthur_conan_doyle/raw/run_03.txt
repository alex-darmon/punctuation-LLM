I confess that a profound unease had settled upon me. Lord Alistair’s fascination with the young woman from the toll-house, a Miss Croft, had become a matter of some consternation, and his decision to meet her that evening against my express advice filled me with a sense of foreboding. He was headstrong, and his recent inheritance of Blackwood Manor had only served to amplify a certain recklessness in his character. Fearing he might be walking into some manner of trap, I determined that my duty was to him and not to the niceties of his privacy.

I slipped out of the manor by a side door and made my way towards the old woods that bordered the estate. I kept to the shadows of the ancient oaks, my feet making no sound on the thick carpet of moss, until I reached the crest of a small hillock which overlooked the river path. From this vantage, I saw him almost at once. He was not a hundred yards distant, standing by the water’s edge, and the lady beside him, her slight figure wrapped in a dark shawl, could be none other than Miss Croft. They had clearly arranged this meeting, for they were engaged in a conversation of the most earnest nature. I saw him take her hand, and she did not pull away, but rather leaned towards him, her head bowed as she spoke in low, urgent tones. He, for his part, listened with a grave and tender expression, and once he raised her hand to his lips. To play the part of a sneak upon my employer was a thing I abhorred, yet what choice had I? If some danger were to befall him, I was near enough to shout a warning, though too far, I admit, to offer any immediate aid. It was a damnable position, and I could only resolve to confess my trespass to him later.

They had been speaking for some quarter of an hour when I became aware of a second presence in the woods. A flicker of movement among the ferns caught my eye, and then I saw him; a burly, coarse-looking fellow moving with a stealth that belied his size. It was Thomas Croft, the lady’s brother, a man known in the village for his sullen temper and his strength. He was much closer to the pair than I was, creeping along the riverbank as if stalking some animal. At that moment, I saw Lord Alistair put his arm about Miss Croft’s waist, drawing her closer. She seemed to yield to him, her head resting for an instant upon his shoulder. He bent as if to kiss her, but before his lips could meet hers, they both started back. The brother had burst from the trees. He ran at them, not with the wild gesticulations of a madman, but with a grim and terrible purpose. I saw his fist fly out, and Lord Alistair staggered back, clutching at his jaw. The lady gave a single, piercing cry. Croft seized her by the arm, his voice a low growl that I could not make out, and began to drag her away. Lord Alistair recovered himself and took a step after them, his own voice raised in anger, but Croft merely turned, spat upon the ground, and pulled his sister more roughly along the path until they were lost to sight. Lord Alistair stood for a long minute, one hand still pressed to his face, and then he turned and began to walk slowly back towards the manor, his head bowed in a picture of utter humiliation.

I could not bear to see him so, and I ran down the slope to intercept him. His cheek was already swelling, and a dark fury burned in his eyes.

“Good heavens, Harrington! Must you appear from the very earth?” he cried. “Do not tell me you were watching.”

I told him everything: my anxiety for his safety, my decision to follow, and the whole miserable scene I had just witnessed. His eyes flashed at me for a second, but my plain account seemed to take the fight out of him, and he gave a short, bitter laugh.

“So you saw the whole performance? I dare say you had a better view of it than I did,” he said. “A fine suitor I make, to be felled like a bullock at the market fair!”

I offered what scant comfort I could, but in truth, I was as mystified as he. That a man would so violently refuse such a fortune for his sister was beyond all reason. I watched Sir Henry pace the room, his heart full of joyous plans, and I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the autumn air. I felt as a man might who watches a friend walk happily towards a patch of ground which he knows to be a treacherous mire. My duty was to protect him, yet what could I say? My fears were but shadows, my suspicions mere wisps of feeling with no substance to support them. To voice them now would be to ruin his happiness and to brand myself as a morbid killjoy. Our life for the next week fell into a pleasant and almost idyllic routine. Sir Henry saw Miss Stapleton nearly every day, sometimes walking with her on the moor, and on two occasions she and her brother came to dine with us at the Hall. Stapleton was a changed man. He was courteous, engaging, and full of fascinating talk about the flora and fauna of the moor. He made no reference to our previous friction, and his attitude towards Sir Henry was one of respectful cordiality. He still kept a close eye upon his sister, and they were seldom out of each other’s company, but there was none of the fierce, almost feral possessiveness that he had shown before. He seemed to have accepted the situation, and Sir Henry was in the seventh heaven of delight.

For my own part, I found my suspicions lulled by this new state of affairs. I could find no fault in Stapleton’s conduct, and his sister was so clearly happy in Sir Henry’s company that it seemed perverse to imagine some hidden danger. She had a wonderful lightness of spirit which transformed the grim old Hall whenever she entered it. Her laughter echoed in the dark-panelled dining room, and her presence in the drawing room seemed to make the candles burn the brighter. She and Sir Henry were deeply in love; it was plain to see in every glance that passed between them. And yet, I saw something else. On two or three occasions, when she thought no one was watching her, I saw her gaze rest upon her brother with an expression of the most profound and pitiable anxiety. Once, as he was describing with scientific coolness the method by which a certain carnivorous plant entraps its insect prey, I saw her hand tremble and her face go as pale as marble. It was a fleeting look, a shadow that passed as quickly as it came, but it renewed all my old misgivings. It was the look of a person who is living in daily and hourly fear.

It was on the Friday of that week that the affair took a new and sinister turn. Sir Henry had ridden over to Princetown on some business in the morning, and I had spent the day sorting through the Baskerville family papers, a task which I had undertaken in the hope of finding some clue to the old legend. It was a dusty and unrewarding business, and I was glad to give it up as the evening drew in. I was standing at the window of the library, watching the grey curtains of rain sweep across the melancholy moor, when I saw a figure hurrying up the drive. It was a small boy, soaked to the skin and splashing through the puddles, and I saw that he carried a telegram in his hand.

I took it from him and signed the receipt, my mind working swiftly. A telegram for Sir Henry, who was away. My instructions from Sherlock Holmes had been explicit: I was to open all such communications without delay. With a sense of foreboding, I tore open the envelope. The message was from London, and it contained only three words: “Lost portmanteau found.”

It was from the manager of the hotel where Sir Henry had his new boot stolen. At the time, we had put it down to a simple, if bizarre, theft. But now, in the light of all that had happened, it struck me as something more. It was one of a chain of strange and inexplicable events. The anonymous warning sent to London, the theft of one old and one new boot, and now this sudden reappearance of the luggage. What did it mean? Was it possible that the thief, having accomplished his purpose, whatever it was, had now returned the stolen property? And what purpose could be served by the temporary possession of a single new boot?

My mind was still grappling with this small puzzle when Sir Henry himself returned. He was in a black humour, for his horse had cast a shoe and he had been forced to walk the last two miles in the downpour. His mood did not improve when I showed him the telegram.

“Well, what of it?” he said, tossing it onto the table. “Some chambermaid has found it in a cupboard, I suppose. It’s the boot I want, not the confounded bag.”

“But don’t you see, there is a connection here,” I insisted. “Someone has been following you, watching your every move, from the moment you arrived in London.”

“Oh, you and your connections, Watson,” he said, with a flash of impatience. “You see plots and conspiracies in everything. The warning was a joke, a stupid prank. The boots were taken by a common thief. And the man on the tor was a shepherd, for all we know. My life has been full of real troubles these last few days, and I have no time for imaginary ones. The only thing that matters is that Stapleton has come round, and Beryl and I can be together. The rest is just mist and moonshine.”

He was in no mood for my theories, and I did not press them. I could not blame him. He was a practical man, and his mind was filled with the most practical and pleasant of prospects: love and marriage. My vague and shadowy fears must have seemed like the morbid fancies of an idle man.

The next morning, however, brought a development which even Sir Henry could not dismiss so lightly. We were at breakfast when Barrymore, the butler, entered the room. His long, mournful face was set in lines of deep distress.

“If I may have a word, Sir Henry,” he said in a low voice.

“What is it, Barrymore? You look as though you’ve seen the Hound itself.”

“It is my wife, sir. She is in a terrible state. It is about her brother, the convict. Selden, sir.”

Sir Henry put down his coffee cup. “Good heavens, man, what about him? Has he been caught?”

“Worse than that, sir. Much worse. He is dead.”

I sprang to my feet. “Dead! How?”

“He was found this morning on the moor, sir. Not a half a mile from the Black Tor. His neck was broken.”

Sir Henry stared at the butler, his face a mask of horror. “His neck broken? But that means… that means he fell from the tor?”

“That is what the constabulary believe, sir. That he was trying to climb down in the darkness and the storm, and that he lost his footing. But there is something else, Sir Henry. Something strange.” Barrymore lowered his voice still further, and his eyes darted nervously towards the window, as if he feared to be overheard by the moor itself. “The man who found him, a sheep-farmer from near Long Down, he said that at first he thought it was you, sir.”

“Me!” cried Sir Henry. “What in God’s name put that into his head?”

I stood frozen, the pieces of the puzzle clicking into place in my mind with a dreadful and terrifying speed. The stolen boot. The reason was suddenly, horribly clear. It was not to track Sir Henry. It was to give his scent to something. To a dog.

“The Hound,” I breathed. “It was after Selden. It mistook him for you.”

Sir Henry looked from my face to the butler’s and back again. The colour had drained from his own, and his hand, when he reached for the telegram on the mantelpiece, was trembling. He saw it now, all of it. He saw the pattern which he had refused to see before. The warning, the dog, the stolen clothes. It was a plot, a diabolical plot to murder him, and it had only failed because the brute had been put on the wrong scent. Selden, the wretched convict, had died in his place.

“My God, Watson,” he whispered, his eyes wide with a dawning terror. “My God, you were right. There is a devil on this moor, and he is after me.”

His bravado was gone, shattered in an instant. He saw at last the precipice upon which he had been so happily standing. The enemy was not a legend, not a phantom, but a real and cunning murderer who used a beast of hell as his weapon. And this enemy, this schemer, who was he?

My mind leaped at once to the one person whose actions had been suspicious from the first, the one man who had a reason to be on the moor at all hours, the one man who had shown a strange and violent opposition to Sir Henry’s presence.

“Stapleton?” Sir Henry repeated, his voice full of a horrified incredulity. “But he is my friend! He has given his blessing to Beryl and me. It cannot be. Why would he do such a thing?”

“I do not know why,” I answered, my own mind reeling. “But consider the facts. He is the one person who could have learned your London address and sent the warning. The logic was cold and inescapable. Sir Henry sank into a chair, his head in his hands. “But Beryl,” he groaned. “His sister. She would not be a party to such a thing. She loves me, Watson, I know she does.”

And so the plan was laid. I went myself to the little post office at Grimpen and dispatched a carefully worded telegram to my friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, at his Baker Street address. I did not, of course, sign my own name, but used a cipher which he would understand, begging him to send Inspector Lestrade, if he could be spared, on the next available train. The rest of the afternoon passed with a slowness that was a torture to our strained nerves. I made Sir Henry lie down, for he was to need all his fortitude for the ordeal to come. For my own part, I busied myself with our preparations. I took out my service revolver, a trusty Webley, and cleaned and oiled it with the greatest care. I insisted that Sir Henry take his own hunting rifle, a heavy express model capable of stopping a charging buffalo, though I had to argue with him at length before he would consent to carry it to a dinner party. We settled at last that he should leave it, concealed in a thicket, at the point where the moor path diverged from the road, to be picked up on his homeward journey.

As the hour of his departure drew near, a profound gloom settled over the baronet. The thought of the part he was to play, of smiling in the face of the man who was plotting his death, and of speaking pleasantries to the woman he adored while knowing her to be a prisoner, was almost more than his straightforward nature could bear.

“I do not know if I can do it, Watson,” he said, as he stood before the mirror adjusting his tie. His face in the glass was pale and haggard. “To see her, and to say nothing of this… to act a lie… it is beyond me.”

“You must do it for her sake,” I said quietly. “It is the only way to free her. Remember that, whatever he says, whatever you see, you are acting to save her life as well as your own.”

That thought seemed to lend him strength. He squared his shoulders, and a look of grim determination settled upon his features. He was every inch the noble heir of the Baskervilles as he turned to bid me farewell.

“Pray God this dreadful night is the end of it,” he said, and shaking my hand firmly, he walked out into the gathering twilight.

I watched him from the window until his figure was swallowed by the dusk, and then I was left to my own anxious vigil. The great house seemed preternaturally silent around me, each creak of a floorboard, each rattle of a casement in the wind, sounding as loud as a pistol shot in the stillness. I took up my post in Sir Henry’s study, the room which afforded the best view of the long, sweeping drive and the moor beyond. The night was dark, with a sliver of a moon that was frequently obscured by scudding clouds. A damp, chill wind had risen, and it carried with it the familiar, mournful sounds of the moor: the crying of the peewits and the distant, eerie booming of a bittern from the mire. From time to time I fancied that I could hear something else, a low, deep baying, but whether it was the wind in the chimneys or the veritable voice of the hound, I could not tell.

It must have been a little after nine o’clock when I heard the crunch of wheels upon the gravel of the drive. “Dr. Watson, is it not?” he said, peering at me in the dim light of the hall lantern. “I came as soon as I received the summons from Mr. Holmes. A wild goose chase, I’ll be bound, as usual. What is it this time? A lost kitten or a stolen tobacco pouch?”

“This is a bad business, Doctor,” Lestrade whispered, his voice tight with tension. “If the beast comes in this, we shall be firing at shadows.”

“No, Watson. It is addressed to another man. And the date is from three years before she ever came to England, before she supposedly met Stapleton. This is not his wife, Watson.”

“Then who…?”

“She is his wife,” Holmes corrected himself, his eyes alight with the fire of discovery. “But she was the wife of another man first. Stapleton did not find a young, unattached woman in Costa Rica. He found a married woman, Beryl Garcia, and lured her away from her husband. This is what he has been holding over her. This is the chain he uses to bind her to his will. She is not merely his accomplice; she is his captive, terrified that he will expose her as a bigamist and an adulteress to the man she wronged.”

Suddenly, the entire affair was cast in a new and more terrible light. The woman’s desperation, her warnings to Sir Henry, her submission to her brother’s, her husband’s, tyranny; it all made a dreadful kind of sense.

“We have what we need, Watson,” Holmes said, tucking the ledger and the letters inside his coat. “And we must be gone. Stapleton will have confronted the lovers by now and will be on his way back.”

We replaced the tin box, left the house as we had found it, and hurried back to our lookout point. We were just in time. The scene on the moor had reached its conclusion. We saw Stapleton striding back towards Merripit House, his face a mask of thunder. Miss Stapleton, or Mrs. Stapleton as I now knew her to be, was walking beside him, her head bowed. Sir Henry stood alone, watching them go, the very picture of dejection.

“Round two has gone to the villain,” Holmes murmured. “But the final round is yet to be fought. And now, Watson, we hold the winning cards.”

That evening, as we sat with the disconsolate baronet, a message was delivered from Merripit House. It was a note from Stapleton, addressed to Sir Henry. It was brief and chilling.

*‘Your attentions to my sister are unwelcome and must cease. For the sake of her reputation, which you seem so eager to compromise, I have instructed her to remain within the house. Should you attempt to approach her or this property again, you will find me a less forgiving man than you have hitherto. I suggest you consider a long sea voyage. The air in these parts is clearly not conducive to your health.’*

The threat, veiled as it was, was unmistakable. Sir Henry crumpled the note in his fist, his face pale with fury and despair. “The man is a fiend, Holmes! He plays with me as a cat plays with a mouse!”

“He does,” Holmes agreed calmly. “And his play is almost at an end. Watson, be so good as to fetch my violin. I find a little Bach helps to arrange the thoughts. Sir Henry, I will ask you to trust me for one more day. Tomorrow night, we shall draw the net, and you may be assured that the creature we catch will be a far more dangerous beast than any phantom hound.”

And so he sat, long into the night, the mournful, intricate melodies weaving through the old hall. I watched him, his eyes closed, his mind far away in that strange, cold country of logic and deduction where he lived. He was laying his final trap, and I pitied the man for whom it was intended. For when Sherlock Holmes set a snare, it was not a thing from which one could easily escape.

The final, lingering note of the violin seemed to hang in the air of the great hall long after Holmes had laid the instrument aside, a mournful echo that spoke of old sorrows and grim necessities. The fire had burned low in the hearth, and outside the wind soughed across the dark expanse of the moor, a fitting accompaniment to the music and to our thoughts. Sir Henry had retired some hours before, a man walking in a daze, his heart torn between anger and a grief so profound it had struck him dumb. He had placed his trust, and his life, in Holmes’s hands, and now there was nothing to be done but await the morning.

That morning broke grey and cheerless, with a low ceiling of cloud that seemed to press down upon the tors, making the world feel small and claustrophobic. We breakfasted in near silence, the clink of cutlery upon china sounding unnaturally loud in the solemn atmosphere of Baskerville Hall. Sir Henry picked at his food, his brow furrowed in thought, his eyes holding the haunted look of a man who has stared too long into the abyss.

It was Holmes who finally broke the spell. He pushed his chair back from the table and fixed the baronet with his keen, penetrating gaze.

“Sir Henry,” he began, his voice crisp and devoid of emotion, “the time for conjecture is past. The time for action is upon us. Tonight, we will bring this matter to a head, once and for all.”

The baronet looked up, a spark of his old fire returning to his eyes. “I am ready, Holmes. Only tell me what I must do. After Stapleton’s damnable note, I am prepared to believe him capable of anything. To hold that poor woman his prisoner, to threaten me from my own home—the man is a devil.”

“He is a cunning and resourceful adversary,” Holmes corrected, “which is a far more dangerous thing. He believes he has won. He thinks you are cowed, and that his path to the Baskerville fortune is clear. We must use that arrogance against him. My plan is simple in its design, though I will not disguise from you that it is fraught with peril.”

He paused, letting his words sink in. I felt a familiar thrill, a mixture of apprehension and excitement, that always preceded the final act of one of Holmes’s dramas.

“Stapleton has, in a sense, invited you to leave the moor,” Holmes continued. “You shall accept his invitation. You will announce your intention to travel to London on the night train from Coombe Tracey. You will dine with us as usual, but you will set out from here on foot after dinner, explaining that the walk will help to clear your head. You must take the path that leads directly across the moor, the very path that passes Merripit House.”

Sir Henry stared at him, aghast. “Walk past his house? Alone? At night? Good God, Holmes, that is suicide! It is precisely what he wants. He will set his dog on me!”

“Precisely,” Holmes said, his eyes glittering. “He will set his creature upon you, believing you to be alone and unprotected. He will have arranged his alibi; he will be at home, perhaps feigning some scientific work. When your body is found, it will be but another tragic chapter in the legend of your family. The hound of the Baskervilles will have claimed another victim.”

“And you wish me to walk into this trap?” cried the baronet, springing to his feet. “It is madness!”

“It is our only chance,” Holmes replied, his voice calm but firm as steel. “Do you imagine we will be far away? Do you think I would risk your life, which I have been charged to protect, without taking every precaution? Watson and I will be there, Sir Henry. We will be hidden. We will be armed. When the beast comes, we shall be ready for it. But for the trap to be sprung, the bait must be seen to be vulnerable. You are the bait.”

The colour drained from Sir Henry’s face, but he stood his ground. The courage that had taken his ancestors through centuries of war and strife had not deserted him. He looked from Holmes’s resolute face to my own, and I gave him what I hoped was a reassuring nod, though my own heart was thumping like a drum.

“And the lady?” he asked, his voice low. “Mrs. Stapleton… what of her?”

Holmes’s expression softened for a fraction of a second. “We have reason to believe that she is not Stapleton’s sister, but his wife, and that she is as much his victim as you are his target. By exposing him, we may yet save her. It is for her sake, as much as for your own, that you must play this part.”

That was the stroke that won him. The thought of rescuing the woman he loved gave him the strength that the prospect of saving his own life could not. He sank back into his chair, his shoulders slumped for a moment, then he squared them and looked up, a new resolve hardening his features.

“Very well,” he said. “I will do it. I will walk the path. Just be sure, for God’s sake, that your aim is true.”

“You may rely upon it,” Holmes said simply. “Now, we must make our arrangements. A telegram must be sent to London, which you will ostensibly write, booking your rooms. A message must be conveyed to Stapleton, indirectly, of your imminent departure. A chance word to Mrs. Barrymore will see to that; the gossip of the servants’ hall travels faster than any wire.”

The rest of the day passed in a blur of tense anticipation. Sir Henry went about his part with a grim determination that I could not but admire. He despatched the telegram, he packed a valise, and he spoke loudly at luncheon of his urgent need to return to the capital. I watched him, and I felt a profound sense of shame that we were using our friend in this cold blooded fashion, yet I knew, as Holmes did, that there was no other way.

As dusk began to settle, casting long, purple shadows from the ancient stones of the Hall, Holmes motioned for me to join him in his room. He had laid out two heavy revolvers on the bed, along with a box of cartridges.

“Our old friend, Watson,” he said, handing one of the weapons to me. “I trust you have not lost your skill with it. There will be no room for error tonight. We are dealing not with a phantom, but with a beast of flesh and blood, albeit one of monstrous size and ferocity. And we are dealing with a man whose cunning is matched only by his utter depravity.”

“Where shall we lie in wait, Holmes?” I asked, checking the mechanism of my pistol.

“There is a spot I have marked, where the path dips between two small tors. It is perhaps a quarter of a mile from Merripit House, far enough that he will not expect an ambush, yet close enough for us to intervene. We will have a clear line of sight to the path as it comes over the rise. Sir Henry will be silhouetted against the sky. The creature will come from the direction of the house, from the darkness of the mire.”

We left the Hall by a rear door, unseen, and made our way out onto the moor. The sun had now vanished, leaving behind a sky of bruised orange and violet. A chill wind had sprung up, whispering through the dry heather and rattling the reeds in the marshy ground. The great, empty landscape, which had seemed so beautiful in the sunshine, now resumed its ancient, menacing character. It was a place of secrets, a place where dark things could happen.

We found the spot Holmes had chosen, a hollow nestled amongst a cluster of granite boulders weathered into fantastic shapes by the wind and rain. From here, we had a perfect, if limited, view of the path. We settled down to wait, crouching behind the cold stone, the metal of our revolvers chilling our hands.

The minutes crawled by like hours. Darkness fell, not gently, but like a black cloak thrown over the world. A sliver of moon gave a faint, ghostly light, just enough to distinguish the black ribbon of the path from the dark mass of the moor around it. Every sound was magnified: the cry of a night bird, the rustle of some small creature in the bracken, the ceaseless moan of the wind. My nerves were stretched to the breaking point.

“He should be here by now, Holmes,” I whispered, my voice hoarse.

“Patience, Watson. He is a brave man, but he is walking towards what he believes may be his death. We must allow him a few minutes’ delay.”

As he spoke, a new sound drifted towards us on the wind. It was a low, rhythmic pulsing, a deep thrumming that seemed to come from the very ground beneath our feet. It was the sound of a great engine, I thought for a moment, but that was impossible out here. Then I recognised it.

“Holmes,” I breathed, “the fog.”

He needed no telling. Over the ridge to our left, a great bank of white vapour was rolling towards us, a silent, spectral tide. It was as thick as cotton wool, and it swallowed the landscape as it came, erasing the tors, the path, the very sky itself.

“Damn it!” Holmes hissed, a rare curse escaping his lips. “This complicates matters. We shall lose our visibility. Watson, if the fog closes in before he passes, we must move. We will advance along the path towards him. We cannot let him face it alone in this.”

But we had no time. The fog rolled in with uncanny speed, enveloping us in its damp, cold embrace. The world shrank to a circle of a few yards around our hiding place. The faint moonlight was choked off, and we were plunged into an almost absolute blackness, adrift in a sea of white. The sense of isolation was complete and terrifying. I could hear Holmes’s sharp, quick breathing beside me, and it was the only thing that anchored me to reality.

And then we heard it.

It began as a low baying, a sound so deep and resonant it seemed to vibrate in my bones. It was the sound I had heard once before on the moor, the sound that had chilled my blood then and turned it to ice now. But this time it was closer, much closer. It was the cry of a hunter closing in on its prey.

The baying rose in pitch and volume, swelling into a demonic howl that echoed across the shrouded moor. It was answered by a human shout, a cry of pure terror that was abruptly cut off. It was Sir Henry.

“Now, Watson, now!” yelled Holmes, and he sprang from our hiding place, his revolver held high.

I scrambled after him, my heart hammering against my ribs. We stumbled out of the hollow and onto the path, but we could see nothing. The fog was a solid wall in every direction. The only guide we had was the hellish clamour of the hound, which now sounded as if it were almost upon us.

We ran blindly forward into the white abyss. Then, through the swirling vapour, a shape materialised. For a single, frozen instant, I saw it, and the sight will be burned into my memory for all my days.

It was a hound, yes, but such a hound as nightmares are made of. It was enormous, as big as a young calf, its coat a matted, unwholesome black. But it was the head, the eyes, the jaws that were the focus of the horror. Its eyes glowed with a spectral, green light, and from its slavering, open mouth, lined with dripping fangs, there issued puffs of what looked like smoke and flame. Its entire muzzle and chest were smeared with a shimmering, phosphorescent substance that gave the beast the appearance of a demon spat forth from the bowels of the earth.

It was hurtling along the path, not after Sir Henry, but directly towards us. For a horrifying second I realised our mistake. We had run towards the sound, and now we stood between the beast and its intended victim, who must be somewhere behind us on the path.

“Holmes!” I cried, raising my pistol, but my hand was shaking so badly I could scarcely aim.

Holmes was made of sterner stuff. He stood his ground, a dark, motionless figure in the fog. As the glowing beast lunged, he fired. The shot rang out, a deafening crack in the night. A terrible, agonized scream, half animal, half human, tore through the air, and the great hound seemed to leap upwards, its phosphorescent jaws snapping at the empty air. It twisted in a great convulsion and fell to the ground with a heavy thud, where it lay writhing and howling, its spectral light flickering and dying.

I rushed forward and, without thinking, placed the muzzle of my revolver to the creature’s blazing head and pulled the trigger. The howling stopped. A dreadful silence fell, broken only by our own ragged breathing and the acrid smell of gunpowder and some foul, chemical stench from the dead beast.

“Have you a match, Watson?” Holmes’s voice was strained, but steady.

I fumbled in my pocket and struck one. In the small, flickering light, the phantom was revealed for what it was. A dog, certainly, one of immense size and power, a mix of bloodhound and mastiff. But it was no spirit. Its coat was matted with sticky pitch, and smeared over this was a crude paste of phosphorus, the source of the ghastly light. The “flame” from its jaws was nothing more than the effect of this ghastly paint on its breath in the cold night air. It was a monstrous artifice, a trick, but one that had nearly cost our friend his life.

“Where is Sir Henry?” I cried, looking wildly around in the fog.

“There!”

Holmes pointed. A dark shape was lying on the path some twenty yards behind where we stood. We ran to it. It was the baronet. He lay on his face, his hands covering his head, moaning in sheer, abject terror. It took both of us to haul him to his feet. He was shaking uncontrollably, his face the colour of chalk.

“It is all right, Sir Henry,” Holmes said, his voice gentle. “It is over. The beast is dead.”

He stared at the huge, dark carcass on the path, its baleful glow now extinguished. A great, shuddering sob escaped him, and he leaned heavily upon my arm. “My God,” he whispered. “My God, what was that thing?”

“It was what we came to find,” said Holmes. “And now, we must find its master.” He turned, his face a grim mask in the gloom. “Stapleton. He will not be far.”

He cast about on the ground like a hound himself, searching for tracks in the damp earth. The fog was beginning to thin a little, breaking up into great, drifting swathes.

“He would have followed the hound, to ensure his work was done,” Holmes muttered, more to himself than to us. “He would have taken the shortest path back to Merripit House. Through the mire.”

A new sound reached us then, a faint cry carried on the wind from the direction of the great Grimpen Mire. It was a cry of despair, thin and high and horribly human.

“This way!” Holmes commanded, and began to run, not along the path, but out into the treacherous, broken ground that led towards the dark heart of the moor.

Sir Henry, though still shaken, found a reserve of strength. “The villain,” he growled, and he plunged after Holmes, with me close at his heels.

We navigated the treacherous ground, leaping from one grassy tussock to the next, the ground quaking beneath our feet. The fog swirled around us, at times revealing a glimpse of the path ahead, at others closing in and leaving us to navigate by instinct and by the memory of that terrible cry. Another shout came, weaker this time, and then a dreadful, gurgling sound that was cut short. Then, silence. A silence more profound and more terrifying than all the noise that had come before.

We stopped at the edge of a great, green scummy pool, one of the many bottomless pits that dotted the mire. The surface was still, but a few bubbles rose slowly and popped, releasing a foul-smelling gas. Something had been disturbed. Holmes shone his pocket lantern across the surface, and the beam picked out something half submerged near the far bank. It was a stick with a green net attached. A butterfly net.

“He is gone,” Holmes said, his voice flat. “In his haste, blinded by the fog and by his rage at the failure of his plan, he must have missed the path he knew so well. The mire has claimed him, as he intended it to claim Sir Henry. There can be no more fitting a grave.”

We stood for a moment in silence, contemplating the awful justice of the scene. The man who had used the moor as his weapon had been slain by it.

Our work, however, was not yet done. We turned back, our first thoughts for the living. We helped the exhausted baronet back to the Hall, where the lights burned like a welcoming beacon in the darkness. Then, without a moment’s rest, Holmes and I set out once more, our destination Merripit House.

We found the front door unlocked. The house was silent and dark. We moved from room to room, until in an upper chamber, we found her. She was not tied up, but she was a prisoner nonetheless. She sat in a chair, rigid with fear, her eyes wide and staring. At the sight of us, she gave a little cry, but when she saw Holmes’s face, a flicker of hope, of recognition, dawned in her eyes.

“Mr. Holmes?” she whispered.

“It is over, madam,” he said gently. “You are safe. Your husband will not trouble you, or anyone, ever again.”

The story she told us then, in broken sobs and whispers, filled in the last of the gaps. Stapleton, returning from setting the hound loose, had been in a state of maniacal fury. He had realised from the sound of the pistol shots that his plan had miscarried. He had burst into the house, told her that he was sure Holmes and Watson were on the moor, and had cursed her for, as he saw it, her treachery. He had bound and gagged her, fearing she would warn us, before rushing out into the fog to see for himself what had happened. It was the last she ever saw of him.

“He told me,” she choked, “he told me that whatever happened, he would drag me down with him. He was a fiend.”

We stayed with her until the dawn, which came at last, clear and bright, washing the moor clean of its shadows. The long night was over. The curse of the Baskervilles was finally and forever laid to rest.

“Well, Watson, you look as thunder-struck as I was!” cried Sir Henry, clapping me on the shoulder as he strode back into the hall, his face now as radiant as it had been dejected a short hour ago. “It is all right, man! The fellow is a gentleman after all, if a trifle hot-headed.”

“Healed?” I echoed, scarcely able to credit my ears. “After what I witnessed? Sir Henry, the man was like a lunatic!”

“And for that he has apologised most profusely,” said the baronet, pouring himself a stiff brandy and waving the decanter in my direction, an offer which I declined. “It seems, Watson, that we have misjudged him. He is not a madman, merely a brother who is, perhaps, excessively protective of his only living relative.”

He went on to recount the substance of their interview, and I must confess that Stapleton had played his part with a cunning that I found myself grudgingly admiring. He had presented himself not as a tyrant, but as a guardian. He spoke, it seems, of a previous attachment his sister had formed in their former life in South America; an attachment to a scoundrel who had trifled with her affections and left her heartbroken. This past sorrow had rendered her fragile, he claimed, and he, Stapleton, had sworn to shield her from any further emotional turmoil. His outburst on the moor was, he explained, the reflexive action of a man who saw what he thought was a casual flirtation that might lead to a repeat of that tragedy.

“He thought I was merely playing the part of the landed baronet, toying with a simple country girl,” Sir Henry explained, his voice earnest. “When I assured him, in no uncertain terms, that my intentions were entirely honourable and that my dearest wish was to make his sister my wife, his entire demeanour changed. He wrung my hand, Watson, and begged my pardon for his hasty and ill-formed conclusions.”

“And he now consents to your suit?” I asked, my own suspicions far from allayed.

“He does, with one condition,” said Sir Henry, his enthusiasm momentarily checked. “And I must say, Watson, given the circumstances he described, I find it a perfectly reasonable one. He has asked for a period of time, a sort of probation, if you will. He wishes for me to continue my acquaintance with his sister, but to refrain from any immediate talk of marriage or of the future. He wants to be certain, and for her to be certain, that my affections are constant and true before any formal engagement is announced. Three months, he suggested. For three months I am to be a suitor, but a patient one.”

I stared at my friend in amazement. The sheer audacity of the scheme was breathtaking. Stapleton, having failed to drive Sir Henry away by overt hostility, had now devised a means to chain him to the moor for a full three months, a prisoner of his own honourable intentions. It was a trap, as clear to me as the granite of the tors, yet one so cleverly baited with the promise of love that its victim not only walked into it willingly, but did so with a song in his heart.

“My dear Sir Henry,” I said, choosing my words with care, “does this not strike you as somewhat… contrived? To grant you leave to court his sister, but to place such a curious moratorium upon the very object of that courtship?”

“Not at all, Watson!” he boomed, his good cheer impenetrable. “It shows the man cares! It shows he is not some social climber eager to marry his sister off to a title. He is testing my character, and I for my-part am happy to prove it to him. And to her! Now, all this drama has given me a monstrous appetite. What say you to ringing for Mrs. Barrymore and seeing what she can find for us in the larder?”

I could say no more. To argue further would be to cast doubt not only upon Stapleton’s sincerity, but upon Miss Stapleton’s honour and my friend’s own judgement. I was his guardian, not his gaoler, and he would not thank me for placing bars upon the cage when he himself believed it to be an open meadow. And so, I held my peace, but that night I wrote a long and detailed letter to Holmes, recounting every particular of the strange day’s events, from the angry scene on the moor path to the subsequent, and to my mind far more sinister, reconciliation. My heart was heavy with foreboding, and I felt more than ever the oppressive weight of the responsibility which I had undertaken.

The days that followed settled into a strange and frustrating routine. Sir Henry, true to his word, entered into his period of “probation” with gusto. Scarcely a day passed that he did not walk or ride over to Merripit House, and often I would accompany him, my presence being, I believe, a source of some comfort to him and, I fancied, a check upon the absolute freedom of action that Stapleton might have wished.

Our visits were, on the surface, pleasant enough. Stapleton was the model of a genial host, discoursing with enthusiasm upon his botanical specimens or the archaeological curiosities of the district. He would lead us to his collection of lepidoptera, pointing out a rare Camberwell Beauty or a Clouded Yellow, his light eyes gleaming with an intellectual fervour that was, I had to admit, quite compelling. Yet I could never shake the feeling that one of those eyes was always fixed upon the baronet and his sister. He was the chaperon, ever present, ever smiling, and a more effective warder could not have been devised.

As for the lady, she was a study in quiet anxiety. Her beauty, which had so captivated our friend, seemed to me to be shadowed by a perpetual and deep-seated fear. When Sir Henry spoke to her, her replies were often brief and her eyes would flicker towards her brother, as if seeking permission even to breathe. Once, as we walked in the garden, Sir Henry pointed out a particularly fine bloom, and for an instant her face lit up with a genuine smile. Then she caught Stapleton’s gaze from the study window, and the light in her eyes was extinguished as quickly as a candle flame in a draught. She murmured some excuse about the chill and hurried back indoors.

“She is shy, Watson, is she not?” Sir Henry remarked to me on our walk home. “A delicate, sensitive soul. It is no wonder her brother is so protective.”

I saw not shyness, but terror. I saw a woman living under a terrible duress, sending out desperate signals that my friend, in his infatuation, was hopelessly misinterpreting. On one occasion, she pressed a folded piece of paper into my own hand as we were taking our leave. My heart leapt, for I thought that here at last was the message of warning I had been expecting. I concealed it until I was out of sight of the house, and then opened it with trembling fingers. It contained but a single, cryptic sentence: “The wind on the tors sings a sad song for those who will not listen.” I showed it to Sir Henry, who laughed it off as a piece of romantic poetry. To me, it sounded very much like another desperate, cloaked attempt to bid him flee.

It was shortly after this that a new and unnerving element introduced itself into our lives at the Hall. It began one evening as Sir Henry and I sat before the fire, sharing a post-prandial glass of port. The wind was high that night, howling in the old chimneys and rattling the window-panes in their leaded frames. Amid the general cacophony of the gale, I became aware of a new sound, a low, mournful, ululating cry that seemed to drift across the moor.

“Did you hear that?” I asked, holding up a hand for silence.

Sir Henry, who had been staring into the flames, looked up. “Hear what? The wind?”

“No, it was something more. Listen.”

We sat in silence, and a moment later it came again, fainter this time, but unmistakable. It was a sound of profound and chilling sorrow, a long, drawn-out wail that rose and fell with an almost human cadence. It was not the baying of a hound, not the cry of any animal I could name. It was eerie, ethereal, and it sent a shiver down my spine.

“Good heavens, what is that?” whispered the baronet, his face paling.

“Some trick of the wind in the rocks, I imagine,” I said, with a confidence I was far from feeling. “The tors are full of strange acoustic effects.”

But the sound persisted, coming and going through the night, a ghostly lament that haunted the dark hours. The next day, I made some enquiries of Dr. Mortimer when he called upon us. He smiled in his amiable, scientific way.

“Ah, you have heard the Whispering Woman of the Waste,” he said. “It is an old legend hereabouts. They say it is the ghost of a mother whose child was lost in the mire centuries ago. She wanders the moor on windy nights, forever calling his name. A fanciful tale, of course. As you suspect, Dr. Watson, it is merely the wind vibrating through a particular fissure in the rocks on Vixen Tor. A natural phenomenon, but one that has spooked many a newcomer to Dartmoor.”

A natural phenomenon. Perhaps. But its timing was peculiar, and I could not dismiss the thought that Stapleton, with his intimate knowledge of the moor, might know of such a place and such a legend. What better way to play upon the nerves of a man already living under the shadow of a family curse? Another piece of the psychological puzzle, designed to unman Sir Henry, to make him prey to suggestion and to fear. I added this new development to my next report to Baker Street.

Sir Henry’s own nerves were, I could see, beginning to fray. The strain of the fruitless courtship, the constant oppressive presence of Stapleton, and now this nightly, spectral wailing were taking their toll. His hearty laugh was heard less often in the halls, and a perpetual frown of worry was etched upon his brow. He grew impatient, and it was this impatience that drove him finally to an act of supreme folly.

“I cannot bear it any longer, Watson,” he burst out one morning, flinging his napkin down upon the breakfast table. “This farce of a courtship! I see her, but I cannot speak to her. I am near her, but we are a world apart. Her brother is a wall between us. I feel her eyes on me, pleading, but for what I do not know. I must speak with her alone! I must and I will.”

“And how do you propose to do that?” I asked tiredly. “Stapleton sticks to her like a limpet.”

“I have arranged it,” he said, his voice low and determined. “Yesterday, whilst you were examining that stone circle with Stapleton, I had a moment. Just a moment. I told her to meet me. Tonight. At eleven o’t clock. By the old yew arch on the path to the house.”

I sprang to my feet. “Sir Henry, are you mad? You cannot! It is a trap! Can you not see that this is precisely what he wants? To lure you out onto the moor at night, alone?”

“I will not be alone, for she will be with me,” he retorted stubbornly. “And it is not a trap. It is a desperate plea from a woman in distress. I saw it in her eyes, Watson. She agreed. She will be there. I must know the truth. I must know if her heart is her own, or if she is merely the puppet you believe her to be. And no, I will not be dissuaded.”

My protests were in vain. He was a man driven by love and by exasperation, a dangerous combination. He was deaf to reason. I knew then that my own duty was clear. I could not stop him from going, but I would not let him go alone.

That evening, a thick, white fog, so characteristic of the region, began to roll in from the low-lying mire, blanketing the landscape in a damp and impenetrable shroud. It was a night for dark deeds. At half past ten, Sir Henry slipped from the house, a heavy walking-stick in his hand and his face set with determination. Ten minutes later, I followed, my revolver cold and heavy in my coat pocket.

I kept a safe distance behind him, my footsteps muffled by the grass at the edge of the path. The fog was so dense that his form was but a slightly darker shadow in the gloom ahead. He moved cautiously, and I followed suit, my senses on high alert. The world was reduced to a few yards of visibility, the air cold and clammy, tasting of wet earth and decay. The silence was absolute, save for the drip of condensation from the gorse bushes and the distant, mournful cry of the so-called Whispering Woman, which had begun its nightly lament.

I saw Sir Henry pause as he reached the familiar branching of the paths. He was near the arranged meeting spot. I concealed myself behind a granite boulder, peering out into the swirling whiteness, my heart thumping against my ribs. He stood there for what seemed an eternity, a lonely figure waiting in a sea of fog.

Then a new sound broke the silence. It was not the light footstep of a lady. It was a clumsy, stumbling tread, accompanied by a harsh, guttural breathing. A figure blundered out of the mist, not from the direction of Merripit House, but from the moor itself. It was a man, ragged and unkempt, his face smeared with dirt. For a horrifying instant, I thought the hound had a human accomplice, but then I recognised the feral, hunted look in the man’s eyes. It was Selden, the escaped convict.

He saw Sir Henry and froze, his mouth falling open in a silent gasp of surprise. Sir Henry, equally startled, raised his stick. And then it came.

It began as a low growl that vibrated through the very soles of my boots, a sound of such profound menace that the blood in my veins turned to ice. The convict spun around, his face a mask of primal terror. The growl swelled into a deep, baying roar that was not of this world. It was the sound I had heard in my nightmares, the embodiment of the curse.

Out of the fog it came. I saw it only for a second, but the image was burned forever upon my mind. It was a thing of immense size, a vast, black beast that seemed to shimmer with a ghostly, internal fire. Its eyes glowed with a hellish, red light, and its jaws, dripping with a phosphorescent saliva, gaped open in a silent snarl.

It was not heading for Sir Henry. Its terrible eyes were fixed upon the other man, the running man. Selden gave a single, choked scream and tried to flee, scrambling back the way he had come. The creature lunged, covering the ground in two prodigious bounds. I saw it rise up, a black shape against the white fog. There was a sickening sound of tearing cloth and breaking bone, a brief, agonised cry, and then a dreadful silence, broken only by a wet, worrying sound.

Sir Henry stood as if turned to stone, his face a ghostly white in the gloom. The beast, its work done, raised its head, let out another of its soul-shaking bays, and then melted back into the fog as swiftly and silently as it had appeared.

I ran to my friend, who was swaying on his feet. “The hound!” he croaked, his voice a hoarse whisper. “The hound of the Baskervilles! It is real, Watson. The curse is real.”

I grabbed his arm, my own hand shaking uncontrollably. “We must get back to the house. Now, Sir Henry! Now!”

He was in a daze, but he allowed me to lead him away from that terrible place. As we passed the spot where the convict had fallen, a flicker of morbid curiosity made me cast the beam of my pocket-lantern towards the ground. The body lay in a twisted heap. The man was wearing a rough tweed suit. My blood ran cold as I saw that it was the very suit that Sir Henry had discarded, the one Barrymore had given to his brother-in-law. For a moment, I thought… but no, a glance at the matted beard and brutal features confirmed it was the convict. The hound, drawn by the scent of Sir Henry’s clothing, had claimed the wrong victim.

We half-ran, half-stumbled back to the Hall. I bundled the baronet into a chair, forced a large glass of brandy into his trembling hand, and watched as some semblance of life returned to his eyes. His bravado was gone, shattered. In its place was the stark, abiding terror of a man who has looked into the face of his own doom and knows that he has only been granted a temporary reprieve.

My own mind was racing. The trap had been sprung, but the wrong prey had been caught. Stapleton’s plan had failed, but only by chance. He had revealed his hand. The hound was not a spectre, but a creature of flesh and blood, monstrous and deadly, its terrifying appearance doubtless enhanced by some devilish trickery with phosphorus.

There was no more time for observation, for conjecture, for reporting from a distance. The game was afoot in deadly earnest now. I strode to the writing desk in the study, my course of action finally, terribly clear. I took up a telegraph form and, with a hand that was now perfectly steady, I wrote out the message that I should have sent weeks ago.

I glanced at Sir Henry, who had not moved from the great oaken chair into which I had thrust him. His glass was empty, but his eyes were still fixed upon some dreadful point in the middle distance, and his face, illuminated by the single lamp I had lit, was a mask of waxy horror. He could not be left alone in such a state, and yet the urgency of my appeal to Holmes felt paramount. The two needs warred within me.

“Barrymore,” I called, my voice sharper than I intended.

The butler appeared in the doorway almost at once, his long, melancholy face even more drawn than usual. He had heard our frantic return, the slamming of the great door, and had hovered since in the shadows of the hall, a silent and anxious sentinel.

“Sir?” he asked, his gaze flickering from me to the petrified figure of his master.

“Sir Henry has had a grave shock upon the moor. The convict, Selden, is dead.”

Barrymore’s stoic composure shattered for an instant. His hand flew to his mouth, and a low gasp escaped him. “Dead? Lord have mercy. Not… not the hound?”

“I cannot say for certain what it was,” I prevaricated, seeing no need to inflict the full and ghastly truth upon the entire household. “But the man is dead. I must get a telegram to London at once. I am leaving Sir Henry in your care. On no account is he to be left alone. Stay with him here, in this room. Bolt the door after me, and do not open it to anyone but myself. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Dr. Watson. Perfectly, sir.” He stepped into the room, his expression a mixture of fear and resolute duty, and cast a look of profound pity at the baronet.

I clapped a hand on Sir Henry’s shoulder. “I will not be long,” I said, my voice softer now. “Barrymore will sit with you. You are safe within these walls.”

He gave a slight, convulsive shudder but did not turn his head. His spirit, it seemed, was still out there on the dark moor, standing over the mangled body of the man who had died in his place.

Leaving them together, I took up my revolver from the table where I had laid it, checked that it was loaded, and slipped it into the pocket of my coat. I let myself out of the hall, the sound of Barrymore sliding the heavy bolt into its socket echoing behind me. The night was as black and as threatening as before. The fog had thinned somewhat, but a cold, damp wind now swept across the uplands, carrying with it the mournful sigh of the sedge and the distant, dreary cry of a curlew. Every rustle in the heather, every shadow that danced in the periphery of my vision, seemed to take on a sinister form. My nerves were stretched to the breaking point, and I confess that my hand strayed more than once to the reassuring butt of the pistol in my pocket as I strode swiftly down the long, dark drive.

The miles to Coombe Tracey seemed to stretch into an eternity. I was a man fleeing from a nightmare, yet at the same time I felt as if I were plunging deeper into it with every step. The road was a grey ribbon unwinding into blackness, flanked by the low, brooding shapes of the tors. My imagination, inflamed by the horrors I had witnessed, peopled the darkness with lurking menaces. I saw the glint of Stapleton’s spectacles in every patch of moonlight on wet granite; I heard the pad of a gigantic hound in the rustle of the wind through the gorse. It was with a profound sense of relief that I finally saw the scattered lights of the little town below me.

I roused the sleepy station-master, who also managed the telegraph office. He looked at me with frank curiosity as I paid for the urgent transmission, his eyes lingering on my mud-spattered trousers and the wildness which must have been apparent in my expression. I offered no explanation, waiting only for his assurance that the message was on its way before I turned on my heel and began the dreadful journey back.

My return was no less fraught with tension. The wind had risen to a keening gale that shrieked and gibbered amongst the rocks, and I had to lean into it as I breasted the hills. It felt as though the very spirit of that malevolent place was attempting to drive me back, to keep me from the house which it had marked for its own. I did not slacken my pace, however, spurred on by the image of Sir Henry’s shattered face and the knowledge that, until Holmes arrived, I was his sole protector.

When at last I reached the avenue of Baskerville Hall, the great house loomed before me, its mullioned windows dark save for that single square of light in the study. It looked less like a home than a fortress under siege. My knock was answered almost immediately by Barrymore, who unbarred the door with a haste that spoke of his own vigil.

“Thank God you have returned, sir,” he whispered, his face haggard in the lamplight. “It has been a long hour. The master has not spoken a word.”

I found Sir Henry just as I had left him, though the decanter of brandy at his elbow was now considerably lower. I dismissed the butler and took the seat opposite my friend, leaning forward into the light.

“Sir Henry,” I said gently. “You must rally yourself. We have work to do.”

He slowly raised his head, and the eyes that met mine were no longer merely terrified; they were filled with a profound and hopeless despair. “It’s no use, Watson,” he said, his voice a dead monotone. “You saw it. You saw the beast. The curse is true. My fate is sealed, as was my father’s, and his father’s before him.”

“It is not a curse,” I retorted with a force I did not entirely feel. “What we saw was a creature of flesh and blood. A hound, yes, but a mortal one. Large, savage, but a mortal beast trained to a devilish purpose. Stapleton is its master. There is no phantom from hell in this, only the cold-blooded villainy of a man.”

“A man who coats his hound in hellfire and sets it upon my family?” he countered, a bitter, broken laugh escaping him. “Call it what you will, Watson. It comes for me. It tasted my scent tonight, and it will not be satisfied until it has my life. It missed me once. It will not miss me again.”

“It will not get the chance,” I said, rising and walking to the window. I drew the heavy velvet curtains, shutting out the oppressive darkness of the moor. “I have sent for Holmes. He will be here by tomorrow, I have no doubt. Until he comes, you will not set foot outside this house. We will see to every door and window. No one enters, and you do not leave. Not for any reason. Is that understood?”

He nodded mutely, like a man accepting a prison sentence. “And then?” he asked. “When Holmes comes? What can he do against such a thing?”

“He will do what he always does,” I answered, trying to inject my own faith in my friend into his wavering spirit. “He will see the truth, and he will set a trap for the trapper. Stapleton has shown his hand, Sir Henry. He has made his move and failed. Now, it is our turn.”

We sat in silence for a long time after that, the only sounds being the howl of the wind outside and the ticking of the great clock in the hall. I persuaded Sir Henry to eat a little of the cold supper Barrymore brought to us, and then I saw him to his room. I inspected his window, ensuring the heavy wooden shutters were barred, and I locked his door from the outside, placing the key in my own pocket. My own room was adjacent to his, and I lay down upon my bed without undressing, my revolver on the nightstand beside me, listening for any sound, any hint of a threat. But the night passed without incident, and when the first grey light of dawn crept through my window, I felt that we had weathered the first stage of the siege.

The morning brought with it a fragile sense of normalcy. The sun shone with a watery brightness, and the moor, viewed from the dining-room window, seemed almost benign, its autumn colours of russet and gold washed clean by the night’s wind. Sir Henry joined me for breakfast. He was pale and subdued, but the abject terror of the previous night had receded, leaving behind a grim and weary resignation. He spoke little, but he ate, and I took that as a hopeful sign.

We had just finished our meal when a footman entered with a letter upon a silver tray.

“For you, Sir Henry,” the man said. “A boy has just brought it from Merripit House.”

My eyes met the baronet’s. A message from Stapleton. My blood ran cold at the audacity of it. I took the letter from his unresisting fingers and broke the seal. The handwriting was small, precise, and curiously agitated. I read it aloud.

“My dear Sir Henry, I trust you slept well after your excursion upon the moor. I was most distressed to learn from one of the local farmers this morning of the sad end of the escaped convict. It is a grim business, and it serves as another potent reminder that the moor is no place for any man to be wandering after nightfall. I hope the news has not unsettled you unduly. My sister and I were thinking of you, and she joins me in extending an invitation. We should be very glad if you would dine with us this evening. A quiet meal between friends may serve to distract you from these morbid events. We should expect you at eight o’tclock, if you are so good as to accept. Yours very sincerely, Jack Stapleton.”

I lowered the letter, a feeling of revulsion and a kind of horrified admiration rising within me. The man was a consummate actor. Not a word of his own part in the night’s terrible drama. Only the solicitous enquiry, the friendly invitation, the pretence of shared concern. It was a spider inviting the fly into his parlour, and the spider did not even know that we had watched him spin his web the night before.

“The unmitigated villain!” I exclaimed. “The infernal hypocrite!”

Sir Henry stared at the letter as if it were a venomous snake. “He wants me to go there, Watson. To his house. At night. Across the moor.”

“It is a trap, of course,” I said. “He failed to get you in the open, so now he means to lure you to his own ground, to the very edge of the Grimpen Mire. We shall, naturally, send our sincerest regrets.” I moved towards the writing desk to pen a refusal.

“Wait,” said Sir Henry, a strange new light in his eyes. The despair seemed to be burning away, replaced by a slow, smouldering anger. “He thinks me a fool, does he? He thinks I’ll walk into his snare like a witless sheep?”

“His arrogance is his weakness,” I observed. “He believes we suspect nothing.”

“Exactly,” Sir Henry said, his voice growing stronger. “He believes I am still the unsuspecting newcomer, perhaps a little shaken by local legends, but nothing more. He doesn’t know that you saw him, Watson. He doesn’t know we saw the hound.” He stood up and began to pace the room, his dejection replaced by a febrile energy. “What if I were to accept his invitation?”

“Are you mad?” I cried, aghast. “That is precisely what he wants! It would be suicide.”

“Not if you were with me,” he shot back, his face flushed. “Not if we were prepared. You said it yourself, Watson. It is our turn to make a move. He has set a trap for me. What if we were to set a trap for him?”

I stared at him, torn between admiration for his returning courage and alarm at his recklessness. The plan had a certain desperate appeal, a directness which suited Sir Henry’s nature, but it was fraught with peril. We would be on Stapleton’s territory, close to the lair of that monstrous beast. We knew the danger, but we had no clear idea of how to counter it.

“It is too risky,” I argued. “We know what we face, but not its full nature, nor how he controls it. We must wait for Holmes. He will devise a proper strategy, one that does not place you squarely in the creature’s path.”

“And what if Holmes is delayed?” Sir Henry demanded. “What if this is our only chance? Stapleton has invited me tonight. If I refuse, he will grow suspicious. He will wonder why I suddenly shun him after the events of last night. His invitation is a test, Watson. If I decline, he will know that something has changed, that I suspect him. The opportunity will be lost. He will simply lie in wait for another, one of his choosing, not of mine.”

He had a point, a terribly valid one. The logic was sound, and it left me in a dreadful quandary. My instructions, my very instincts as Holmes’s deputy, were to keep the baronet from harm. Yet to do so now might be the very thing that sealed his fate later. We argued back and forth for the better part of an hour, our voices low and intense. I held fast to my position that we must wait, while he, with the stubbornness of a man who has been pushed to the brink and now wishes to push back, insisted that we must act.

It was in the midst of this heated debate that the door to the study opened without a sound. We both turned, startled. A tall, thin figure stood there, clad in a long travelling coat. He was leaning upon the door frame, a dry, amused smile playing upon his thin lips. His hawk-like face was pale with fatigue, but his grey eyes were as sharp and as piercing as ever.

“An interesting tactical dilemma,” he remarked, his voice calm and precise. “Though I fear, Sir Henry, that your proposed course of action has a boldness that borders upon the suicidal. You will, of course, do nothing of the sort.”

For a moment I could only stare, dumbfounded. Then a great wave of relief, so profound it left me weak, washed over me.

“Holmes!” I cried, springing to my feet. “Thank God! How did you get here so quickly?”

“The telegraph, my dear Watson, is a remarkable invention, and the railway network is not far behind,” he said, stepping into the room and shaking my hand. He then turned to the baronet, who was looking at the famous detective with an expression of utter astonishment. “Sir Henry Baskerville, I presume. I am Sherlock Holmes. I regret that our first meeting must be under such grave circumstances, but I am glad that I have arrived in time to prevent you from making a donation of your person to the collection of my esteemed friend, Mr. Stapleton.”

“But… but how?” Sir Henry stammered. “Your telegram, Watson. You only sent it last night.”

“I have been in the district for some days,” Holmes explained, stripping off his gloves. “I preferred to conduct my initial inquiries without my presence being known. The man on the tor, Watson, whose presence so vexed you, was none other than myself. Your reports have been invaluable, but there is no substitute for personal observation. When your wire arrived this morning, confirming the final, terrible piece of the puzzle, I knew the time for concealment was over.”

He had been here all along. He had been watching, just as I had. The thought was both a comfort and a mild rebuke. All my struggles, my fears, my lonely vigil, had been observed by those keen, critical eyes.

Holmes wasted no more time on pleasantries. He was all business. He drew a chair up to the table, and for the next hour he subjected me to the most rigorous cross-examination. I recounted every detail of the previous night: the meeting of the lovers, Stapleton’s furious interruption, my subsequent conversation with Sir Henry, and finally, the ghastly pursuit across the moor, the death of the convict, and our sight of the hound.

He listened without a word of interruption, his long fingers steepled before him, his eyes closed. Only when I described the creature’s appearance, its immense size and the ghastly, shimmering light that clung to it, did his eyelids flicker.

The true danger lies in the creature’s teeth and claws, not in its appearance. You were certain it was the convict’s body, Watson?”

“Absolutely,” I confirmed. “The scent of Sir Henry’s suit, which Barrymore had given him, drew the beast. It was a fortunate mischance that saved our friend’s life.”

“Fortunate indeed,” Holmes agreed, his gaze now fixed upon the baronet. “Stapleton’s plan is as clear as day. He is the next in line to the Baskerville fortune, once you are removed. He keeps the beast hidden in the heart of the mire and unleashes it when the time is right. The death of Sir Charles was his first success. Your death, Sir Henry, was to be his second, and final, victory.”

Holmes turned, and his face was grim. “His greatest error is that he does not know we are aware of his plot. We spent the remainder of the afternoon in quiet preparation. Holmes produced two service revolvers from his valise, the cold blue steel of them a comforting reality against the spectral fears that had haunted us for so long. He checked the mechanisms with swift, practiced fingers, laying out the cartridges upon a flat stone. “Watson, you will take one,” he said. “Our quarry is no phantom, but flesh and blood. As dusk began to settle upon the moor, tinging the western clouds with streaks of violet and blood orange, Sir Henry made ready to depart. He was dressed for his dinner engagement, and I could not but feel a pang of pity for this brave gentleman, who must now walk into the lion’s den, his every pleasantry a deception, his every smile a mask.

“Remember,” Holmes said, placing a hand on the baronet’s shoulder. “You are to tell them you intend to walk back. Make it clear. Stapleton must have no doubt as to your route. We will be positioned along the path, somewhere between Merripit House and the spot where the track rises towards the Hall. Godspeed, Sir Henry. Your part is the most difficult of all.”

The baronet wrung our hands, his own cold as ice, and then, with a squared jaw and a last, determined glance at us, he set off down the slope. We watched his sturdy figure diminish against the vast, darkening landscape until he was but a speck, swallowed by the gloom.

For a time we remained in silence, the sense of impending crisis heavy in the air. The wind had dropped, and an unnerving stillness had fallen over the moor. From the direction of the Grimpen Mire, a low, white bank of fog was beginning to creep, its cold, damp tendrils reaching out across the heather like ghostly fingers.

“The fog, Holmes!” I exclaimed in dismay. “If it grows thick, we shall see nothing.”

“It is a risk, Watson, a considerable one,” he replied, his keen eyes scanning the horizon. “It aids our concealment, but it will also aid our enemy. It may well be that Stapleton has counted upon it. All the more reason for us to be in position without delay. Come.”

We left the relative comfort of the hut and made our way across the broken ground, our revolvers held ready in our pockets. The path that led from Merripit House was well known to me, and it was upon this track that Holmes intended to lay our ambush. He chose a spot where the path dipped between two low, craggy tors, a natural defile that would force any traveller to pass within a few yards of our hiding place. We settled ourselves amongst a jumble of granite boulders, the cold stone seeping through our coats, and began our vigil.

The minutes stretched into an eternity. The fog grew steadily thicker, swirling around us in dense, white wreaths, until we could scarcely see a dozen feet in any direction. The world was reduced to our narrow hiding place, the looming shapes of the rocks, and the muffled, unnatural silence. All the tales of the moor came flooding back into my mind: the whispers of phantom hoof-beats, the spectral lights, and the baleful cries that were said to presage death. My nerves were stretched to the breaking point, and I found myself starting at every rustle of the heather, every drip of moisture from the lichen-covered stones.

Beside me, Holmes was as still and silent as the granite itself. I could just make out his sharp, hawk-like profile against the grey murk, his senses, I knew, alert to the slightest sound.

“Can you hear anything, Holmes?” I whispered, my own voice sounding loud and strange in the stillness.

“Only the beating of your heart, my dear Watson,” he murmured back, a ghost of a smile in his tone. “Patience. Our man is methodical. He will not act until he is certain Sir Henry is alone and beyond any hope of rescue. He will let him get some distance from the house first.”

And so we waited. The fog was now so thick that it felt as though we were entombed, wrapped in a cold, wet shroud. It muffled all sound, creating a profound and disquieting peace. It must have been past ten o’clock when a new sound finally broke the silence. It was distant at first, a low, deep, and continuous murmur, a sound so strange and unearthly that it made the hair on my arms stand on end. It was not a bark, nor a howl, but a guttural, vibrating growl that seemed to come from the very bowels of the earth.

Holmes gripped my arm. “It comes, Watson! It comes! Be ready.”

The sound grew louder, nearer, and with it came another. It was the frantic patter of running feet. Out of the wall of fog before us, a figure suddenly burst into view: Sir Henry, his face pale as death, his eyes wide with an unspeakable terror. He glanced wildly over his shoulder and ran on, his breath coming in ragged, sobbing gasps. He did not see us as he passed our hiding place, his every faculty consumed by the horror that pursued him.

And then we saw it. My heart, which had been pounding in my chest, seemed to freeze solid, and a cry of pure, primal fear was choked in my throat. From the same bank of fog from which our friend had emerged, a shape loomed. It was a hound, yes, but such a hound as Hell itself might have spawned. It was enormous, coal black, its bulk far exceeding that of any mastiff I had ever seen. But it was not its size, nor its slavering jaws, nor the savage intensity of its purpose that struck one rigid with horror. It was the ghastly, shimmering light which coated the beast. Its eyes glowed with a dull, malevolent fire, and its entire muzzle, its hackles, and the dewlap beneath its throat were outlined in a flickering, spectral luminescence. It did not run, but bounded, its great paws seeming to spend but a moment on the ground, a silent, demonic apparition of vengeance.

For a single, dreadful instant I was paralysed, my mind struggling to accept the reality of the hell-hound of the legend. But Holmes’s nerve held true. The sharp crack of his revolver broke the spell. “Fire, Watson! For God’s sake, fire!” he roared.

I raised my own weapon, my hand trembling, and fired. I heard the bullet sing past the creature in the fog. But Holmes was a magnificent shot. His first bullet had struck the beast, for it let out a hideous cry, a howl of pain and fury that was more terrible than its silent pursuit. It faltered in its stride, but only for a second. Its baleful, glowing eyes fixed upon us, and with a fresh roar of rage, it turned from Sir Henry and launched itself directly at our hiding place. I saw the flash of Holmes’s revolver again, and then again, and at the same instant I fired my own remaining cartridges into the charging mass.

The creature’s momentum carried it forward. It rose upon its hind legs, its colossal, fiery form towering over us, its fangs bared in a final, ghastly snarl. I threw myself to one side, catching a glancing blow from its heavy paw that sent me sprawling amongst the rocks. For a moment I thought Holmes was lost. But my friend had not flinched. As the beast fell, he emptied the last chamber of his revolver into its skull. The immense body crashed to the ground, so close that its foul, hot breath washed over my face. It twitched convulsively for a few moments, its mighty limbs kicking at the air, and then lay still.

I staggered to my feet, my whole body shaking. The spectral light which had so terrified us was already fading, and in the dim light of our pocket lantern, we saw not a demon, but a gigantic hound, a cross of bloodhound and mastiff, gaunt, savage, and smeared with some vile, phosphorescent compound. The mystery of the hound of the Baskervilles was no more.

Holmes was bending over the creature, his analytical mind already reasserting itself. “We have him, Watson, we have him,” he said, his voice tense with triumph. “What a beast! See how he has applied the phosphorus to the jaws and eyes. A devilish ingenuity.” He paused, prodding the body. “Where is Sir Henry?”

Our friend was lying upon the path, face down, where he had fallen in a dead faint. We rushed to his side and with some difficulty managed to revive him with the contents of my flask. He sat up, his eyes still wild with terror, and stared at the great, black body lying inert upon the ground.

“My God,” he whispered. “My God. It is real.”

“It was real,” Holmes corrected him gently. “And it is dead. The curse is broken. You are safe.”

But our work was not yet done. The hound was merely the instrument; the true villain was still at large. “Stapleton!” Holmes cried, leaping to his feet. “We must take him before he can escape. Come, Watson! We must make for Merripit House at once!”

Leaving Sir Henry to recover his wits, we raced along the path, our lanterns cutting bobbing circles in the dense fog. The silence that had returned to the moor was now more sinister than ever, and I could not shake the feeling that Stapleton, having heard the shots, would be long gone. The house was dark as we approached, not a single light showing in any window. The front door was ajar, swinging gently in a stray puff of wind.

“He is fled,” I panted.

“Perhaps,” Holmes said grimly, pushing the door fully open. “But he may have left something, or someone, behind.”

We entered the house, our revolvers once more in our hands. A lamp was burning low in the dining room, illuminating a scene of disarray. An overturned chair, a spilled glass of wine, and the remains of the dinner for three suggested a hasty departure. But the house was silent. We moved from room to room, our footfalls echoing in the emptiness, finding nothing but the naturalist’s collections of moths and butterflies pinned in their neat cases, a grotesque and orderly testament to the man’s cold-blooded nature.

It was in an upper bedroom that we made our discovery. The door was locked, but a low moaning from within spurred Holmes to put his shoulder to it. With a splintering of wood, the lock gave way. The room was a small attic chamber, and there, tied securely to an old post that supported the roof, was a woman. Her head was covered by a sheet, and she was gagged so cruelly that her face was swollen and purple. It was the work of a moment for Holmes to cut her bonds and remove the gag. As the sheet fell away, we saw the terrified, tear-streaked face of the lady we had known as Miss Stapleton.

She collapsed into my arms, sobbing hysterically. “Is he safe?” she cried. “Is Sir Henry safe?”

“He is safe,” Holmes assured her, his voice unusually gentle. “But your brother… where is he?”

Her eyes blazed with a sudden, fierce hatred. “My brother!” she said, her voice thick with scorn. “He is no brother of mine. He is my husband! That villain, that monster! His name is not Stapleton; it is Rodger Baskerville. He was the son of Sir Charles’s younger brother, the one who fled to South America.”

The final pieces of the puzzle clicked into place. “Of course,” Holmes murmured. “Of course. The family resemblance. The man I saw in London. He is a Baskerville himself, and with Sir Henry gone, he would inherit everything.”

“He forced me to do it,” she went on, her words tumbling out in a torrent of fear and anger. “He told me that if I helped him, he would share the fortune with me and we could be free. But I could not bear to see Sir Henry harmed! I sent the warning to London. I tried to speak with him on the moor. Tonight, when he told me his plan, I refused to be a part of it. I told him I would warn Sir Henry. And so he struck me, and he tied me here, to silence me, lest his plan should fail.”

“Where did he go?” Holmes demanded. “Did you hear him leave?”

“I heard the shots,” she gasped. “He came running up here a few minutes after. He said his dog was dead and you were coming. He took a rope and a lantern, and he ran out towards the back of the house. He said he was going to his hiding place, in the middle of the Grimpen Mire.”

“The mire!” I exclaimed. “He will be lost! No man can cross it in the dark, let alone in this fog.”

“He has a path,” she said, pointing a trembling finger towards the window. “He marked it with wands. It leads to the old tin mine on the central island, where he kept the hound. He believed no one could ever follow him there.”

There was no time to lose. Holmes cast a single, sharp glance at me, and we were off once more, plunging back out into the cold, foggy night. With Mrs. Baskerville’s directions and our own knowledge of the moor, we found the start of the secret path, a series of slender willow wands set at intervals of fifty yards or so. The ground beneath our feet was treacherous, a quaking morass of green-scummed pools and shuddering peat that threatened to swallow us at every step. The fog was our greatest enemy, and more than once we lost sight of the next marker and had to cast about blindly, the ooze sucking at our boots.

It was in one of these dreadful, glutinous patches that we found the first sign of him. Half-sunk in the black, viscous mud was a man’s boot. It was one of a new pair, a brand I recognised as the one Sir Henry had lost at his hotel in London. Stapleton had used it to give the hound our friend’s scent. Now, in his panicked flight, it had been torn from his foot by the greedy bog.

We pressed on, our lanterns held high, our eyes straining for the next marker. The path grew ever more indistinct. We found another wand, and then another, but the fourth we could not see. A great, despairing cry suddenly echoed across the mire, a long, drawn-out scream of terror and agony that was abruptly cut short. We stood frozen, listening. But there was only the gurgle of the bog and the drip of water. Somewhere out there in the darkness, a man had met his end. We shouted, but only the melancholy echo of our own voices came back to us. The Grimpen Mire had claimed its own, and the last of the evil Baskervilles had been swallowed into the foul slime of the great bog which had been the centre of his wicked schemes.

We made our way slowly and cautiously back to Merripit House, and in the morning, we returned with Sir Henry to Baskerville Hall. The long night was over. The baronet, though profoundly shaken by his ordeal and by the terrible truth about the woman he had loved, was a free man. The shadow that had hung over his family for generations had been lifted, not by the breaking of a supernatural curse, but by the cold logic of my friend, Sherlock Holmes.

So peculiar a request, and so sudden a change of front, might have aroused suspicion in a mind more accustomed to the darker complexities of human nature. His own heart was so full of love for the lady that he was ready to believe anything that might smooth the path to their union, and he accepted Stapleton’s apology with the same open generosity with which it was apparently offered. He readily agreed to the three months’ delay, and the two men parted on the best of terms. The baronet was in the highest of spirits, his dejection of the morning utterly vanished, and he spoke of nothing but his future happiness and the plans he would make for his and his bride’s life at Baskerville Hall.

I confess that I did not share his elation. My own brief observations of the scene upon the moor left me with a sense of profound unease. The sheer violence of Stapleton’s anger had seemed to me to be something more than a brother’s overzealous protection; it had the quality of a proprietor’s rage, a fury at the trespassing upon what he considered his own. His swift and complete reversal struck me as being altogether too convenient, and I could not shake the feeling that some deeper, more sinister game was being played. I tried to voice some of my doubts to Sir Henry, but he was in no mood to hear them. He laughed away my concerns, attributing my caution to my own bachelor status, and clapped me on the shoulder with a heartiness that made further protest impossible.

“You are a good fellow, Watson,” he cried, his face glowing, “the best of friends, but you see shadows where there is only sunlight. All is well, man! The last of the Baskerville curse is broken, and a new and happy chapter is about to begin!”

I could say no more. My duty was to my friend, and my friend was clearly convinced of his own good fortune. Yet as I retired to my chamber that night, the fog swirling once more against the window panes, I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the moorland weather. I resolved then and there to communicate everything that had transpired to Holmes, and to beg him, if his own inquiries in London permitted, to turn his full attention to the strange naturalist of Merripit House.

It was two days later that I received a telegram in reply. It was as brief and cryptic as was my friend’s habit. It contained only three words: “Coming. Stay close.”

The relief I felt upon reading this message was immense, for the weight of my solitary responsibility had begun to press upon me very heavily indeed. I said nothing to Sir Henry of Holmes’s impending arrival, for I did not wish to cloud his present happiness with my own forebodings. The baronet spent his days in a state of blissful anticipation, making frequent visits to Merripit House. He reported that Stapleton was now a model of civility, though still of a somewhat reserved and eccentric temper, and that Miss Stapleton, though quiet and a little pale, seemed content. He respected his promise not to press his suit for the allotted time, and their meetings were always conducted in her brother’s presence.

It was on the evening of the third day that Holmes arrived. I had been watching the road from my window, and I saw the familiar, lean figure step down from the dogcart which had brought him from the station. I hurried down to meet him in the great hall.

“Well, Watson,” he said, shaking my hand while his keen grey eyes swept the ancient, shadowed hall, “you seem to have found yourself in a veritable fortress. I trust our friend the baronet is safe?”

“He is safe and, in his own mind, deliriously happy,” I answered. I then led him to the privacy of the billiard room, where, over a glass of wine, I gave him a full account of the events of the past few days, from the strange interview on the moor to Stapleton’s sudden and inexplicable consent to the marriage.

He listened with that absorbed stillness which was so characteristic of him, his fingertips pressed together, his gaze fixed upon the ceiling. He made no comment until I had entirely finished.

“And you say Stapleton requested a delay of three months?” he asked at last.

“Yes, Holmes. He claimed he needed time to adjust to losing his sister.”

“Or time for something else to come to fruition,” Holmes murmured, more to himself than to me. “His actions are not those of a concerned brother, Watson. They are the actions of a man playing for time, a man who has met an unexpected obstacle and must adjust his plan. Did you observe the lady’s reaction to this arrangement?”

“Sir Henry says she is quiet, but content.”

“He sees what he wishes to see,” Holmes said, rising from his chair and beginning to pace the room. “A man in love is the most untrustworthy of observers. This woman, who was so earnest in her warnings to Sir Henry, so desperate for him to leave this place of danger, now placidly accepts a three month engagement? It is not credible. She is acting under compulsion, Watson. The brother’s influence is absolute.”

“But what can be his motive, Holmes? If he intended harm to Sir Henry, why would he consent to a marriage that would bind their families together? It makes no sense.”

“On the contrary, it may make a terrifying kind of sense,” said Holmes, his face grim. “We are missing a piece of the puzzle, a vital fact that would make all of this horribly clear. Tell me about this house, Merripit House. What do you know of its history, and of the Stapletons themselves before they came here?”

“Very little,” I admitted. “They are said to have come from the north of England. He established a school which failed, and then they came here a couple of years ago.”

“A school,” Holmes mused. “That is interesting. Many a scoundrel has found a school to be a convenient cloak for other activities. We must learn more. Tomorrow, Watson, you and I shall pay a visit, not to Merripit House, but to the good Dr. Mortimer. He is the local antiquarian, is he not? He may have access to records, or to local gossip, that could shed some light. For now, we must be vigilant. You have done well, my dear fellow, but the crisis is far from over. It is, I suspect, only just beginning.”

His words sent a fresh wave of apprehension through me. The baronet’s cheerful optimism seemed a flimsy shield indeed against the cold, calculating intellect that Holmes suspected was at work. We kept our own counsel that evening, and Holmes, presenting himself to Sir Henry as having come down for a breath of country air, was a model of the affable guest. He drew the baronet out on the subject of his Canadian experiences, and I could see that his charm and interest were entirely winning our friend over. Yet I saw, too, the occasional flicker in Holmes’s eyes as he watched Sir Henry, a look of profound, almost sorrowful, concentration, as a physician might observe a patient who is unaware of the gravity of his own condition.

The next morning, under the pretence of a long walk upon the moor, Holmes and I made our way to Dr. Mortimer’s house. The doctor was delighted to see my friend, whose name was of course well known to him, and he ushered us into his study, a room cluttered with skulls, archaeological finds, and learned volumes.

Holmes came to the point at once. “We are interested, Doctor,” he began, “in your neighbours, the Stapletons. My friend Watson has been much struck by their unusual attachment to one another, and I confess, my own curiosity is piqued. What can you tell us of their background?”

Dr. Mortimer adjusted his spectacles. “A fascinating subject, Mr. Holmes! Mr. Stapleton is a man of science, an entomologist of some repute, though of a nervous and excitable temperament. As for their origins, they are, as you know, from the north, though I have heard it suggested that they lived for a time in South America. The failure of his school was apparently a great blow to him financially.”

“South America,” Holmes repeated, his eyes lighting up with interest. “That is a new detail, and a most suggestive one. And the lady?”

“Ah, Miss Stapleton! A most beautiful and exotic creature. Quite the tropical bloom to find upon our grim moor. Her complexion, her dark hair… one would say she has a Latin strain in her blood. She is devoted to her brother, though I have sometimes thought she seems a little afraid of him.”

Holmes leaned forward. “You say his school failed. Do you happen to recall the name of this establishment?”

Dr. Mortimer furrowed his brow in thought. “It is on the tip of my tongue. He mentioned it once. It was a most respectable academy, I believe. Let me see… St. Oliver’s? No, that is not it. Ah, I have it! It was called The Priory. Yes, that was the name.”

I saw Holmes stiffen, and a look of such utter concentration came over his features that it was as if he had ceased to be in the room with us. He sat motionless for a full minute, and then he rose to his feet.

“Dr. Mortimer,” he said, his voice taut with suppressed excitement, “you have been of greater assistance than you can possibly imagine. We must not take up any more of your valuable time.”

We took our leave with an abruptness that must have seemed discourteous to our host, but Holmes was in no state to attend to social niceties. He did not speak a word as we strode back across the moor, his head sunk on his chest, his hands clasped behind him. It was not until we were in sight of Baskerville Hall that he stopped and turned to me.

“Watson,” he said, and his voice was low and grave, “I think I have it. The whole black, diabolical business. It is more cunning than I had supposed, and the stakes are higher than we ever dreamed.”

“Good heavens, Holmes! What is it?”

“That school, Watson. The Priory. Do you not remember the case of the missing heir, the one I investigated three years ago? The boy who was abducted from the Priory School in Yorkshire? “You think Stapleton is this Vandeleur?” I cried, aghast.

The next two days passed with an agonising slowness. The weather turned foul, and a thick, white fog, the dreaded vapour of the moor, rolled in and blanketed the entire landscape. It clung to the windows of the Hall like a damp shroud, and the mournful sound of the fog-horn from the sea was the only sound that broke the oppressive silence. It was a time of great tension. Sir Henry held to his promise with a stoic fortitude, though I could see the strain in the tight set of his jaw and the way his eyes would stray to the swirling whiteness outside.

On Friday evening, Inspector Lestrade arrived, a small, ferret-faced man, but with an air of bulldog tenacity about him. He greeted Holmes with a grudging respect born of many past encounters where my friend’s genius had triumphed over the more methodical ways of Scotland Yard.

“So, Mr. Holmes,” he said, rubbing his cold hands together, “you’ve got another queer one for us down in these parts. The Grimpen Mire, eh? A fine place to end a career. I’ve brought the warrant, as you asked.”

“Excellent, Lestrade,” Holmes said briskly. “Your part will be a simple one, but vital. You, Watson, and I will conceal ourselves near the moor path. When the time comes, I shall give a signal. You will not fire unless I do, or unless Sir Henry’s life is in immediate and dire peril. Is that understood?”

We both nodded our assent.

At seven o’clock, we watched from an upper window as Sir Henry set out for Merripit House, his stout figure quickly swallowed by the rolling banks of fog. An hour later, the three of us, armed and cloaked, slipped out of the Hall and made our way onto the moor.

The fog was a living thing. It was cold and wet, and it swirled about us, limiting our vision to a mere twenty yards in any direction. It muffled all sound and distorted every shape, so that the familiar tors became looming, monstrous giants in the gloom. I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the dampness of the night. It was the chill of pure, primal fear.

Holmes led the way with an unerring confidence, as if he could see through the impenetrable mist. He brought us to a spot where a cluster of low, granite boulders stood beside the path, forming a natural trench.

“We will wait here,” he whispered, his voice a faint rasp in the profound silence. “From this position we command a view of the path as it comes from Merripit House. Keep your eyes fixed upon that direction, and your pistols ready.”

We crouched down behind the cold, wet rocks, the three of us, a small island of purpose in an ocean of swirling vapour. The silence was absolute, broken only by the drip of water from the rocks and the thumping of my own heart against my ribs. I have been in tight places before, but I do not think I have ever felt such a dreadful sense of suspense. Minutes stretched into an eternity. Lestrade shifted his feet from time to time, his cockney impatience at war with the solemnity of the moment. Holmes was as still as the granite beside him, a motionless silhouette, his keen face thrust forward, his entire being concentrated upon the path that was lost in the fog.

Suddenly, he stiffened. “Hush!” he breathed.

I strained my ears, and through the fog I heard it; a faint, rhythmic tread upon the gravel path. It was Sir Henry, returning from his dinner. The sound grew louder, steadier. He was walking at a good pace, his nerves evidently holding firm. My hand tightened upon the butt of my revolver.

Then, from the depths of the moor, from the direction of the Great Grimpen Mire, another sound came to our ears. It was a low, whining murmur, a deep, guttural vibration that seemed to make the very ground tremble beneath us. It grew and grew, swelling into a deep-throated baying, a sound so malign and so awful that it froze the blood in my veins. It was not the bark of any dog that I had ever heard. It was a roar of unspeakable fury and hunger.

Lestrade gave a stifled gasp and half-rose to his feet. “My God, what is that?” he whispered.

“Down!” hissed Holmes. “Down, man, or your life is not worth a farthing!”

At that same moment, we saw it. Out of the bank of fog, not fifty yards from us, a creature bounded into view. It was a hound, yes, but such a hound as hell might have whelped. It was enormous, coal-black, and of a lean, spectral gauntness. But it was not its size or its shape which sent a wave of utter terror through me. It was its head. The muzzle, the eyes, the entire jaw and dewlap were outlined in a flickering, hellish blue flame. From the open mouth, which dripped with this ghastly light, came puffs of smoke and a terrible, savage growling.

I am not a man given to imaginings, but as I saw that foul apparition tear through the mist, its eyes burning with a smouldering glare, my mind reeled. I was paralysed, my fingers numb upon the trigger of my pistol. Lestrade let out a cry of pure horror and buried his face in his hands. Even Holmes, for a single, stark instant, seemed stunned into immobility.

Sir Henry had stopped dead in the path. He stood staring, his face a mask of disbelief and terror, his mouth open as if to scream, though no sound came out. The hound was leaping towards him, a silent, flaming messenger of death.

It was the sight of our friend’s mortal peril that broke the spell. Holmes and I fired as one. The crack of our revolvers was deafening in the stillness, and for an instant, the great beast was lit up by our flashes. I heard a hideous, screaming howl of pain, and saw the creature stumble, but it did not fall. In its fury, it launched itself with even greater speed directly at Sir Henry’s throat.

“Again, Watson, again!” cried Holmes.

I fired my remaining barrels into the flank of the beast. It was Holmes’s shot, however, that was the decisive one. He had taken a steadier aim, and his bullet struck the creature squarely in its skull. With a final, agonizing yelp, the hound somersaulted past Sir Henry and fell to the ground, where it lay kicking and twitching, its flaming jaws snapping at the empty air.

We rushed forward. Sir Henry had collapsed upon the path, not from any wound, but from the sheer shock and horror of the attack. He was alive, thank God, he was alive! As I bent over him, I saw the truth of the hound. The ghastly light about its jaws was already fading, and a pungent, chemical smell, the odour of phosphorus, filled the air. It was a huge beast indeed, a mix of bloodhound and mastiff, gaunt from starvation and savage with fury, but it was a thing of flesh and blood.

As Lestrade and I attended to the baronet, Holmes sprang away from us, his face alight with a terrible energy. “The man, Watson! The man!” he cried, pointing out into the mire. “He will not escape us!”

I looked where he pointed and saw, for a fleeting second, the figure of a man outlined against a patch of thinner fog. He had thrown his hands up in despair at the sight of his creature’s death, and now he was turning and running frantically into the heart of the great mire.

“Come, Watson, come!” Holmes yelled, already starting in pursuit. “Lestrade, see to Sir Henry! We must not lose our man!”

I followed him without a second thought. We plunged off the path and onto the treacherous, quaking ground of the bog. We could hear Stapleton’s desperate, splashing footsteps ahead of us. He knew the paths, the secret ways through the mire that he had marked with wands, but in his terror and the disorienting fog, his nerve had clearly failed him.

Suddenly, from the darkness ahead, there came a single, terrible scream; a cry of utter despair and final agony that was choked off in a dreadful, bubbling gurgle. Then there was silence, a silence more horrifying than the scream itself.

We stopped, our hearts pounding. “He has gone,” Holmes said in a low, awed voice. “He has missed his footing in his haste, and the mire has claimed him. There is no saving a man from the Grimpen Mire.”

We stood for a moment, listening, but the bog held its secret. The evil of the Baskervilles had been swallowed by the earth itself. We found, a little later, Sir Henry’s old boot, the one Stapleton had used to set his hound upon the scent, caught in the reeds near the spot where he must have gone down. It was the only trace that remained of the cunning naturalist.

Our work was not yet done. We made our way, cautiously now, towards Merripit House. The front door was ajar. We entered to find a scene of chaos, but it was from an upper room that we heard a faint, moaning sound. We broke down the locked door and there, to my horror, we found her. The lady who had passed as Miss Stapleton was bound tightly to an old chair, her mouth gagged, and the marks of a cruel whip upon her shoulders and arms.

We freed her at once. She was half mad with terror and pain, but when she understood that her tormentor was dead, her story came tumbling out. She was, as Holmes had deduced, Stapleton’s wife, a Costa Rican lady of good family whom he had married under a false name and then abused and terrorised into becoming his accomplice. He had brought her to England, promising her a life of ease, only to reveal his fiendish plot. Her warnings to Sir Henry had been genuine, desperate attempts to save him, which had earned her nothing but her husband’s brutal rage. It was he who had tied her up that evening to prevent her from crying out a warning as Sir Henry passed the house.

We brought her back to Baskerville Hall, where Sir Henry, though still shaken to his core, saw to her comfort with a tenderness that spoke volumes. His ordeal was over; the shadow that had hung over his house for generations was finally and completely lifted.

A few days later, back in our familiar rooms in Baker Street, Holmes gave me the final pieces of the puzzle. “The portrait was the key, Watson,” he explained, puffing on his pipe. “Once I saw the resemblance between Stapleton and that rogue Hugo, I knew we were dealing with a Baskerville. A quick inquiry into the family tree showed me Roger, the black sheep who was supposed to have died in South America. His description matched that of a missing schoolmaster, Vandeleur, who had wronged another branch of the Baskerville family. The chain was complete. He came here, discovered the legend, and procured the largest dog he could find in London, hiding it in the old tin mine out on the mire. The phosphorous was a simple, if devilish, touch to play upon the superstitions of the neighbourhood and the fears of his victims. His plan with Sir Henry was the same as with Sir Charles: to frighten him to death. If that failed, the hound was a more direct instrument. Had we not intervened, Sir Henry’s heart, already weakened by terror, would have failed, or the beast would have torn his throat out. Either way, Stapleton, as the next apparent neighbour and friend, would have been above suspicion, and after a suitable period, he would have made his claim as the next of kin from South America.”

“And his wife?” I asked.

“A tragic figure,” Holmes said, his expression softening for a moment. “He used her, Watson, just as he used the hound. Her beauty was the bait to keep Sir Henry on the moor. But love, my dear fellow, is an unpredictable emotion. It entered into his calculations and very nearly ruined them. She fell for the baronet, and in trying to save him, she sealed her husband’s fate as surely as we did.” He stood and looked out of the window at the bustling London street below. “The world is full of obvious things which nobody by any chance ever observes. Out on that dreadful moor, however, we were forced to observe, and a great evil has been put to an end.”

I looked at the sharp, eagle-like profile of my friend, and I knew that he was right. The case of the hound was closed, and the darkness had passed from the house of Baskerville forever.
