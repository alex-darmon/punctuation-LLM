“It is a matter of public safety, Finch,” I insisted. “Every moment we delay gives this fiend a new opportunity for mischief.”

Thus pressed by both myself and the Inspector, my friend Professor Finch gave a sigh of profound weariness. He paced the length of his cluttered study, a tall, stooping figure, his chin buried in the tweed of his waistcoat and his spectacles pushed high upon his forehead. “There will be no more thefts,” he announced, pausing by the window to gaze down upon the rain-slicked London street. “You may set your mind at ease on that score. You ask if I have identified the culprit. I have. The knowledge of his name, however, is a trivial thing when compared to the challenge of securing his person. That I hope to accomplish presently. My own stratagems are in motion; but the matter requires a most delicate touch, for we are dealing with a man of extraordinary cunning and resolve, aided, as I have reason to believe, by a network of informants as shadowy as himself. So long as he believes his movements are untraced, we have some hope of cornering him; but at the first whisper of suspicion, he would melt away into this great city’s teeming millions like a phantom. Without wishing to sound conceited, I am forced to conclude that this individual is more than a match for the official force, which is why I have refrained from seeking your assistance, Inspector. Should I be mistaken, the fault is mine alone; but I am prepared for that. For the present, I can only promise that I shall communicate with you the very instant I may do so without imperilling the entire enterprise.”

Inspector Davies, a man whose patience was as short as his stout frame, seemed wholly unconvinced by this speech, or by the dismissive reference to his constabulary. His broad, ruddy face had taken on a deeper shade of crimson, while his knuckles were white where he gripped the head of his walking-stick. He was about to voice a protest, I am certain, but he was forestalled by a sharp rap upon the door. A moment later, Finch’s young footman, Peters, entered the room.

“Begging your pardon, sir,” he said, with a slight bow, “the gentleman is here about the Etruscan vase.”

“Excellent,” said Finch, his expression brightening. “Show him up at once, Peters.” He then turned to a heavy oak cabinet and produced from it a small, intricately carved wooden box. “A new acquisition from a Florentine maker,” he remarked to us, his tone light and conversational. “Observe the mechanism. A most ingenious pressure-plate lock.”

“We are here to catch a thief, not to admire your trinkets,” grumbled Davies.

“Patience, Inspector, patience,” said Finch with a thin smile. “Perhaps we may find a use for this trinket yet. Just ask the gentleman to place his portfolio upon the central table, Peters.”

I confess I was as baffled as the Inspector, for my friend had mentioned nothing of an Etruscan vase or an expected visitor. There was, however, a large porcelain urn on a pedestal near the fire, and towards this our guest was directed as he entered. He was a man of middle years, with a neat grey beard and the spectacles of a scholar, carrying a large leather portfolio under his arm.

“Ah, Mr. Abernathy, I presume?” said Finch, his voice smooth as silk. “Thank you for coming on such short notice. The vase is there. If you would be so good as to set down your case and give me your preliminary opinion.”

The man stepped forward, his eyes fixed upon the urn with a professional air. He placed his portfolio on the table as instructed. “A fine specimen, Professor,” he began, leaning forward for a closer look. “The patina appears…”

He never finished the sentence. As his hand reached out to touch the porcelain, Finch made a swift, darting movement. There was a dull thud, a click of locking steel, and the sharp cry of our visitor.

“Gentlemen,” Finch declared, his voice ringing with triumph, “may I present to you Mr. Silas Croft, the man who stole the Marlovian Folio and the Portland Sceptre.”

It had all transpired in a heartbeat. I stared, astonished, at the scene before me: at Finch’s exultant face, and at the so-called Mr. Abernathy, who now stood shaking, his right hand clamped firmly to the table by a peculiar metal device that had sprung from the surface. For a second we all stood frozen, a tableau of shock and revelation. Then, with a snarl of pure animal fury, Croft tore his trapped hand free, splintering the tabletop and ripping the mechanism from its screws. He launched himself not at us, but at the fireplace. With the desperate strength of a cornered wolf, he seized the heavy iron poker and spun to face us, his scholarly demeanour vanished and replaced by a mask of murderous rage. The four of us, for Davies, Finch, and I had been joined by the footman Peters, braced for his charge. He was a whirlwind of savage energy, the poker whistling through the air, and for a full minute he kept us all at bay. It was only when Davies, with a bull-like rush, ducked under a wild swing and tackled him about the knees that we were able to overwhelm him. Even then, it was a near thing, and we did not feel secure until he was bound hand and foot with curtain cords.

“We have his portfolio,” said Professor Finch, breathing heavily. “I suspect it will take us to Scotland Yard, where its contents may be of some interest. And now, gentlemen,” he continued, adjusting his dishevelled cravat, “our little drama has reached its conclusion. I am at your disposal, and you may be assured that I will answer any questions you care to put to me.”

PART II. _The House on the Salt-Marsh._

CHAPTER I. THE LOST COAST OF ESSEX.

There is a stretch of the English coast, lying between the mouths of the Crouch and the Blackwater, that has for centuries resisted the taming hand of man. It is a lonely and desolate region of tidal creeks, shimmering mud-flats, and vast, whispering reed-beds that stretch for miles towards a grey and indifferent sea. Here, the land and the water are in a constant, silent struggle, and the horizon is a flat, unbroken line, marked only by the occasional silhouette of a sea-bird or the distant sail of a solitary barge. The air itself is heavy with the scent of salt and decay, and a profound silence broods over the landscape, a silence broken only by the cry of the curlew and the rustle of the wind in the dry reeds.

Nature in this grim district is of a singular and melancholy character. There are no grand cliffs or picturesque coves; only the slow, inexorable creep of the tide over endless plains of estuarine mud. The few paths that traverse this wilderness are treacherous and winding, often vanishing completely at high water, leaving the unwary traveller stranded in a featureless maze. There are no true inhabitants of this watery desert. A few hardy fishermen may have their huts upon the higher ground, but they are a race apart, as silent and weathered as the land itself. The stoat hunts in the tangled sea-lavender, the heron stands motionless in the shallows, and the marsh-harrier drifts on the wind. These are the sole keepers of this bleak domain.

In the whole of England, there can be no more forlorn a vista than that from the ancient sea-wall near the hamlet of St. Jude’s. As far as the eye can see, the great salt-marsh stretches away, a patchwork of drab greens and glistening browns, dissected by the silver threads of a hundred hidden channels. On the very edge of the world, a low, dark smudge indicates the presence of Foulness Island. In this great expanse, there is no sign of human endeavour, nor of anything that speaks of comfort or of life. There is no warmth in the pale sun, no cheer in the vast, empty sky; above all, there is an absolute and soul-crushing stillness.

It has been said that there is nothing of human endeavour upon the broad marsh. That is not precisely so. Looking out from the sea-wall, one might, if the light is right, discern a single dark shape rising from the reeds some two miles distant. It is the roof of a house, its slates green with lichen, its chimneys stark against the sky. An old causeway, now little more than a line of weed-choked stones, once led to it, but it is now passable only at the lowest of tides. Here and there, dark objects are sunk into the mud alongside the path: the rotted stumps of ancient posts, placed there by hands long since turned to dust. For a hundred years this house had stood empty, a place of local legend and ghost stories.

It was to this very house, in the autumn of eighteen hundred and seventy-one, that two brothers had come. They were men of letters and of private means, twins named Alistair and Julian Croft, and they sought only solitude in which to pursue their arcane studies of Anglo-Saxon history. They were lean, quiet men, with the pale faces of scholars, and they seemed to the local people to be as strange and out of place as the house they had chosen to inhabit. They brought with them a great library of books, and for a year they lived in perfect seclusion, their only contact with the outside world a weekly visit from a boy who brought them supplies. They were content in their isolation, surrounded by the silence of the marsh and the ghosts of a forgotten age. An observer would have said they were two halves of a single soul, living a life of placid intellectual rigour, far from the temptations and troubles of the modern world.

That is not to say that the world had forgotten them entirely, for one man, at least, held them in his thoughts. This was the local physician, a Doctor Armitage, a man of science and of some small repute in the county for his studies of the more obscure maladies of the mind. He had met the brothers upon their first arrival, and had been struck by the intensity of their bond, a thing so profound as to seem almost unnatural. He had called upon them once in their strange new home, and had found them amidst their books, as content as two monks in a scriptorium. Yet he could not shake a certain professional unease; for he knew that such perfect isolation, such a complete turning away from the society of one’s fellows, can breed strange humours in the soul. The marsh, too, he knew to be a place of melancholy, its endless vistas and profound silence apt to weigh heavily upon even the most robust of spirits. He made a mental note to call upon them again before the winter storms made the causeway impassable, for he felt, in some inexplicable way, a sense of foreboding.

His visit was, however, forestalled by a change in the brothers’ quiet life, a change that came not from without, but from the very ground upon which their strange house stood. It was a day of exceptional tides, when the sea drew back its grey curtain farther than any living soul in St. Jude’s could recall. The old causeway, usually a treacherous line of half-submerged stones, lay exposed almost to its full length, a muddy spine stretching out across the glistening flats. Seizing the opportunity, the two brothers, who had not set foot from their property in many months, decided upon a walk, a constitutional to clear their heads from the dust of ancient manuscripts.

It was Julian who saw it first. A glint of yellow in the black ooze beside one of the ancient, rotted posts. Thinking it no more than a shard of coloured glass, he knelt and worked it free from the sucking grip of the mud. It was heavy, far heavier than he expected, and as he wiped away the slime and weed, his breath caught in his throat. It was a torque, a great neck-ring of beaten gold, crafted in the ancient Saxon style, its ends fashioned into the snarling heads of wolves. It was a thing of magnificent, barbaric beauty, a treasure from the very age to which they had dedicated their lives.

He called out to Alistair, his voice trembling with an excitement that was almost reverence. Alistair came, and when he saw the object in his brother’s hand, his pale face became a mask of covetous astonishment. He took it from Julian, his long, scholarly fingers tracing the intricate knotwork, his eyes alight with a fire Julian had not seen before. For a moment, they were united in their triumph, two scholars who had stumbled upon a discovery that would make their names immortal. They carried it back to the house as though it were a holy relic, their quiet routine shattered by the intrusion of this golden ghost from the past.

That night, however, the first shadow fell between them. Alistair, by silent consent the leader in their intellectual pursuits, had taken possession of the torque. He had cleaned it and polished it, and now it lay upon a square of black velvet on the great oak table in their library, gleaming under the lamplight. Julian, who had found it, felt a small, uncharitable pang of resentment. He had wished to hold it again, to feel its weight, but Alistair seemed to guard it, his chair drawn close, his attention fixed upon it to the exclusion of all else.

“It is the work of a master craftsman,” Alistair murmured, his voice hushed. “A king’s ransom. A king’s possession.”

“It is our possession now, brother,” Julian replied, forcing a smile. “A most remarkable find.”

Alistair did not look up. “Yes,” he said, though his tone was distant, as if he were speaking to himself. “Mine.”

The word, though softly spoken, was as sharp as a shard of glass. It was the first time in their lives that the word ‘mine’ had been used to delineate a boundary between them. Julian felt a chill that had nothing to do with the autumn draughts that crept through the old house. He said no more, but retreated to his books, though he found he could not concentrate. His eyes kept straying to the back of his brother’s head, and to the hypnotic gleam of the gold on the table.

In the days that followed, the change in Alistair became more pronounced. An observer, had there been one, would have noted a subtle alteration in his very bearing. He held his head higher, his shoulders squared as if against a heavy cloak. He neglected their shared work on the Saxon chronicles, spending his hours with the torque, studying its every detail, weighing it, and, when he thought himself unobserved, holding it to his neck. His talk was all of the power of the ancient sea-kings who had ruled this coast, of their might and their pitiless justice. He began to affect a more archaic turn of phrase, speaking of his ‘demesne’ when he meant the house, and of the local fishermen as ‘churls’.

Julian watched this transformation with a growing sense of dread. He tried to reason with his brother, to draw him back to their proper work. “Alistair,” he said one evening, as a great gale began to batter the windows, “this obsession is unhealthy. The torque is a historical artifact, not a crown. We must write to the Antiquarian Society, and share our discovery.”

Alistair, who had been staring into the fire, the golden circlet lying in his lap, turned to him slowly. His eyes, usually so mild and thoughtful, held a look of cold command. “Share it?” he said, his voice a low growl. “Does a king share his sceptre? This is a symbol of power, Julian, not a trinket for dusty academics to squabble over. This land yielded it to me, and it is mine to hold.”

“It yielded it to me!” Julian cried, his patience finally snapping. “My hands pulled it from the mud!”

“Your hands served my purpose,” Alistair replied with an imperious wave. “The Fates knew to whom it truly belonged. You were merely the vessel, the instrument of its recovery.”

The arrogance of the statement struck Julian dumb. He saw his brother no more; in his place stood a stranger, a haughty, deluded creature wearing his brother’s face. He fled the room, his heart pounding with a mixture of fear and rage, and locked himself in his bedchamber. Through the door, and above the howling of the wind, he could hear Alistair begin to chant, a strange, guttural verse in the old tongue they had studied, the words rising and falling in a boastful, kingly cadence.

The storm raged for three days, a furious siege of wind and water that turned the marsh into a boiling sea and shook the old house to its very foundations. The causeway vanished completely, and they were as marooned as sailors on a rock. In this enforced confinement, the madness, for Julian could call it nothing else, took complete hold of Alistair. He now wore the torque constantly, the snarling wolf-heads nestled in the hollow of his throat. He ate little, but drank deeply of their stock of wine, striding about the library, declaiming upon his imagined kingdom, his voice ringing with a terrifying authority. He no longer called Julian ‘brother’, but ‘thane’, or, when displeased, ‘thrall’.

Julian, for his part, remained cloistered in his room, a prisoner in his own home. He was filled with a terrible, consuming grief for the brother he had lost, and a primal fear of the man who had taken his place. He listened to the mad pronouncements from the library, to the sound of Alistair smashing a chair in a fit of rage, and he knew that this could not continue. The storm that raged without was nothing to the tempest that had broken within the walls of their sanctuary. He resolved that, when the gale abated, he would make a desperate attempt to cross the marsh and fetch help. But first, he knew, he must separate Alistair from the source of his mania. He must take back the golden torque.

On the third night, the storm reached its apotheosis. Rain lashed the windows like flung gravel, and the chimney-stack moaned with the passage of the wind. Julian, unable to sleep, heard a sudden crash from below, followed by a roar of fury. Peering from his room, he saw that the great window in the library had finally given way, and the storm was pouring in, sending books and papers flying. In the centre of the chaos stood Alistair, his hair wild, his face a mask of primal fury, screaming defiance at the wind as if it were a rival army.

This was the moment. Now, while his brother was distracted by his war with the elements, Julian crept down the stairs. The library was a scene of utter destruction. The lamplight flickered wildly, casting monstrous, dancing shadows. Alistair stood with his back to him, his hands raised to the shattered window. The golden torque seemed to burn with a cold light against his pale neck.

“Alistair,” Julian whispered, his voice shaking.

The figure turned, and the face was not that of a scholar, but of a cornered warlord. The eyes were wide and mad. “You dare?” Alistair hissed. “You come to me now, skulking like a thief, when my hall is breached by the sea-devils?”

“Give it to me, brother,” Julian pleaded, taking a step forward. “Give me the gold. Let us end this madness.”

Alistair laughed, a harsh, grating sound. “My crown? You would usurp me? I, who am king of this wasteland? I will see you in the mud first!”

He lunged, not like a man of letters, but with the ferocious speed of a predator. Julian, though slighter of build, was prepared for it. He sidestepped the clumsy rush, and the two brothers, the two halves of a single soul, crashed together in a desperate, pathetic struggle. It was a whirlwind of flailing limbs and guttural cries, a conflict made grotesque by its setting amidst the ruins of their scholarly life. They wrestled across the floor, slipping on wet pages, knocking over stacks of priceless books. Julian’s only thought was to get his hands on the torque, to wrench the cursed thing from his brother’s neck.

He managed to get a grip on the cold metal. Alistair roared in pain and fury and slammed Julian’s head against the stone mantelpiece of the great fireplace. The room swam before Julian’s eyes, and his grip slackened. Alistair rose above him, his face contorted, his hands reaching for Julian’s throat.

“I should have cast you out long ago,” he snarled, the firelight glinting in his crazed eyes. “You have no place in my kingdom!”

At that very instant, a bolt of lightning, of a brilliance that seemed to turn the world white, struck the ancient chimney-stack directly above them. There was a crack like the world’s end, a deafening explosion that was part thunder and part the tearing of ancient mortar. For a second, Alistair was silhouetted against a flash of impossible light, his mouth open in a silent scream. A roar of falling stone and brick followed, and the great hearth, the very heart of the house, collapsed inwards. Alistair vanished in an avalanche of soot-blackened masonry and shattered stone.

Then, silence. A silence more profound than any the marsh had ever known, broken only by the hiss of the rain on the newly-formed pile of rubble and the soft flutter of a single, sodden page as it drifted to the floor.

Julian lay there for a long time, dazed and bleeding, the dust settling around him. Slowly, painfully, he pushed himself to his feet. Where his brother had stood, there was now only a jagged cairn of ruin. He called Alistair’s name, once, twice, his voice a broken croak. There was no reply. Protruding from the top of the pile, gleaming even in the dim, chaotic light, was a curve of yellow metal. The torque. It had been thrown clear, and lay there, untarnished and malevolent.

He did not know how long he spent digging, his bare hands scrabbling at the sharp-edged stones, but when the grey, weeping dawn finally came, he knew it was hopeless. His brother was gone, buried in the heart of the house they had chosen as their refuge. The storm had passed, and a weak, watery sunlight was beginning to touch the marsh.

Numb with a grief so vast it felt like a physical void, Julian picked up the golden torque. It felt cold and heavy, a dead weight in his hand. He looked at it, at the snarling wolves and the intricate, pitiless designs, and he was filled with a hatred so pure it was almost a form of madness itself.

Without a thought for his own hurts, he stumbled out of the ruined house and onto the causeway. The tide was low again. He walked, his feet sinking into the mud, until he reached the deep channel that snaked through the flats. He stood there for a moment, a solitary, black-clad figure in the immense, grey landscape. He looked at the torque one last time, this treasure of a forgotten age, this catalyst of his own personal ruin. Then, with all the strength he had left, he drew back his arm and hurled it out into the water. It traced a brief, golden arc against the sky, and vanished into the dark, swirling current with a faint splash.

He did not wait to see the ripples fade. He turned and walked back towards the broken house, his house. The silence of the marsh pressed in on him, but it was no longer the companionable silence of solitude. It was the absolute, soul-crushing stillness of utter desolation; a hollow echo in the chamber of his heart where his brother had once been. An observer would have said he was a man who had at last found the perfect solitude he had sought, a man utterly and completely alone in a world devoid of life or comfort.

For three days he had tasted no water; for a week, no food had passed his lips save for a few dried roots he had clawed from the parched earth. He had been one of a hopeful party of twenty, and he alone now drew breath. The others, men, women, and children, were but scattered white objects upon the plain behind him, their bones a grim testament to the desert’s victory.

His name was John Ferrier, and he had come west in search of fortune. He had found only ruin. Now, his quest was not for gold but for a swift and merciful end. He had climbed to this vantage point not to seek a path forward, but to choose a place to die, a final resting place from which his vacant eyes might survey the dominion of his destroyer. He raised the heavy rifle, not with any intent to use it, for his pouch was empty of shot, but as a crutch, a last companion in his utter solitude. His gaze swept the horizon one final time, a silent, bitter farewell to a world that had so thoroughly defeated him. The silence was, as ever, absolute; a palpable weight that pressed upon the eardrums and seemed to squeeze the very air from his lungs.

It was in this profound stillness that he first perceived it. Not a sound, for there was none, but a discrepancy. A flaw in the perfect, monotonous desolation. Far below, near a cluster of the ubiquitous chaparral bushes, lay a small patch of colour, a scrap of blue against the universal grey. At first, he took it for a trick of his failing vision, a phantasm born of hunger and despair. He closed his burning eyes, and when he opened them again, it was still there. Curiosity, that last and most stubborn of human instincts, stirred within him. With a groan that was half a sob, he began the slow, tortuous descent from the rocky outcrop.

Each step was an agony. The sharp stones cut into his worn boots, and his knees trembled with a weakness that threatened to pitch him headlong into the alkali dust. Yet he pushed on, drawn by that inexplicable spot of colour. As he drew nearer, the patch resolved itself into a piece of fabric, a child’s dress by the look of it, and it was not lying still upon the ground. It moved. It was a slight, fluttering motion, but in that lifeless landscape, it was as startling as a thunderclap.

He broke into a stumbling run, his heart hammering against his ribs with a force he had not felt for days. He rounded the clump of dwarfish bushes and stopped dead, his breath catching in his throat. There, lying in the meagre shade, was the body of a woman. Her face was turned to the sky, her expression one of peaceful release, but it was not her that held his gaze. Clinging to her still form was a little girl, no more than five years of age. It was she who wore the blue dress, and she who now looked up at him, her eyes wide with a terror that rendered her mute. She was as gaunt and as haggard as he was, her face smudged with dirt and tears, but she was alive. Impossibly, miraculously, alive.

For a long moment, the man and the child simply stared at one another, two shipwrecked souls on a vast and empty ocean. The traveller, John Ferrier, felt a sensation so alien that he could not at first place it. It was a pang in the region of his heart, a feeling that went beyond his own selfish misery. He had come to this place to die, and he had found a reason to live.

He slowly sank to his knees, taking care not to make any sudden movement that might frighten the child further. “It’s all right,” he said, his voice a dry, rasping croak. “I won’t hurt you.” He unslung the water-canteen from his shoulder. He knew it to be all but empty; he had drained the last few drops that morning. But he shook it, and was rewarded with the faintest slosh. With trembling hands, he unscrewed the cap and tilted it towards the child’s cracked lips. A few precious, lukewarm drops trickled out. She drank them greedily, her eyes never leaving his face.

When the canteen was truly empty, he looked around him. The mother was clearly beyond any help. He gently disengaged the child’s grasp from her dress and, with a strength he did not know he possessed, drew the little girl into his arms. She was light, so terribly light, little more than skin and bone, but she was warm. He looked at the woman’s peaceful face. “I’ll look after her,” he whispered to the dead. “I swear it.”

He could not linger. The sun was beginning its inexorable descent, and the desert nights were as perilous as the days. He stood, the child held securely against his chest. She had not spoken a word, but her small arms had wound their way around his neck. He retrieved his rifle, now not a crutch but a weapon once more, a tool to protect this new and fragile charge. He cast one last look at the endless, ghastly caravan route behind them, the trail of bones that had almost claimed him. Then he turned his face towards the setting sun and began to walk. He did not know where he was going, or what succour he might find there. He knew only that he was no longer alone, and that knowledge lent him a strength that was not of the body, but of the soul.

For two days they journeyed, a grim and silent pair. The man would walk until his legs buckled, rest for an hour with the child sleeping in his arms, and then rise to stumble on again. He found a brackish, shallow pool, thick with alkali, but which provided enough moisture to keep the fever of thirst at bay. He snared a lean and stringy lizard, and they shared its flesh, cooked over a tiny fire of chaparral twigs. During the day, the child, whose name he learned was Lucy, would point out shapes in the clouds or the shimmering heat haze. At night, she would sleep, her small body curled against his for warmth. He was her whole world, her protector, her beast of burden, her only hope. And she, in turn, was his. She was the anchor that kept his mind from drifting away on the tides of delirium; she was the purpose that drove his failing limbs onward.

On the evening of the third day, his strength gave out. He had seen a line of low-lying hills on the horizon and had been making for them, a last, desperate gamble. Now, with the hills still a hazy dream in the distance, he collapsed onto the sand, the world spinning into a vortex of blackness. He was dimly aware of Lucy’s small hand on his cheek, of her frightened voice calling his name. “John,” she was saying, “John, wake up.” He had failed. After all his struggles, after this brief, brilliant flare of hope, the desert would claim them both. He closed his eyes, and surrendered to the inevitable.

He was roused by a sound. It was a low, rumbling noise, a sound he had not heard in what felt like a lifetime. The creak of wheels, the lowing of cattle, the murmur of human voices. He forced his eyelids open. The world was a blur, but he could make out a great cloud of dust moving towards them, a cloud from which these impossible sounds were emanating. It was a caravan. A vast train of wagons, scores of them, was winding its way across the plain, a moving city in the heart of the wasteland.

Hope, sharp and painful, pierced through the fog of his exhaustion. He tried to rise, to call out, but he had no strength left. He could only lie there, his hand protectively on Lucy’s shoulder, and watch as the lead wagon drew nearer. It was a large, canvas-covered vehicle, drawn by a team of six oxen. Men walked alongside it, armed with rifles, their faces hard and bearded, burned dark by the sun.

The caravan halted, and a man dismounted from a horse beside the lead wagon. He was tall and broad-shouldered, with a great, flowing brown beard and piercing grey eyes that seemed to see into a man’s very soul. He strode towards them, his expression one of stern curiosity. He looked down at the prostrate man and the terrified child, taking in their skeletal frames, their ragged clothes, their desperate plight.

“Who are you?” the man demanded, his voice a deep baritone that held the unmistakable ring of authority.

John Ferrier moistened his lips. “A traveller,” he rasped. “My name is John Ferrier. This is Lucy. We are the last of our party.”

The leader’s keen eyes surveyed the scene, noting Ferrier’s rifle, his protective posture over the child, the utter desolation surrounding them. “From whence have you come?”

The leader was silent for a moment, his gaze unreadable. He looked from Ferrier to the child, and a flicker of something akin to compassion softened his stern features. Ferrier drank, the cool water a blessed miracle on his parched throat, and then ensured that Lucy drank her fill.

The leader’s grey eyes fixed him with an intense, searching stare. “You owe your lives to the providence of the Lord, who has led us to you in your hour of need. We are a people apart, a chosen flock exiled from the lands of the Gentiles. Ferrier, a simple, non-religious man, could only answer with the truth. “I have no faith but in my own two hands, sir. And in my duty to this child.”

But a man cannot live by his own hands alone in this land; he must have the support of his brethren. And a child cannot be raised without a community. We will give you both. We will take you in, feed you, clothe you, protect you. We will make you one of us.” He knelt down, so his face was level with Ferrier’s. “All we ask in return is that you embrace the truth of our Prophet, and accept the celestial bonds of our community. Your life, and the life of this child, for your allegiance. What say you?”

John Ferrier looked from the Prophet’s powerful, unyielding face to the small, trusting countenance of the child beside him. He saw the great train of wagons, the hardy men and women, the promise of food, water, and safety. He saw the alternative in the bleached bones that lay scattered across the plain behind him. The choice was no choice at all.

“I accept,” he said, his voice gaining a sliver of its former strength.

The leader nodded, satisfied. “It is well. Rise, John Ferrier, and be welcomed into the bosom of the Saints.” Strong hands helped him to his feet, and a woman with a kind face came and took Lucy, cooing softly to her and promising her milk and a soft bed. Ferrier watched them go, a profound sense of relief washing over him. He had placed his fate, and Lucy’s, into the hands of these stern, zealous people. He had bought their survival at a price he did not yet understand. Their journey through the wilderness had ended; but a new journey, of a far different and more complex nature, was about to begin.

A strange uniformity of feature and of dress pervaded them. Each was clad in the same coarse, homespun garments, and an expression of placid and uncomplaining fortitude was stamped upon each and every face. They were a people of one mind and one purpose, and that purpose was the establishment of their faith in the heart of the wilderness. The journey continued for many weeks. Day after day, the long procession of pioneers crept across the desolate landscape. They forded rivers, they traversed mountain passes, and they endured the scorching sun of the plains. Yet through it all, there was no dissent, no grumbling. At dawn and at dusk, the entire company would assemble, and the voice of the Prophet would rise in prayer and exhortation, reminding his flock of the glorious destiny that awaited them. Ferrier, a practical man with little time for divinity, listened to these sermons with a sceptical heart; but he held his tongue, for he knew that his life, and what was more, Lucy’s life, depended upon his silent acquiescence. He did the work assigned to him, hunting for game to supply the communal pot, and his skill with a rifle soon earned him a measure of respect among the brethren.

At last, after a journey which seemed to have no end, they came to a halt. They had reached a great, wide valley, cupped in a circle of snow-capped mountains. A river wound its way through the valley floor, and a large lake glistened in the distance like a sheet of silver. Here, the Prophet declared, was their journey’s end. This was the place which the Lord had chosen for them; this was the Zion which they had been promised. A great cry of joy and thanksgiving went up from the assembled multitude, and they fell upon their knees to praise the God who had led them through the wilderness to this new Eden.

Years rolled by, and the desert began to blossom under the unremitting toil of the Saints. A city rose from the dust of the valley, a city of wide streets and pleasant houses, each with its own garden. Fields were ploughed, crops were sown, and the barren land yielded a rich harvest. The settlement of Salt Lake City became a by-word for industry and prosperity. And in all the community, there were no two who prospered more than John Ferrier and his adopted daughter, Lucy.

Ferrier, by his shrewdness and his unflagging industry, had turned the small plot of land allotted to him into one of the most productive farms in the entire valley. His herds multiplied, his granaries were full, and he became a man of considerable substance. Yet he remained what he had always been: an outsider. He conformed to the outward observances of the Mormon faith, attending their services and paying his tithes, but he never truly became one of them. He was respected for his wealth and his integrity, but he was not loved, for the brethren sensed that in his heart he was not of their persuasion.

It was for Lucy’s sake that he bore this isolation. As she grew from a child into a woman, her beauty became a legend in the community. She was the flower of Utah, a lily blooming in the desert. Her gentle nature and her sweet disposition endeared her to all, and John Ferrier guarded her as a dragon guards its treasure. He lavished upon her all the affection of his lonely heart, and he dreamed of a future for her in which she would be free to choose her own path, far from the rigid constraints of the Saints.

This, however, was a dream that was not to be realised. In the theocracy of Utah, the will of the individual counted for little against the will of the community, as expressed through its leaders. The Prophet, Brigham Young, ruled with an authority that was both temporal and spiritual. His word was law, and his decisions were not to be questioned. Below him was the Council of Elders, men who had been with him from the beginning, and who shared in his power. Two of the most influential of these were Elder Stangerson and Elder Drebber, men who had grown rich and powerful in the new settlement. Both were widowers, and both had sons who had come of age.

As Lucy blossomed into womanhood, the eyes of these powerful men turned towards her. A union with the daughter of the wealthy John Ferrier was a prize worth seeking, not only for the girl’s own charms, but for the worldly goods that would one day be hers. It was the established custom of the Saints that a man might take more than one wife, and the harems of the Elders were already well-stocked. It was considered a great honour for a maiden to be admitted into the family of one of the leaders of the church, a sign of divine favour.

One day, the Prophet himself paid a visit to Ferrier’s farm. He was an imposing figure, with his shrewd, calculating eyes and his air of absolute authority. He spoke to Ferrier of his prosperity, and praised his industry. Then, coming to the point, he spoke of Lucy.

“Your daughter has come of age, Brother Ferrier,” he said, his voice smooth and persuasive. “She is a credit to the community, a model of virtue and piety. It is time that she was sealed to a worthy man, for the glory of the church and the increase of the faithful. There are two young men, the sons of Elder Stangerson and Elder Drebber, who have sought her hand. They are fine, upstanding youths, and a credit to their fathers. You would do well to consider their suit.”

A cold dread settled upon John Ferrier’s heart. He had seen the way the two young men, coarse and brutish youths who took after their fathers, had looked at Lucy. The thought of his gentle, sensitive daughter being yoked to either of them was a horror beyond words.

“My daughter is young, Your Excellency,” he stammered. “She has no thought of marriage yet.”

“She is seventeen,” replied the Prophet, a hard glint in his eye. “It is her duty to marry, and your duty to see that she marries within the faith. I give you a month to consider the matter. At the end of that time, she must choose between Joseph Stangerson and Enoch Drebber. It is the will of the Lord.”

He turned and left, leaving Ferrier in a state of shock and despair. The choice he had been offered was no choice at all. It was a command, a decree from which there was no appeal. To defy the Prophet was to defy the entire community, to risk ruin and perhaps even death. For there was a darker side to the Mormon paradise, a secret society of enforcers known as the Danite Band, or the Avenging Angels, who dealt with those who transgressed against the authority of the church.

Ferrier did not tell Lucy of the Prophet’s visit, but he could not hide his anxiety. She saw the worry in his eyes, the new lines of care that had appeared on his face. She, too, had a secret, a secret that she had been cherishing in her heart.

Some months before, a young man had come to the valley, a Gentile prospector on his way to the silver mines of Nevada. His name was Jefferson Hope, and he was a tall, fine-looking fellow, with a frank and open manner and a spirit of adventure that was a world away from the dour piety of the young Mormon men. He and Lucy had met by chance, and had been instantly drawn to each other. Their meetings had been brief and secret, snatched moments by the river bank or in the shadow of the mountains. They had spoken of love, and of a future together, a future in a world where they could be free.

Now, as she saw the shadow that had fallen over her father, Lucy confessed her secret to him. Ferrier listened in silence, his face a mask of conflicting emotions. He was horrified at the risk she had taken, but at the same time, a spark of hope was kindled in his heart. This young man, this Jefferson Hope, might be the means of their salvation.

“He is a good man, Father,” Lucy said, her eyes pleading. “He loves me, and I love him. He has promised to come back for me, and to take us both away from this place.”

Ferrier made a decision. He would not sacrifice his daughter on the altar of a faith he did not believe in. He would defy the Prophet, and he would trust in this young stranger to deliver them. He wrote a letter to Jefferson Hope, telling him of their peril and urging him to come with all speed. He gave the letter to a friendly Gentile trader, who promised to see it delivered.

Then began a period of anxious waiting. The month that the Prophet had granted them was slipping by. Every day, Ferrier found a small, ominous mark scrawled upon his door, a sign that he and his daughter were under the watch of the Avenging Angels. The faces of his neighbours grew cold and hostile. He was a marked man, an apostate in the making.

On the last day of the allotted month, as despair was beginning to close in, there came a soft whistle from the darkness outside the farmhouse. It was the signal that he had arranged with Jefferson Hope. Ferrier’s heart leaped with joy. He opened the door, and the young prospector slipped inside, his face grim and determined.

The escape was hastily planned. Lucy, her face pale but resolute, packed a small bundle of belongings. Ferrier gathered up all the cash and portable valuables that he possessed. They crept out of the house like thieves in the night, and made their way to the canyon where Hope was waiting. The stars were their only guide as they rode out into the wilderness, leaving behind them the city of the Saints and the life they had known.

For two days they fled, pushing their horses to the limits of their endurance. They travelled by night and hid during the day, terrified of the pursuit that they knew must surely follow. On the third night, they reached the foothills of the mountains. They were exhausted and hungry, but their spirits were high, for they believed they had made good their escape. They made a small camp in a secluded ravine, and for the first time in weeks, John Ferrier allowed himself to sleep the sleep of the just.

It was a sleep from which he was to be rudely awakened. He was roused by a shout of alarm from Jefferson Hope, who had been keeping watch. Ferrier sprang to his feet, his heart pounding. A desperate fight ensued, but the odds were hopeless. Jefferson Hope, fighting like a cornered tiger, was struck down by a blow from behind. Ferrier, old as he was, fought with the strength of desperation, but he too was quickly overpowered. Lucy, screaming in terror, was seized by two of the masked men.

The leader of the band, a man whose voice Ferrier recognised with a chill of horror as that of Elder Stangerson, stepped forward.

“John Ferrier,” he said, his voice cold and merciless, “you have defied the Prophet and betrayed the covenant. You have sought to flee from the justice of the Lord. Ferrier, bound and helpless, could only watch in agony as his daughter was dragged away. He cried out her name, but his voice was lost in the vast, uncaring wilderness. His captors rode away, leaving him alone in the desert, without food, without water, without hope.

He was not, however, entirely alone. Lying senseless on the ground a few feet away was the young man who had tried to save them. Jefferson Hope was still breathing, though his life hung by a thread. The Avenging Angels, in their haste, had left him for dead.

As the first light of dawn broke over the mountains, Jefferson Hope stirred and opened his eyes. He saw the empty camp, the bound figure of John Ferrier, and he understood. A great and terrible change came over his face. The boyish good-humour was gone, replaced by a grim and terrible resolve. He managed to crawl over to Ferrier and, with his teeth, gnaw through the ropes that bound him.

The old man looked up at the mountains, his eyes filled with a terrible, vacant despair. “My life is over,” he said. “There is nothing left for me but to die.”

And so, Jefferson Hope left him. He left him sitting there in the dust, a broken man waiting for the end. He himself had a purpose now, a purpose that would consume his life. He would follow them. He would haunt them. He would be their shadow and their doom. He had lost his love, but he had found a mission. The mission of revenge.

Months bled into years. The story of the stolen bride and the Avenging Angels became a whispered legend amongst the gentiles on the frontier, a dark tale of fanaticism and cruelty. Jefferson Hope, the ghost at the edge of the tale, listened to it all, his face a mask of stone. He learned that Lucy Ferrier had indeed been forced into a marriage with the younger Drebber, a brutish lout who saw her only as a prize. He heard that she, who had been so full of life and laughter, had become a silent, pale shadow, her heart broken beyond repair.

This knowledge was a constant spur, goading him, denying him a moment’s peace. He could not storm the city of the Saints alone; he knew that to be madness. The Prophet’s power was absolute, his spies everywhere. To be captured was to be executed without trial or mercy. So Hope waited, his patience as vast and terrible as the desert itself. He heard from a traveling merchant that Joseph Stangerson and Enoch Drebber, enriched by the confiscated property of John Ferrier and others, had risen high in the church. He was leaner, harder, his face tanned to the colour of old leather and his eyes holding the steady, watchful gaze of a predator. The city was a fortress, its people a single, cohesive unit bound by faith and fear. Every robed figure was an enemy; every glance a potential betrayal. Hope kept his head down, finding menial work and lodging in the poorest quarter of the city. He moved like a wraith through the streets, his heart a cold, heavy stone in his chest. The sight sent a surge of white-hot fury through Jefferson Hope, so powerful that he gripped the stone wall he hid behind to steady himself.

He knew then that a simple message would not suffice. He had to get her away, to spirit her from this prison. He found a man, a gentile outcast like himself, who for a high price was willing to keep a wagon and horses ready at a designated spot outside the city walls.

He did not know how long he wandered. He found himself back in the mining camps, taking the most dangerous jobs, courting death as a release from his torment. But death would not have him. He seemed to bear a charmed life, surviving rock-falls and tunnel collapses that killed men on either side of him.

He spoke of the wealthy Elder Drebber, whose young wife, the daughter of the apostate Ferrier, had wasted away and died. Of a broken heart, some said. Jefferson Hope heard the words, and a great and terrible calm descended upon him. He stood up from his corner table and walked out of the saloon, leaving his half-finished drink behind. He did not weep; the wellspring of his tears had long since run dry, baked into salt by the desert sun and the heat of his hatred. The despair was gone, burned away in an instant. He would be the instrument of the fate they had so long evaded.

His new mission began at once. He learned that soon after Lucy’s death, the schism he had heard of had broken wide open. Drebber and Stangerson, having fallen from the Prophet’s favour, had wisely chosen to liquidate their ill-gotten wealth and flee Utah. A strange ailment had taken root in his chest, a throbbing torment that his doctors named an aneurism, a weakness of the great artery near the heart. They told him that any great shock or exertion might prove instantly fatal. The diagnosis, which might have crushed another man, only hardened Hope’s resolve. He was living on borrowed time; he had a debt to collect before his own account was closed.

He had almost lost their trail completely. For years, there was no word, no clue. He drifted, working odd jobs, his health failing, the fire of his vengeance banked but never extinguished. It was in New York, amidst the squalor of the docks, that the scent, grown cold for so long, became fresh once more. They had fled to the Old World, to the great wilderness of London.

With the last of his money, Jefferson Hope worked his passage across the Atlantic on a cattle-boat. He arrived in London, a penniless, ailing man, a stranger in a strange and monstrous city. He became a cabman, one of the thousands who plied their trade upon the teeming streets. He arrived in London, a penniless, ailing man, a stranger in a strange and monstrous city. But the sight of the endless rows of houses, the teaming, anonymous crowds, did not daunt him. It was, he thought, the perfect hunting-ground. In this labyrinth of brick and fog, he could draw the net around his quarry. He needed a way to search the city, to be everywhere at once, to observe without being observed. The answer was obvious. He became a cabman, one of the thousands who plied their trade upon the teeming streets. And so he drove, day and night, his eyes scanning every face in the crowd, a dying man with a single, terrible purpose: to find the two men who had destroyed his life, and to bring upon them a vengeance that had been nursed for two decades in the bitter wilderness of a broken heart.

For weeks that stretched into months, his quest yielded nothing. London was a far greater and more confusing warren than any American city he had known. The endless procession of faces, pale and hurried in the grey light, began to blur into one another. He learned the thoroughfares and the back alleys, the grand squares of the wealthy and the squalid courts of the poor. He listened to the gossip of his fellow cabmen at the ranks, picking up scraps of information about the doings of the city, ever watchful for a name or a description that might match his quarry. His health continued to fail. Each morning he awoke with a dull, throbbing pain in his chest, a grim reminder that his own time was short. There were days when despair was a physical weight upon him, and the thought of giving up, of simply letting his disease claim him in some forgotten garret, seemed a sweet release. Yet the memory of Lucy’s face, and the image of her father lying dead in the desert, would always rekindle the fire within him. His was not a common desire for revenge; it was, to his mind, a sacred duty, the only reason for his continued existence.

The moment, when it came, was as sudden and as mundane as to be almost anticlimactic. He was waiting in a long line of cabs outside the Criterion Theatre on a wet Tuesday evening, the gas lamps casting greasy halos upon the slick pavement. A crowd of theatre-goers was spilling out, the men in their evening dress, the ladies gathering their wraps against the drizzle. And then he saw him. Enoch Drebber, florid and portly as ever, was standing under the awning, arguing loudly with a commissionaire. He was drunk, his face flushed, his gestures extravagant and belligerent. Age had not been kind to him; it had merely accentuated the coarseness of his features and the arrogance of his bearing.

For an instant, Jefferson Hope was frozen. The blood rushed to his head with such force that he felt a sharp, lancing pain in his chest, and for a terrifying second he thought the aneurism had burst. He gripped the side of his cab, his knuckles white, and took a deep, shuddering breath. The hatred, cold and patient for so long, now surged through him like a fever. He watched as Drebber, having lost his argument, was joined by a younger man and hailed another hansom. Hope pulled his own cab out of the line, his movements swift and sure, and followed them at a discreet distance.

He tracked them to a private boarding house, Charpentier’s, in Torquay Terrace. This, he now knew, was Drebber’s lair. His first instinct was to leap from his cab, to confront the man then and there, and to end his long hunt with a single act of violence. But his grim patience, honed over twenty years, asserted itself. A simple murder was not what he sought. His brand of justice required something more, a ceremony of retribution. For years he had carried the idea in his mind, a ghastly lottery of fate. He went to a chemist and, with a plausible story of needing to put down a suffering old dog, procured a quantity of a swift and potent poison. Back in his miserable room, he carefully worked two pills from a piece of bread, dissolving the poison into one and leaving the other entirely harmless. He placed them in a small pillbox, identical in every way. It was to be a trial by ordeal. He would be the accuser and the judge, but he would let God, or chance, be the executioner. Drebber would be given a fifty percent chance of survival, a far greater mercy than he had ever shown to Lucy or her father.

Now that he had found one, finding the other was a simpler matter. He kept a constant watch on the boarding house. He saw Drebber coming and going, always loud, often intoxicated. He also saw Joseph Stangerson, his face sallow and lined with a perpetual anxiety, visiting Drebber. The sight of his second tormentor, the man who had shot him and left him for dead, stirred a different, colder fury in his heart. Stangerson was Drebber’s secretary, it seemed, and their relationship was evidently a strained one. Hope overheard their loud arguments on more than one occasion.

His opportunity came on a night of driving rain. He had been loitering with his cab near Torquay Terrace when he saw a commotion at the door of Charpentier’s. Drebber, in a state of advanced intoxication, was being forcibly ejected from the house by a young man, whom Hope later learned was the son of the proprietress. Drebber stood swaying in the street, shouting curses back at the house, a solitary and pathetic figure in the downpour. It was perfect. Jefferson Hope flicked his reins and drew his cab up beside the drunken man.

“Cab, sir?” he called out, his voice low and steady.

Drebber, blinded by rage and rain, stumbled towards the vehicle without a second glance at the driver. “Take me to… take me to the devil, for all I care!” he slurred, climbing heavily inside. “Just get me away from this wretched place.”

Hope closed the door and set the horse into a walk, turning not towards the brighter, more populated streets, but into a warren of dark and deserted lanes. For several minutes, neither man spoke. Drebber slumped in a drunken stupor in the back, while Hope, his heart hammering against his ribs, drove them towards a place of reckoning he had scouted for just such a purpose: an empty house in a neglected street in Brixton. When he finally pulled the horse to a halt, the silence was broken only by the drumming of the rain on the cab’s roof.

“We’re here,” Hope said, his voice a guttural whisper.

Drebber stirred. “Here? Where in blazes are we? This isn’t my hotel.” He tried to open the door, but found it held fast. A flicker of alarm penetrated his drunken haze. “What is the meaning of this, cabman? Let me out!”

Jefferson Hope slowly turned on his seat, the dim light from a distant gas lamp falling upon his gaunt and haggard face. “It has been a long time, Enoch Drebber,” he said.

Drebber stared, his jaw slackening. Recognition dawned in his eyes, followed by a wave of pure, abject terror. He shrank back into the corner of the cab, his florid face turning a pasty white. “You!” he gasped. “Jefferson Hope!”

“I am,” said the cabman. “I have come a long way to find you. All the way from the salt plains of Utah. You took everything from me, Drebber. You and Stangerson. You destroyed the woman I loved, and you murdered her father. For twenty years, I have followed you, and now the account is to be settled.”

He produced the small pillbox. “I am not a common murderer,” he continued, his voice resonating with a strange, solemn fervour. “I mean to offer you a chance. In this box are two pills. One is harmless. The other is instant death. I will take one, and you will take the other. We will leave it to the justice of Heaven to decide which of us is to live and which is to die.”

Drebber, paralysed with fear, could only stare at the box. The choice, so grim and yet so simple, seemed to shatter the last of his drunken bravado. He began to plead, to beg for his life, offering money, anything, but Hope was implacable. With a hand as steady as stone, he opened the box and held it out. Trembling, Drebber’s fingers fumbled for one of the pills and he swallowed it. Hope took the other and did the same.

For a moment, they sat in utter silence. Then a terrible convulsion seized Drebber. A look of supreme horror and agony contorted his features, his eyes bulged, and he fell forward. He was dead before his body hit the floor of the cab. Jefferson Hope’s pill had been the harmless one. Providence, it seemed, had sided with the avenger.

A profound calm descended upon Hope. He felt no elation, only a grim satisfaction, the quiet peace of a task long overdue and finally completed. He dragged Drebber’s body from the cab and into the empty, dilapidated house. As he did so, a small object fell from Drebber’s finger and tinkled upon the floorboards. Hope bent and picked it up. It was a simple gold wedding ring. He stared at it, and a sudden, fierce thought struck him. It was Lucy’s ring, the one she had been forced to wear. He had it back. In his haste, however, as he propped the body against the wall, his nose began to bleed, a consequence of the immense excitement. An idea, cunning and vengeful, came to him. He dipped his finger in the blood and, on the wall above the corpse, he scrawled the single word: RACHE. It was German for revenge, a red herring to confound the official police he so despised. Then he pocketed the ring, returned to his cab, and vanished into the London night.

His work was only half done. Now he had to find Stangerson. He knew the secretary would be in a state of terror after Drebber’s disappearance and death, which would soon be all over the papers. He would either flee or go to ground. To forestall his escape, Hope acted quickly. He placed an advertisement in several newspapers concerning Drebber’s movements on the night of his death, a veiled message that only Stangerson would understand, intended to sow confusion and panic. He also enlisted the help of some of the street children he had befriended, a network of eyes and ears that was more effective than any police force.

It was one of these urchins who brought him the news he needed. Stangerson had not fled the country, but had taken rooms at Halliday’s Private Hotel, a quiet establishment near the centre of the city. Hope knew he had to act at once. This time, there would be no luring his victim into a cab. Stangerson was on his guard. A direct assault was the only option.

That night, armed with a small ladder he had procured, he made his way to the hotel. He found Stangerson’s window, which was on a lower floor, and quietly set his ladder against the wall. The thought of his own precarious health crossed his mind; the exertion of the climb might be enough to kill him. But the thought was fleeting. He was a man possessed by a single idea, and his own life was of no consequence.

He slipped through the unlocked window into the darkened room. A low light was burning, and by its glow he saw Stangerson, not in bed, but sitting fully dressed in a chair, his face pale and haggard. The man had clearly not slept, living in dread of the very thing which was now upon him. As Hope stepped into the room, Stangerson looked up, and a low cry of horror escaped his lips.

“The time has come, Joseph Stangerson,” Hope said, his voice a low rasp. He advanced into the room, holding out the pillbox once again. “You know why I am here. Your partner in crime has already faced his judgment. Now it is your turn. Choose your pill.”

But Stangerson was made of sterner, or perhaps more desperate, stuff than Drebber. He had no intention of participating in this ghastly ritual. With a snarl of fury, he snatched up a heavy stick from the corner and lunged at Hope. The avenger, weakened by disease and exhaustion, was taken by surprise. Stangerson struck a savage blow at his head, which Hope only partially parried. The two men grappled, crashing about the small hotel room in a desperate, silent struggle. Hope was the stronger man, but Stangerson fought with the frenzy of a cornered rat. He was trying to get to the window, to shout for help. Hope knew he could not allow it. As they wrestled near the bed, Hope’s hand closed on the hilt of the long knife he always carried. He had not intended to use it, for it spoiled the grim symmetry of his justice, but Stangerson gave him no choice. He drove the blade deep into the secretary’s side. Stangerson gasped, his struggles ceased, and he collapsed onto the floor, dead.

Breathing heavily, his head reeling from the blow he had received, Hope stood over the body. Once more, he used the blood to write his grim signature, RACHE, upon the wall. He placed the pillbox, containing the one deadly and one harmless pill, upon the bedside table, a testament to his original intent. His work was done. Both men who had ruined his life were now dead. He slipped back out the window and down the ladder, melting away into the London darkness just as the first sounds of alarm were beginning to stir within the hotel.

It was in the aftermath of this second act that he made his fatal error. In the struggle with Drebber in the empty house, in his haste and excitement, he had dropped the ring. Lucy’s wedding ring. He did not realise his loss until the next day. The discovery filled him with a disproportionate sense of panic. That ring was the only tangible thing he had left of her, a holy relic of his lost love. He had to get it back. It was this one sentimental weakness that would be his undoing. He sent a friend, an old woman who sometimes helped him, to the police station to answer the advertisement he had seen in the evening paper, an advertisement for a plain gold ring found at the scene of the Brixton mystery.

The reply sent him to an address: 221B Baker Street. He was to ask for a Mr. Sherlock Holmes. Hope was wary. He knew it was a trap. The police would not advertise for the owner of such a crucial piece of evidence; this Holmes must be the private detective the papers had mentioned, the one whose theories were so at odds with those of the official force. And yet, the desire to reclaim the ring was overwhelming. He resolved to go, but to be on his guard. He was a dying man in any case; what had he to lose?

He arrived with his cab and was shown up to a room where a keen-eyed, energetic man greeted him. This was Holmes. The detective spoke of needing a hand with his luggage for a journey, a transparent ruse. Hope played along, a grim smile touching his own lips. He knelt to help with a buckle on a portmanteau, his senses on high alert, his muscles tensed. He was ready for a fight, for one last, desperate struggle. He never got the chance. As he bent to his task, there was a sudden, sharp click. He felt the cold steel snap around his wrist. The trap was sprung, not with violence, but with a piece of brilliant, simple trickery.

As he was wrenched to his feet and introduced to the astounded detectives, a strange mixture of fury and admiration filled him. He had been hunted, and now he was caught. His epic twenty-year quest for vengeance had ended here, in this quiet London sitting-room, at the hands of a man as clever and as relentless as himself. His purpose was fulfilled, his life’s work complete. Now, only the final account remained to be paid.

It was then that a strange thing occurred. Through the great and awful stillness, a new sensation made itself felt—a low, deep, and continuous vibration, which seemed to tremble through the very rock on which he sat. Was it some new trick of his failing senses, a phantom of his heat-addled brain? He opened his eyes, which he had thought to have closed for the last time upon the world, and stared out towards the horizon. At first he saw nothing but the familiar, dreary expanse. It was no natural dust-devil, for it advanced with a steady and inexorable purpose. Within the heart of the cloud, he began to make out darker shapes, a long, sinuous line of movement that coiled across the desert floor.

Hope, a sentiment he had believed to be utterly extinguished, flared up anew in his breast. He staggered to his feet, shielding his eyes with a trembling hand, his whole being concentrated upon that one distant point. The cloud grew larger, the sound more distinct. It was the lowing of cattle, the creaking of wagon-wheels, the distant shouts of men. It was life! A great tide of human life, flowing miraculously into this dead heart of the world. Ferrier fell to his knees and sobbed, the dry, racking sobs of a man whose tears had long since run dry. He pointed a shaking finger towards the approaching marvel, trying to rouse the sleeping child. “Lucy, my dear! Look! We are saved!” he croaked, his voice a mere rasp in his throat.

The caravan which was thus advancing upon them was one of a singular and memorable character. In the history of the world, there have been few such pilgrimages. An entire people, driven from their homes by persecution, were marching resolutely into the unknown, seeking a promised land where they might build their temple and practise their faith unmolested. They were the Latter-day Saints, the children of Joseph Smith, known to the world as the Mormons. Their long procession of wagons, laden with all their worldly goods, stretched for more than a mile across the plain. Beside the wagons walked the men, rifles on their shoulders, their faces grim and set with purpose. The women and children, whose pale faces peered from beneath the canvas hoods, bore the hardships of the journey with a quiet fortitude, sustained by the unwavering conviction of their faith. At the head of this great column, riding upon a fine bay horse, was a man of commanding presence, with a shrewd, powerful face and a great, flowing brown beard. This was the Prophet, the leader, the law-giver of his people: Brigham Young.

It was he who first caught sight of the solitary figure standing upon the rocky outcrop. He brought his horse to a halt, raising a hand that brought the entire vast procession to a standstill. The silence of the desert, momentarily broken, descended once more, but now it was a silence filled with a thousand watching eyes. The Prophet beckoned to a group of his lieutenants, men whose stern, bearded visages marked them as the elders of his church. After a brief consultation, four of them spurred their horses forward and galloped up the slope towards the two castaways.

John Ferrier watched them approach, his heart pounding with a mixture of hope and apprehension. The riders reined in their horses before him, their keen eyes taking in every detail of the scene: the gaunt, ragged man and the pale, unconscious child. One of them, a tall, severe-looking man, dismounted and knelt beside Lucy, placing a cool hand upon her forehead. He produced a water-flask from his belt and, moistening a corner of his kerchief, began to gently dab her lips and brow.

“They are at their last extremity,” he said, turning to his companions. “Another day would have seen the end of them.”

“Are they of the faithful?” asked another, his gaze hard and suspicious.

John Ferrier found his voice. “We are travellers from Illinois,” he said hoarsely. “We have lost our party. For God’s sake, give us water.”

The men exchanged glances. Then the first one rose to his feet. “Our Prophet will see you,” he said, not unkindly. “Follow us.”

Supported by two of the Saints, for his legs would scarce obey him, John Ferrier stumbled down the slope towards the waiting caravan. He carried the child in his arms, her small weight now an almost unbearable burden. He found himself standing before the great leader, whose piercing grey eyes seemed to look into his very soul.

“We have found these stragglers in the wilderness,” announced one of the scouts. “A man and a child. They are gentiles.”

Brigham Young looked down from his horse at the two pathetic figures. His expression was stern and impassive. “Who are you, and whence do you come?” he asked, his voice deep and resonant.

With what little strength remained to him, John Ferrier recounted his story. He told of the party of settlers with whom he had started, of the wrong turning they had taken, of the gradual dwindling of their supplies, and of how he and the little girl were now the sole survivors. He spoke simply and without embellishment, but the stark tragedy of his tale was plain enough to see in his haggard face and wasted frame.

When he had finished, there was a long silence. The Prophet stroked his great beard, his eyes fixed upon the child, who had begun to stir feebly in Ferrier’s arms.

“Your story is a hard one,” he said at last. “This is a cruel land for those who are not guided by the hand of Providence. You ask for our help. We are a people who have known suffering, and we do not turn away the afflicted. We will take you in and nurture you. But we demand something in return.”

“Anything,” whispered Ferrier. “My life is yours if you will but save the child.”

“Life is not for you to give, nor for me to take,” the Prophet responded. “We are journeying to a new Zion in the desert, a land promised to the Saints. There, we shall build a new society, founded upon the laws delivered to our martyred Prophet, Joseph. If we save you, it must be that you cast off your gentile ways and embrace the one true faith. John Ferrier looked from the implacable face of the Mormon leader to the pale, trusting face of the little girl in his arms. What were creeds and doctrines to him now? What was any faith compared to the sanctity of this one small life? His own beliefs had long since been eroded by the harsh realities of the frontier. Here was a chance for survival, a chance for Lucy to grow to womanhood. The choice was no choice at all.

“We accept,” he said, his voice firm. “We will be as you are, and worship your God.”

A murmur of approval ran through the assembled elders. Brigham Young gave a curt nod. “It is well,” he declared. “Let their names be enrolled in the book of the faithful. See that they are given food and water, and a place in the wagon of Elder Bence. From this day forward, you are of the flock. See that you do not stray, for in all this vast land there is no salvation outside the bosom of the church.”

And so it was that John Ferrier and Lucy Ferrier were rescued from the jaws of death and absorbed into the great Mormon exodus. A new chapter of their lives had begun, in circumstances more strange and wonderful than any they could have imagined. Ferrier, tended with a rough but effective kindness, soon regained his strength, though the memory of his ordeal would forever cast a shadow in his eyes. Little Lucy, under the care of the Mormon women, blossomed like a desert flower after a rare spring rain. The colour returned to her cheeks, and her laughter, a sound which her guardian had feared never to hear again, soon echoed in the wagon that was now their home.

The great caravan pressed onward. They crossed the mountains, forded the rivers, and traversed the endless plains, their faith a burning pillar of fire to guide them through the night of their wanderings. At last, after a journey of many months and more than a thousand miles, they reached their destination. From the mouth of a great cañon, they looked down upon a vast and beautiful valley, watered by a river that flowed into a great inland sea of salt. Here, in this virgin wilderness, sheltered by a ring of snow-capped mountains, their long journey was at an end.

“This is the place,” declared Brigham Young, and a great shout of joy and thanksgiving rose from the throats of the weary pilgrims.

In the years that followed, a miracle was wrought in that valley. The desert was made to bloom as a rose. Through hard work, ingenious irrigation, and a spirit of absolute communal co-operation, the Saints transformed the barren land into a verdant paradise. A city of wide streets and pleasant houses rose upon the plain, with a great and magnificent Temple at its heart. This was Salt Lake City, the new Jerusalem, the capital of a strange and formidable empire carved out of the wilderness.

Among the most industrious and respected of the settlers was John Ferrier. A shrewd and practical man, he put the lessons of the frontier to good use. The small plot of land allotted to him soon grew into a prosperous farm, and through wise trading and investment, he amassed a considerable fortune. He remained, however, an outlier in the community. Though he conformed outwardly to the Mormon faith, attending their services and paying his tithes, he never truly embraced their doctrines. Most notably, he resisted all pressure to take more than one wife, a practice central to the faith of the Saints. He remained a widower, his household consisting only of himself and his adopted daughter. His great wealth and his reputation as one of the original pioneers who had crossed the desert with the Prophet afforded him a degree of protection and independence that few other men enjoyed. Yet he was a man marked, a gentile spirit in a community that demanded absolute conformity, and the elders watched him with patient, calculating eyes.

As for Lucy, she grew and flourished with the new city. The pale, frail child rescued from the desert became a young woman of singular beauty and grace. Her golden hair and deep violet eyes, combined with a sweet and gentle nature, made her the acknowledged belle of the settlement. She was her father’s pride and joy, the sole object of his fierce, protective love. He educated her himself, sharing with her the books and ideas he had brought from the world beyond the mountains, and she grew up with a spirit and intelligence that set her apart from the other young women of Zion. It was inevitable that such a flower would not be allowed to bloom unnoticed for long. The young men of the community cast longing eyes upon her, and more than one of the powerful, grey-bearded elders considered how well she would grace their households as a junior wife.

They were the sons of the most influential patriarchs in the Council of the Elders: Joseph Stangerson, a youth with a heavy, brooding countenance and a reputation for sullen piety; and Enoch Drebber, a coarse and brutish young man whose father’s great wealth allowed him a license which he frequently abused. Both were considered excellent matches by the standards of the community, and both began to press their suits with a grim and unwelcome persistence, viewing Lucy less as a woman to be won than as a prize to be claimed. John Ferrier, seeing the predatory gleam in their eyes, felt a cold knot of fear tighten in his stomach. He deflected their advances with all the skill and diplomacy he could muster, but he knew he was only postponing the inevitable confrontation. The day was coming when the church would make its claim upon his daughter, and he did not know how he could defy it.

He was not one of the Saints, but a ‘gentile,’ a prospector and hunter from the mountains of Nevada. His name was Jefferson Hope. He had called at the farmhouse to ask for assistance, and Ferrier, ever hospitable to a fellow outsider, had invited him in.

He spoke of a world of freedom and individual striving, a world beyond the narrow confines of the valley, and his words found a deep resonance in the hearts of his two listeners. When his eyes met Lucy’s across the lamplit table, there was an instant spark of recognition, the silent acknowledgement of two kindred spirits. For his part, Hope was utterly captivated. He had seen many women in his travels, but none with the unique combination of delicate beauty and spirited intelligence that he found in Lucy Ferrier. He fell in love that night, completely and irrevocably.

He found excuses to prolong his stay in the valley, and under the benevolent and watchful eye of John Ferrier, the young couple’s attachment quickly deepened. They would ride out together into the foothills, or walk by the banks of the stream in the quiet twilight hours. He would take her away from this stifling community, back to the free world, where they could build a life on their own terms.

He had been strong once, a man whose sinews were hardened by a life of toil and adventure in the open air; but the desert, in its patient, inexorable way, had sapped that strength, leaving behind this gaunt caricature of a man, stumbling onward through a shimmering haze. He did not walk so much as lurch, a puppet whose strings were being cut one by one.

He paused, leaning heavily upon his rifle, and scanned the horizon. It was a fool’s errand, he knew, for the horizon had offered nothing but false promises for many a league. Yet some stubborn ember of hope, which not even this furnace of a land could entirely extinguish, compelled him to look. At first, he saw only the familiar monotony: the grey dust, the stunted, thorny bushes, and the distant, jagged line of mountains that seemed to recede with every step he took towards them. And then, he saw it.

It was a dark speck, a mere smudge against the alkali plain, far to the south-west. He stared, his eyes burning with the effort, convinced that it was but another trick of the heat, a phantom born of his delirium. He had seen such things before: great lakes that vanished upon approach, and cool, shady groves that dissolved into the quivering air. He closed his eyes, pressing the heels of his hands against them until colours exploded behind his lids, and then looked again. The speck was still there. It had not moved, nor had it wavered. It was solid. It was real.

A surge of energy, the last desperate rally of a dying organism, flooded through him. He shifted the rifle to a more comfortable position across his shoulder and set his face towards the distant object. His gait was no longer a mere shamble; there was purpose in it now, a grim and terrible resolution. He did not know what the speck was, whether it promised salvation or merely a new form of despair, but it was something other than the endless, soul-crushing emptiness. That was enough. For an hour he forged ahead, his eyes fixed upon his goal, and slowly, agonizingly, the speck began to take on a more definite form. It was a wagon, or what was left of one, with a great, dark shape lying on the ground beside it.

As he drew nearer still, the details of the tragedy became brutally clear. The dark shape was a dead ox, its legs stiffly pointing towards the pitiless sky, its body already swollen in the heat. The covered wagon was tilted at a perilous angle, one of its wheels having shattered completely. It was a scene of utter ruin, a monument to a failed journey, and for a moment the man’s heart sank. He had wasted his final strength only to find a scene of death that mirrored his own impending fate. He was about to turn away, to seek a more private place in which to meet his end, when he perceived a slight movement from the shadow cast by the broken vehicle.

He approached with caution, his rifle held ready, for the wilderness was a place of many dangers, and desperation could make any creature unpredictable. Peering under the canvas flap, he saw two figures. One was a man, stretched prostrate upon the ground, his face turned away, his form still and lifeless. The other was a small child, a little girl, who was crouched beside the man, her head resting upon his chest. She could not have been more than five or six years of age, and her small frame was wracked by pathetic, silent sobs. She was clad in dusty, travel-worn clothes, and her face was smudged with dirt and tears, but even in this state of misery, there was a delicacy about her features and a wealth of fine, golden hair that fell in tangled masses about her shoulders.

At the sight of the traveller, she looked up, her blue eyes wide with a mixture of terror and a faint, childish curiosity. She did not scream, for her throat was too dry for any sound. The traveller lowered his rifle, his own hardship forgotten in the face of this more poignant sorrow.

“Water,” he rasped, his voice a dry crackle. It was not a question, but a statement of their shared and terrible need.

The little girl shook her head slowly, and a single tear traced a clean path through the grime on her cheek. She gestured with a small hand towards an empty cask lying on its side near the wagon. The traveller nodded in understanding. He unslung his own canteen, and shaking it, was rewarded with the faintest sound of a slosh. There was but a mouthful or two left, a few precious drops he had been hoarding with a miser’s desperation. Without a second thought, he knelt down and uncorked it, holding it to the child’s lips.

She drank greedily, her small hands clutching at his wrist, and the life seemed to flow back into her. The glaze of despair in her eyes was replaced by a look of profound gratitude. When she had finished, she looked at the still form of the man beside her and then back at the traveller, her expression a silent, desperate plea.

The traveller leaned over the prostrate man. He was a powerfully built individual, in the prime of his life, but his face was grey and gaunt, his lips black and swollen. The traveller placed a hand upon his chest and felt the faintest flutter of a heartbeat. He was alive, but only just. Putting the canteen to the man’s lips, he allowed the last few drops to trickle into his mouth. The man coughed, a dry, hacking sound, and his eyelids flickered open. He stared up at the stranger leaning over him, his gaze vacant and uncomprehending.

“She is safe,” the traveller said, his voice softer now. “The girl is safe.”

A glimmer of understanding seemed to dawn in the man’s eyes. He tried to speak, but only a faint moan escaped him. He made a weak gesture towards a small pouch at his belt. The traveller, divining his meaning, opened it. Inside was a small piece of dried venison, hard as a rock. It was their last provision. The traveller broke off a tiny piece and, after softening it with his own saliva, placed it in the child’s mouth. He did the same for her father. It was a meagre offering, but in that desolate waste, it was the very stuff of life.

For the rest of that day, he tended to them, sheltering them as best he could from the sun in the scant shade of the wagon. As evening drew on, bringing with it a merciful coolness, the man had recovered enough to speak. His name, he said, was John Ferrier, and the girl was his adopted daughter, Lucy. They were, he explained in a weak but steady voice, part of a great wagon train of pioneers, dissenters who had broken away from the main body after a dispute over doctrine. They had set out on their own, hoping to find a fertile valley in which to settle, but misfortune had dogged their every step. Hostile Indians had harried them, sickness had thinned their numbers, and finally, the desert itself had claimed them, one by one, until only he and the child remained. This final disaster, the breaking of the wheel and the death of their last ox, had been the finishing stroke. They had been preparing to die when he, their saviour, had appeared as if by a direct interposition of Providence.

The traveller, in turn, introduced himself as Jefferson Hope. He was a hunter and a prospector, he told them, a man who had spent his life in the wild places of the continent, far from cities and settlements. He had been making his way from the mountains of Nevada towards the new territories in the east when his own supplies had run low. As they spoke, a bond of mutual respect formed between the two men: the grim, God-fearing farmer and the self-reliant, worldly adventurer. They were men of different moulds, yet they were united by a shared spirit of independence and a deep, unspoken understanding of the hardships of life on the frontier.

That night, as the stars blazed with an icy brilliance in the black sky, Hope took stock of their situation. It was desperate, but perhaps not entirely hopeless. He was a skilled tracker, and his knowledge of the desert was profound. He knew that even in this arid land, there were hidden springs and secret patches of grazing, known only to the Indians and to the few white men who, like himself, had made the wilderness their home. He remembered hearing talk of a series of springs, a good two days’ journey to the north, at the foot of a long, flat-topped mountain. To reach it on foot, with a weakened man and a small child, would be a herculean task, but it was their only chance.

“We cannot stay here,” he announced, his decision made. “At dawn, we walk.”

John Ferrier, his strength partially restored, nodded his assent. He looked at his daughter, who had fallen into an exhausted sleep, her small hand clutching his. “Whatever you say, Mr. Hope. My life and hers are in your hands.”

They salvaged what they could from the wagon: a blanket, the empty water cask, and what little food remained. At the first sign of dawn, they set out, leaving the broken wagon and the dead ox behind them as a lonely monument to their ordeal. The journey that followed was an epic of human endurance. Hope led the way, his long, easy stride eating up the miles, his eyes constantly scanning the ground for tracks, for signs of water, for any indication that they were on the right path. Behind him trudged John Ferrier, carrying Lucy in his arms. The child was too small and weak to walk for long, and her weight was a terrible burden for the still-recovering man, but he never complained. His jaw was set like granite, and his eyes were fixed on the back of the man who was his only hope.

For two days they toiled under the blazing sun, their world a blur of heat and dust and thirst. At night, they huddled together for warmth against the biting desert cold, listening to the yapping of the coyotes on the plain. Hope shared his scant knowledge and his boundless resolve, pointing out how to find sustenance in the roots of certain plants, and how to read the stars to keep their course. He spoke to Lucy in a gentle voice, distracting her from her misery with tales of the grizzly bears and the mountain lions he had hunted, and the girl, who had at first been terrified of this gaunt and wild-looking man, came to look upon him as her guardian and protector.

On the evening of the second day, when it seemed they could go no further, Hope stopped and pointed. “There,” he said. “Look.”

Far in the distance, at the base of the long mesa that had been their landmark, they could see a faint, dark green line. It was the unmistakable sign of willows and cottonwoods, trees that grew only where there was water. The sight gave them renewed strength. They pushed on, and an hour later, they were stumbling through a grove of trees towards a clear, cold spring that bubbled up from the earth. They threw themselves down and drank, letting the life-giving liquid wash the dust from their throats and the despair from their hearts. They had made it. They were saved.

They rested there for several days, regaining their strength in that small oasis. John Ferrier, a man of deep if unconventional faith, gave thanks to God for their deliverance, but he knew well that the instrument of that deliverance had been Jefferson Hope. A profound debt had been incurred, a debt which the farmer swore he would one day repay.

It was on the morning of their fourth day at the spring that they were discovered. Hope, who was out scouting for game, was the first to see them. It was a sight so incredible that for a moment he thought his mind was playing tricks on him again. A vast procession was moving slowly across the plain, a column of people and wagons and livestock so long that it stretched for miles. It was no mere party of adventurers; it was a nation on the move, an army of pilgrims. They were orderly and organized, with armed outriders on their flanks and a look of solemn purpose upon their faces. As they drew nearer, he saw that they were a people of all ages, from grizzled old men to babes in arms. They were the main body of the pioneers from which John Ferrier had broken away, the people who called themselves the Latter-day Saints.

Hope returned quickly to the spring. “We have company,” he said grimly to Ferrier. “A great many of them.”

Ferrier’s face grew pale. He had left these people on a matter of principle, and he knew that their leader, a man of iron will and unshakable conviction, did not look kindly upon dissenters. Before they had time to decide upon a course of action, a party of horsemen broke away from the main column and galloped towards their oasis. They were hard, sunburnt men, with rifles held across their saddles, and an air of absolute authority.

Their leader, a tall man with a stern, bearded face, reined in his horse and looked down at the three of them. His eyes passed over Hope and came to rest on John Ferrier.

“You are John Ferrier,” he stated. It was not a question.

“I am,” Ferrier replied, getting to his feet and placing himself in front of Lucy.

“You left the flock of the faithful to wander in the wilderness,” the man continued, his voice cold and accusing. “Providence has seen fit to punish your arrogance, yet it has also seen fit to spare you. The Prophet has sent us to bring you back into the fold.”

At that moment, a carriage, drawn by four fine horses, was driven up, and a man dismounted. He was of middle-age, with a large, square head, a florid complexion, and shrewd, intelligent eyes. His expression was one of benevolence and command. All the men around him, even the stern-faced captain of the guard, treated him with a profound and unmistakable reverence. This was clearly a man of immense power.

He walked forward and looked at John Ferrier, his gaze lingering on the sturdy farmer and the beautiful, golden-haired child. “I am Brigham Young,” he said, his voice surprisingly mild. “I am the leader of God’s chosen people, on our journey to the Promised Land which He has prepared for us. We have found it, in a great valley beyond those mountains. There we shall build our new Zion. You were lost, my son, but now you are found. There is a place for you and for the child among us.”

John Ferrier was in a terrible dilemma. His natural independence rebelled at the thought of rejoining a community whose strictures he had fled. But he was no longer a free agent. He had Lucy to consider. To refuse this offer was to choose death for them both, for they could not survive alone. To accept was to deliver himself and his beloved child into the hands of this powerful, magnetic man, to become cogs in the great machine he was building.

He looked at Jefferson Hope, a silent question in his eyes. Hope’s face was a mask. As an outsider, a Gentile, he was viewed with suspicion by these people. He had no place among them, nor did he desire one. His path lay elsewhere.

With a heavy heart, John Ferrier made his choice. “We will come,” he said to the Prophet.

Brigham Young smiled, a benign and fatherly expression. “It is the will of the Lord,” he said. He then turned his shrewd eyes upon Jefferson Hope. “And you, stranger? You who are not of the faith? What is your business with these, my sheep?”

“I found them dying in the desert,” Hope said simply. “I brought them here.”

“Then you have done the Lord’s work,” Young declared. “We are in your debt. You may travel with us until you wish to take your own road. You will be given food and protection.”

And so it was that Jefferson Hope, the lone wanderer, found himself travelling in the midst of a disciplined and zealous host. He and John Ferrier spoke little of what had passed, but a deep understanding remained between them. A few days later, the great caravan passed through a canyon and emerged into a vast and beautiful valley, with a great salt lake shimmering in the distance. Here, the Prophet declared, they would build their city.

The time soon came for Hope to leave. His life was in the mountains and the silver-streams, not in the building of a new society. He sought out John Ferrier to say his farewell.

“I owe you a debt I can never repay,” the farmer said, grasping his hand. “You have given me back my life, and what is more, you have given me back my Lucy.”

Hope looked over at the little girl, who was playing nearby, now clean and well-fed, a picture of childish innocence. A strange protective tenderness filled his heart. “Look after her, Ferrier,” he said, his voice thick with an emotion that surprised him.

“I will,” Ferrier promised. “With my life.”

“I’ll be back this way in a few years,” Hope said. “Prospecting. I’ll look you up.”

“Our door will always be open to you, Jefferson Hope. You will always find a welcome in the house of John Ferrier.”

With a final handshake, and a last, long look at the little girl with the golden hair, Jefferson Hope turned his back on the new-born city of the Saints and walked away, heading back towards the familiar solitude of the mountains. He was a man accustomed to being alone, yet as he went, he felt a new and unfamiliar pang of loneliness, a sense that he was leaving something of great value behind him. He did not know then that their paths were fated to cross again, nor could he possibly imagine the dark and terrible tragedy in which that future meeting would culminate. He only knew that he had saved a life, and in doing so, had somehow irrevocably altered his own.

The years which followed were a chronicle of toil and triumph for the refugee Saints. From the collection of rude huts and makeshift shelters which had first sprung up on the shores of the great salt lake, there arose, as if by enchantment, a flourishing and populous city. Streets were laid out in broad, regular avenues; houses of adobe and of timber were erected; and in the very centre of the settlement, the foundations were laid for a temple of such size and magnificence as to dwarf all other structures. The barren valley, which had known only the coyote and the chaparral bush, was made to blossom like a rose. By a miracle of engineering and of tireless industry, water was channelled from the mountain streams, and soon the grey alkali flats were transformed into verdant fields of corn and wheat. The handful of starving wanderers had become a mighty and prosperous nation, isolated by deserts and mountains from the outer world, and ruled with a rod of iron by the Prophet and his Council of Elders.

In this general prosperity, John Ferrier had his full share. A shrewd and hardworking man by nature, he had put his shoulder to the wheel with the best of them, and his industry had been rewarded. His farm, which lay on the outskirts of the new city, was one of the most fertile and best-managed in the whole valley. His herds of cattle and flocks of sheep were without equal, and it was common knowledge that, next to the great Stangerson himself, who was head of the Council of Four, John Ferrier was the wealthiest man in the community. His wealth, however, had not altered his simple nature, nor had it brought him into the inner circle of the Church’s hierarchy. He remained an anomaly among the Saints: a man of great possessions who had steadfastly refused to take unto himself more than one wife. In a society where a man’s piety and standing were often judged by the number of his spouses, Ferrier’s monogamy was a source of constant remark, and, in some quarters, of suspicion. He was a good Mormon in all outward respects, but he had never embraced the doctrine which lay at the very heart of their new revelation. The truth was that he had but one treasure in the world, and he guarded it with a jealousy which was at once fierce and tender. That treasure was his adopted daughter, Lucy.

Under his loving care, the pale and fragile child who had been rescued from the brink of death had blossomed into a maiden of surpassing beauty. The dry, wholesome air of the mountains had brought a rich colour to her cheeks and a brightness to her eyes. She was tall and graceful, with the same wealth of golden hair which Jefferson Hope had remembered, and a spirit as free and untamed as the wild-flowers which grew in the high mountain meadows where she loved to wander. Her bright, fearless eyes and ready smile were a strange contrast to the demure and subdued manner of the other maidens of the Elect, who had been taught from birth that a woman’s highest calling was meek submission. Lucy had been raised in a household where she was not a servant, but a queen, and her father’s adoration had given her a confidence and an independence of thought which were rare in that community. As she grew into womanhood, her beauty became a common topic of conversation. The young men of the city would watch her with admiration as she rode her spirited pony through the streets, and many an elder cast a thoughtful and appraising eye upon the daughter of the rich John Ferrier. In Utah, every girl of marriageable age was a prize to be claimed for the good of the Church, and it was clear to all that such a prize as Lucy Ferrier would not be allowed to remain unwed for long.

It was in her eighteenth year that the man who had once been her saviour returned into her life. Twelve years had passed since Jefferson Hope had turned his back upon the new-born city of the Saints. Those years he had spent in the manner of his kind, as a wanderer and a prospector. He had hunted in the Rockies, trapped in the Sierras, and sought for silver in the mountains of Nevada. His face was leaner and more weathered than before, and his dark hair was touched with grey at the temples, but his eyes were as keen and his step as firm as ever. He was a man who had lived his life in the great solitudes of nature, and he had the self-reliant and watchful air of one who is accustomed to depending upon no one but himself. He had made and lost a dozen small fortunes in his search for the precious metals, but a restless spirit still drove him on. Business had brought him at last to the territory of Utah, and, true to the promise he had made so long ago, he rode down from the canyons into the great valley to seek out the man whose life he had saved.

He had expected to find a humble farmstead; he found instead a handsome and spacious house, surrounded by well-tended orchards and fields which stretched as far as the eye could see. A look of astonishment came over his face as he dismounted and made his way to the door. It was opened by John Ferrier himself, who had grown stout and grey in his prosperity. For a moment the farmer stared at the tall, bearded stranger, and then a look of incredulous recognition dawned in his eyes.
“Jefferson Hope!” he cried, his voice breaking with emotion. “By all that is holy, it is you!”
In an instant, the two men were grasping hands, their faces alight with a joy that needed no words. Ferrier drew him into the house, plying him with a hundred questions at once. “I knew you would come!” he repeated again and again. “I told Lucy you would not forget us. We owe you everything, my friend, everything!”

At the mention of her name, a young woman who had been seated in the parlour rose and came towards them. Hope, who had retained in his mind the image of a small, golden-haired child, stopped short and stared. Before him stood a woman, whose beauty was so striking that it took his breath away. It was Lucy, but a Lucy transformed. She looked at him with her clear, honest eyes, a faint blush rising to her cheeks as she recognised the half-legendary figure from her childhood.
“You remember me, then?” he asked, his voice softer than he had intended.
“I have never forgotten you,” she said simply. “My father has spoken of you every day of my life. You are the hero of our household.”
From that day forward, Jefferson Hope became a constant visitor at the Ferrier farm. He had intended to stay but a few days before returning to his prospecting in the mountains, but the days turned into weeks, and still he lingered. The peaceful domesticity of the household was a balm to his lonely and adventurous soul. He would spend the long evenings with Ferrier, smoking a pipe upon the veranda and listening to the farmer’s tales of the community’s early struggles and present prosperity. But it was not the company of the father that held him there. It was the daughter.

A deep and powerful attachment had sprung up between the rough, solitary wanderer and the beautiful, spirited girl. He was like no man she had ever known. The young men of the city, with their pious talk and their narrow ambitions, seemed like boys beside this quiet, self-possessed man who had faced death in a hundred forms and looked upon the world with clear, undeceived eyes. He spoke to her not of the Prophet and the Saints, but of a wider world beyond the mountains, a world of great cities and strange peoples, a world where a man, and a woman too, could be the master of their own destiny. As she listened, a longing for that world was born in her own heart, and she came to see the valley of the Saints not as a paradise, as she had been taught, but as a beautiful and prosperous prison.

For his part, Hope found in her the one thing he had unknowingly been searching for all his life: a companion for his soul. Her innocence and her spirit charmed him, and her quiet courage called forth all the protective tenderness in his nature. He had lived a hard and lonely life, and the thought of this pure, lovely creature had been a bright spot in his memory for twelve long years. Now, finding her grown into a woman who surpassed all his dreams, he fell in love with a depth and a passion which shook him to the very core of his being.

They said little of their feelings, but in the long, quiet looks that passed between them, everything was understood. In the cool of the evenings, when the long shadows of the Wasatch range crept across the valley, they would sometimes walk together in the secluded groves that fringed the farm. It was there, under the silent watch of the great mountains, that he finally spoke.
“Lucy,” he said, his voice husky with an emotion he could not conceal, “I am a poor man, and a wanderer. I have nothing to offer you but a life of hardship, perhaps, and a home in some rough mining camp. But my heart is yours, and has been since the day I first saw you. If you would consent to be my wife, I would protect you and cherish you all the days of my life.”
She looked up at him, her eyes shining with tears. “I have no wish for any other life than one with you,” she whispered. “But my father—and the Church—they would never permit it. I cannot marry a Gentile.”
“Then we will leave this place,” he said, his voice firm and resolute. “I have some money saved. I will take you and your father away from here, to Nevada, or to California. We will make a new life, a free life, together.”
A look of hope, mingled with fear, came into her face. “Could we truly do it?” she breathed. “Could we escape?”
“We will do it,” he answered, taking her hands in his. “I will make my arrangements. I know these mountains better than any man in Utah. Trust me, Lucy. I will not fail you.”

And so their troth was plighted, and they began to make their secret plans. It was agreed that Hope would return to Nevada, realise his assets, and come back with horses and supplies for their journey. The escape was to be made under the cover of darkness, for they both knew that if their intentions were discovered, the vengeance of the Church would be swift and terrible.

Their secret, however, was not as well-kept as they had imagined. The frequent visits of the Gentile prospector to the Ferrier farm had not gone unnoticed. In a community where every man was his brother’s keeper, and where the doings of every household were a matter of public concern, such a thing could not be concealed. Whispers began to circulate, and from whispers grew suspicion. The Elders of the Church, who had long looked upon John Ferrier’s independence with disfavour, now saw it turning to outright defiance. It was one thing for him to refuse to add to his household, but it was another, and far more serious, thing for him to contemplate giving his only daughter, and a child of the covenant, to an unbeliever. The matter was discussed in the highest councils of the Saints, and it was decided that the time had come for action.

One evening, a short time after Jefferson Hope had departed for Nevada, a carriage drew up before John Ferrier’s house, and from it stepped the Prophet himself, Brigham Young. He was accompanied by the four principal Elders of the Church, among them the grim-faced Stangerson. Ferrier, who saw them coming from his window, felt a cold premonition of disaster, but he met them at the door with a calm and dignified expression.
“The Prophet does my humble house a great honour,” he said, bowing.
“It is about the affairs of your house that we have come to speak, brother Ferrier,” said Brigham Young, his voice smooth and paternal, but with an underlying note of steel. He and his companions entered the parlour and seated themselves in a solemn row. Lucy, who had been there when they arrived, made to leave, but the Prophet stopped her with a gesture. “Stay, my child. This concerns you most of all.”
He fixed his shrewd, penetrating eyes upon John Ferrier. “Brother Ferrier,” he began, “you have been a prosperous man in the Lord’s vineyard. The light of providence has shone upon you. You have been blessed with wealth, and with a daughter who is as a flower in the desert. And yet, you have not shown yourself to be truly grateful. You have kept your farm, and your daughter, to yourself. This is not the way of the Saints. Our goods, our lives, and our children all belong to the Lord, and to his Church.”
He paused, and the silence in the room was heavy and oppressive.
“It has come to our ears,” he continued, “that you have allowed an unbeliever, a Gentile, to frequent your home. It is even said that you would consider giving this child of God to be his wife. Is this true?”
John Ferrier’s face was a study in controlled fury. The blood had drained from it, leaving it grey and hard as the mountain stone. “It is true that Mr. Hope is my friend, and the saviour of my life,” he said, his voice low and steady. “It is also true that my daughter loves him.”
“Love!” The word was spoken with contempt by Elder Stangerson. “What is the fleeting fancy of a girl compared to the will of God and the building of his Kingdom? She must be sealed to a man of the faith.”
Brigham Young raised a hand to silence him. “The ewe-lamb must be brought into the fold, brother Ferrier,” he said, his tone becoming harder. “It is not meet that she should stray and be lost to the abominations of the Gentiles. The maid is of a marriageable age. Two of the sons of our most blessed Elders have looked upon her with favour, and are worthy of her. Joseph Stangerson’s son, Enoch Drebber, and Elder Stangerson’s own son, young Joseph.”
At the mention of these names, a look of horror and revulsion passed over Lucy’s face. Drebber was a coarse, brutish man in his middle years, already possessed of a dozen wives, and known for his dissolute habits and his cruelty. Young Stangerson was a pale, fanatical youth, whose cold eyes held a reptilian cunning. The thought of being sealed to either of them was more dreadful to her than death itself.
“I give you a choice,” the Prophet concluded, rising to his feet. “You have one month from this day to see her sealed to one of these two worthy men. If, at the end of that time, she is still unwed, the Church will take the matter into its own hands. Think well on it, brother Ferrier. The Lord has spoken.”
Without another word, he turned and swept from the room, followed by his four grim-faced acolytes. The door closed behind them, and John Ferrier and his daughter were left alone, staring at one another in an agony of fear and despair. The ultimatum had been delivered. Their brief dream of happiness was over, and the hand of the Church, from which there was no appeal, had descended upon them. They were trapped.

For a long time, neither spoke. The ticking of the tall clock in the corner seemed to be counting down the seconds of their lives. John Ferrier sat with his head in his hands, his broad shoulders, which had borne so many hardships, now bowed under the weight of this new and terrible burden. Lucy knelt beside him, her arm around his neck, her pale face pressed against his rough jacket. No tears came to her, for the shock was too deep and the dread too immediate for such a simple release.

“Father,” she whispered at last, her voice trembling, “what is to become of us?”

John Ferrier raised his head, and in his eyes was a look that his daughter had never seen there before. It was the look of a hunted animal, brought to bay at last, yet determined to sell its life dearly. “They shall not have you, my girl,” he said, his voice a low growl. “I came into this desert with nothing but my life, and I owe it to you and to the man who saved us. I will not repay that debt by surrendering you to a life of misery and shame. I would see you lying in the cold ground before I would see you the wife of Enoch Drebber or that snake Stangerson.”

“But what can we do?” cried Lucy, her composure breaking at last. “Where can we go? They are everywhere. Their power is absolute. To defy the Prophet is to defy God himself in this land.”

“There is still one man who does not bow to the Prophet,” said her father, his face hardening into lines of grim resolve. “There is still Jefferson Hope. We must get word to him.”

This, however, was a matter of no small difficulty. From the moment the Prophet and his Elders had left the farmhouse, John Ferrier and his daughter were marked individuals. They were under a ban. When Ferrier went into the city to sell his produce, men who had been his friends for years now turned their backs on him, or crossed the street to avoid his eye. Whispers followed him down the dusty avenues; the children of the Saints would sometimes run from his approach as though he were a leper. The farm, which had been a sanctuary of peace and prosperity, now felt like a prison. From the hills which overlooked their valley, they would often see the solitary figure of a horseman, sitting motionless for hours, a silent sentinel whose purpose was all too clear. They were being watched.

To communicate with Jefferson Hope under such circumstances was to risk everything. He was a Gentile, and the very man whom the Elders had forbidden from their home. Any meeting would be seen as an act of defiance, and would likely bring down immediate retribution. For three days, Ferrier racked his brain for a solution, his anxiety growing with every passing hour. He saw the colour fading from his daughter’s cheeks and the look of constant fear that now lived in her eyes.

On the fourth night, a plan came to him. He knew that Hope, in his work as a silver prospector and hunter, often used a certain remote trail in the foothills, a place far from the usual patrols of the Danites, or Avenging Angels, as the secret police of the Church were sometimes known. He instructed Lucy to burn a light in her window until midnight, a prearranged signal between them that he had never had cause to use before, and then to extinguish it. If Hope was in the region and saw it, he would know that there was trouble.

Leaving his daughter to her vigil, Ferrier saddled his swiftest horse and rode out, not towards the city, but deep into the wilderness that bordered his lands. He rode under the cold light of the stars, a stern, silent man, his rifle across his saddlebow, his mind set on his desperate purpose. He tethered his horse in a rocky defile and proceeded on foot to a high ridge overlooking the trail. There, wrapped in a blanket against the chill of the night air, he waited.

For hours he lay there, as silent and still as the boulders around him. The moon rose, bathing the desolate landscape in a ghostly, silver light. The howl of a distant coyote was the only sound that broke the profound silence. Just as the first fingers of dawn were beginning to probe the eastern sky, he heard it: the faint, rhythmic clink of a shod horse on stone. He peered over the edge of the ridge, and his heart gave a leap. A single rider was making his way up the winding path below. Even in the half-light, Ferrier recognised the tall, athletic figure and the easy, confident way he sat his horse. It was Jefferson Hope.

Ferrier let out a low, piercing whistle, a signal they had used on many a hunting trip. The rider pulled up instantly, his head turning, his hand dropping to the butt of the revolver at his belt. Ferrier whistled again, and this time he stepped out from behind the rocks into plain view.

A look of surprise and concern crossed Hope’s face. He spurred his horse up the steep incline, dismounting as he reached the summit. “John!” he exclaimed, grasping the older man’s hand. “What is it? I saw the light in Lucy’s window and feared the worst. I have been riding since midnight.”

In a few short, grim sentences, Ferrier recounted the visit of the Prophet and the terrible ultimatum that had been delivered. As he spoke, Jefferson Hope’s pleasant, sun-browned face grew hard and dangerous. The friendly light in his grey eyes was replaced by a cold fire, and his jaw was set like a trap of steel. When Ferrier had finished, he did not speak for a full minute, but stared out at the vast, empty expanse of the desert stretching away to the horizon.

“The thirtieth day from their visit, you say?” he asked at last, his voice quiet and even.

“That is the limit they have set,” Ferrier confirmed. “Twenty-six days from now.”

“They will not have her,” said Hope, with a finality that was more potent than any oath. “This is not a land of laws, John, as you well know. It is a land of power. Their power is in their numbers and their organisation. Our power must be in our wits and our knowledge of the wilderness. We must fly.”

“Fly? But where? We should be hunted down before we had gone fifty miles. The desert is theirs.”

“The desert is mine,” Hope corrected him, a faint, confident smile touching his lips. “I have crossed it when no other man would dare. I know the water-holes that they do not, and the paths through the cañons that are not on their maps. It will be a hard journey, a terrible journey, but it is our only chance. We will make for the Carson range, and from there into Nevada. Once out of the Territory of Utah, we are free men.” He laid a reassuring hand on Ferrier’s shoulder. “Go home, John. Tell Lucy to be of good heart. Let your enemies think you are beaten. Let them think you are considering their offer. We must buy ourselves time. I will need to gather supplies, to cache food and water along the route. Do nothing, say nothing, until you hear from me. On the night before the last day, I will come for you. Be ready.”

With that, he wrung Ferrier’s hand, swung himself back into the saddle, and was gone, his figure melting away into the grey gloom of the morning. John Ferrier watched him go, and for the first time in many days, a flicker of hope warmed his weary heart. He had an ally, and such an ally as a man might pray for in his darkest hour.

The days that followed were a trial of the spirit. True to Hope’s advice, Ferrier adopted an air of resignation. When neighbours spoke to him, he answered with a subdued and broken spirit, allowing them to believe that his will was crushed. This change in demeanour did not go unnoticed. One afternoon, a wagon drew up to the farmhouse, and from it descended the two men who were the cause of all their misery: Enoch Drebber and the younger Joseph Stangerson. They had come, they announced, to press their suits in person, that the fair Lucy might make her choice.

They were a study in contrasts, and yet equally repulsive. Drebber was a man of forty, with a coarse, florid face, small, piggish eyes, and a corpulent frame that spoke of a life of indulgence. He laughed too loudly and stood too close, his manner a repellent mixture of bullying familiarity and oily charm. Stangerson, on the other hand, was younger, not yet thirty, and was tall and gaunt, with a pale, sallow complexion and the burning, fanatical eyes of his father. He spoke little, but his gaze followed Lucy’s every movement with a cold, possessive intensity that was more terrifying than Drebber’s open lust.

They sat in the Ferriers’ parlour, an unwelcome and polluting presence. John Ferrier stood by the hearth, his face a mask of stone, while Lucy, pale and trembling, was forced to serve them refreshments.

“Well, lass,” boomed Drebber, draining his glass of buttermilk and wiping his lips with the back of his hand. “Have you made up your mind which of us is to be the lucky man? Don’t you worry, my girl. I have a dozen wives already, but there’s always room for a pretty one like you. You’ll be the jewel of my household, you will.”

Young Stangerson shot him a look of contempt. “It is not for carnal pleasure that we seek this union,” he said, his voice thin and reedy. “It is for the glory of the Church and the sealing of a soul for all eternity. The maiden should consider the honour that is being done to her. My father is an Elder. His household is one of piety and order. She would learn the true path there.”

Lucy looked from one to the other, from the brutish leer of Drebber to the cold, reptilian stare of Stangerson, and a wave of nausea swept over her. She felt she would faint.

“My daughter needs time,” said John Ferrier, his voice dangerously low. “The Prophet granted us a month.”

“Aye, and the month is passing,” said Drebber with a chuckle. “The days are slipping by. We thought we would just remind you. The thirtieth day is a Wednesday. We will expect your answer then. And if it is not the right one,” he added, his voice hardening, “the Council of Four will give the answer for you. Come, Joseph. The girl has seen us. Let her chew on the choice.”

They departed as they had come, leaving behind them a sense of violation and an atmosphere thick with menace. That night, when Ferrier went to bolt his door, he found a small mark freshly painted upon it. It was a simple square with the number ‘20’ inside. Twenty days were left. The psychological warfare had begun in earnest.

Each morning, they would find a new number painted on the door, counting down their dwindling days of freedom. ‘19’. ‘18’. ‘17’. The numbers were a constant, silent threat, a daily reminder of the net that was closing around them. The solitary horsemen on the hills were a more frequent sight. Sometimes, in the dead of night, they would hear the sound of horses circling the farm, the riders never showing themselves, but letting their presence be known. The strain was immense. Lucy grew thin and wraithlike, starting at every sound, while her father’s face became a haggard landscape of sleeplessness and anxiety. Their only comfort was the secret knowledge of Jefferson Hope’s promise.

With but ten days remaining, Hope made his second appearance. He came as silently as a spirit in the dead of night, tapping a secret rhythm on the kitchen window. Ferrier let him in, and the three of them gathered in the darkened room, speaking in hushed whispers. Hope was calm and methodical, a beacon of strength in their sea of fear.

“All is in readiness,” he said. “I have a wagon and two good mules hidden in the red cañon, five miles to the east. I have cached food and water at three points along our route. The first is a day’s journey, the second is three, the third is a week. It will be enough to get us to Nevada. The Avenging Angels will search, but they will search the main trails to the north and the south. They will not believe any man would be mad enough to strike directly east, across the salt desert. But I have done it before.”

He turned to Lucy, his expression softening. “It will be hard, Lucy. Harder than anything you have ever known. But I will be with you. I will not leave your side.”

She looked up at him, her eyes shining with tears, but also with a trust so absolute that it seemed to give her strength. “I am not afraid, Jefferson,” she said softly, “so long as I am with you and with my father.”

“Good girl,” he said, gently touching her hand. “Now, listen closely. We have nine more days of this torment to endure. On the ninth night, the night before your time is up, you must be ready. Have what little you can carry, but no more than a small bundle each. Food, warm clothing. At two hours past midnight, I will be at the edge of the cottonwood grove behind the farm. Do not show a light. Do not make a sound. Slip out of the house and come to me. Is that understood?”

They both nodded, their hearts pounding.

“They will be watching you closely as the day approaches,” Hope went on, his face grim. “They will expect a move. You must do nothing to arouse their suspicion. Go about your day as usual. In the evening, bolt your doors and put out your lights at the normal time. Let them think you are sleeping, awaiting your doom. Then, we slip through their fingers like shadows.”

He stayed for no more than half an hour, finalising the last details of the desperate venture before melting back into the darkness from whence he had come.

The final week began, and the pressure became almost unbearable. ‘7’, ‘6’, ‘5’. The numbers on the door seemed to mock them. One evening, a stone was thrown through the parlour window, a piece of paper wrapped around it. On it was written a single word: ‘Drebber’. The next night, another stone, another paper: ‘Stangerson’. They were being toyed with, like mice by a cat.

John Ferrier, fearing a direct assault, now slept with his rifle by his bed and his pistols on the table. He and Lucy took turns keeping watch through the night, peering out into the oppressive darkness, their nerves stretched to the breaking point.

At last, the final day dawned. The number on the door was ‘1’. Tomorrow, their fate would be decided. Throughout that long, terrible day, they went about their tasks with a forced and painful slowness, every nerve tingling with anticipation of the night to come. The farm had never seemed so dear to them, nor the sunlight so precious. They were looking upon it all for what might be the last time. As evening fell, a great storm blew in from the mountains, and the sky grew dark and heavy with unshed rain. A moaning wind rose, rattling the window frames and howling down the chimney like a lost soul. It was a fitting accompaniment to their mood of dread and suspense.

Following Hope’s instructions, Lucy extinguished the last lamp at nine o’clock. The house was plunged into blackness. They sat together in the dark, not daring to speak, listening to the fury of the wind and the ticking of the clock. Ten o’clock. Eleven. Midnight. Each hour was a lifetime. They had packed their small bundles and placed them by the back door. John Ferrier clutched his rifle, its cold steel a small comfort in the suffocating darkness.

One o’clock. The hour was approaching. Ferrier crept to the window and peered out. The storm was at its height. There was no moon, no stars, only a wild, tumultuous blackness through which the wind screamed. It was a perfect night for an escape. No sentry would be able to see a foot in front of his face.

Two o’clock struck from the tall clock in the corner, its chimes sounding unnaturally loud in the tense silence. It was the hour. It was time to go. John Ferrier took his daughter’s cold hand in his. “Now, Lucy,” he whispered. “Stay close behind me. Not a sound.”

He moved stealthily towards the back door, his moccasined feet making no noise on the wooden floor. He reached for the heavy iron bolt. The wind howled, and under its cover, he slowly, carefully, began to draw it back. The night of their deliverance, or of their doom, was upon them.

It was the voice of one of the sentries, a man named Tobin, known for his brutish zeal. Ferrier’s blood ran cold. They were discovered. All their careful planning, their weeks of agonising suspense, had come to nought at the final instant. He felt Lucy’s hand tremble in his own, and a wave of black despair washed over him. His rifle was in the house, a fool’s error, left by the window in his haste. They were trapped and unarmed.

Before he could utter a word, however, a second shadow detached itself from the deeper blackness of the night. It moved with the silent swiftness of a panther. There was no cry, no sound of a struggle, only a soft, dull thud, as of a heavy fruit falling to the earth, followed by a low, gurgling sigh that was horribly and instantly extinguished. The sentry was no more.

Another figure loomed up beside them, and a familiar, low voice spoke in Ferrier’s ear. “Quickly. There is no time to lose. The relief will be around in ten minutes.”

It was Jefferson Hope. He pushed the door open, and drawing them out into the lashing rain, he closed it gently behind them. “The horses are tethered in the cottonwood grove beyond the creek,” he whispered, his voice a mere breath in the gale. “We must run for it. Keep low and follow me.”

He set off at a crouch, and the two fugitives followed as best they might, their feet sinking into the soft, rain-soaked earth. The storm, which had so nearly betrayed them, was now their greatest ally. It swallowed every sound of their flight, its wild clamour a shield against discovery. They splashed through the swollen creek, the icy water rising to their knees, and scrambled up the muddy bank on the other side. There, as Hope had promised, stood three horses, saddled and ready, their heads drooping miserably in the downpour.

Hope helped Lucy into her saddle with a gentleness that sat strangely upon his grim and resolute bearing. Ferrier, his limbs stiff with age and anxiety, mounted his own horse with some difficulty. In another moment, Hope had swung himself into the third saddle, and they were off, riding hard into the teeth of the wind, away from the accursed valley of the Saints.

They had not gone more than a mile, urging their steeds to a desperate gallop through the darkness and the mire, when the sound they had been dreading reached their ears. A single shot cracked through the tempest, sharp and clear. The alarm had been given. Almost immediately, it was followed by the frantic baying of hounds, and then, more terrifying still, the distant thunder of many hoof-beats. Their escape had been discovered, and the pursuit was on.

“Faster!” cried Hope, his voice rising above the shriek of the wind. “They will ride us down if we linger on this plain! To the hills! We must make for the broken country!”

He veered to the east, towards the dark and jagged line of mountains that loomed against the horizon. It was a terrible ride. The ground was treacherous, pitted with holes and slick with mud. Twice, Ferrier’s horse stumbled, nearly throwing him from the saddle. The rain beat down upon them, cold and merciless, blinding their eyes and soaking them to the skin. Behind them, the sounds of the chase grew steadily louder, a relentless and horrifying chorus of shouts and barking and galloping hooves. They were gaining. The Saints, mounted on fresh and superior beasts, were closing the gap with dreadful certainty.

John Ferrier could feel his strength beginning to fail. The wild ride was a sore trial for a man of his years, and the cold had settled deep into his bones. He glanced at his daughter. She rode bravely, her face pale but set, her eyes fixed upon the broad back of the young hunter who led them. All her faith, all her hope for salvation, rested upon that one man.

Suddenly, a cry of dismay broke from Ferrier’s lips. His horse, which had been labouring heavily, stumbled again, and this time it went down, pitching him forward into the mud. The poor beast struggled for a moment, then lay still with a dreadful, groaning sigh, its foreleg broken.

Hope reined in his own mount and was at Ferrier’s side in an instant, dragging him to his feet. “Are you hurt?” he asked, his voice sharp with urgency.

“No, no, I am uninjured,” panted the old man. “But the horse… it is done for.”

The sounds of the pursuit were terrifyingly close now. They could distinguish individual voices, calling to one another in the darkness. To be caught here, on the open plain, was to be caught to a certainty. For a single, dreadful moment, it seemed that all was lost.

Jefferson Hope’s face was a mask of grim determination in the gloom. “There is but one chance,” he said, his mind working with lightning speed. “Ferrier, you must ride with me. Lucy, give me your bundle. I will cut your pack-horse free. They will follow it, thinking it is one of us.”

He acted as he spoke. With a slash of his knife, he freed the small pack-horse that carried their meagre provisions, slapping it hard upon the flank. The terrified animal bolted into the night, heading straight back in the direction from which they had come. Then, seizing Ferrier by the arm, Hope half-lifted him onto the saddle behind him. Their combined weight was a heavy burden for the horse, but the brave animal responded to its master’s call, plunging onward once more towards the shadowy hills.

The ruse, born of desperation, had its effect. They heard shouts from behind them, and the main body of the riders swerved to follow the fleeing pack-horse. A smaller group, however, perhaps four or five in number, had not been deceived. They had seen the fall, and they kept on, their quarry’s dark shapes silhouetted for a moment against a break in the storm-clouds.

“They are still after us!” gasped Ferrier.

“I know it,” Hope answered between his teeth. “But we have thinned them out. Now it is a fairer race.”

They had reached the foothills, a confusing labyrinth of ravines and rocky outcrops. Here, Jefferson Hope was in his element. He guided the horse through the darkness with an uncanny skill, his eyes seeming to pierce the blackest shadows. He twisted and turned through narrow defiles, splashed through rock-strewn gullies, and scrambled up steep inclines where it seemed no horse could possibly find a footing. The animal under them, a tough and wiry mustang, was as sure-footed as a goat, and it responded to his every touch.

Behind them, the pursuit faltered. The city-bred men and horses of the Avenging Angels were no match for the hunter and his steed in this rugged terrain. The shouts grew more distant, more scattered. At one point, they heard a crash and a scream from the ravine below them, and knew that one of their pursuers had met with disaster in the darkness.

For nearly an hour, Hope pushed on, never slackening his pace, until at last he drew rein in a deep and narrow canyon, its high walls muffling the sound of the wind. “We will rest here,” he said, his voice low and breathy. “They have lost the trail for now.”

He helped the exhausted Ferrier to the ground. The old man was trembling violently, his face ashen. Lucy slipped from her saddle, her legs so stiff she could hardly stand, and went to her father’s side.

“Oh, father, you are chilled to the bone,” she murmured, chafing his cold hands.

Hope, after listening intently for any sound of pursuit, led the horses under a rocky overhang. He returned with a small flask from his saddlebag. “Here, Ferrier,” he said, handing it to the old man. “Drink this. It is brandy. It will put some warmth back in you.”

Ferrier took a long swallow of the fiery liquid, and a little colour returned to his cheeks. “You have saved us, my boy,” he said, his voice thick with emotion. “You have saved us both. How can I ever repay you?”

“There is no talk of repayment between us,” said Hope, his eyes finding Lucy’s in the darkness. “All I ask is that you let me see you both to safety.”

They rested for a short while in the shelter of the canyon, listening to the storm rage above them. The immediate danger was past, but they were far from safe. They were fugitives in a vast and pitiless wilderness, with a powerful and relentless organisation sworn to their destruction. Their one remaining horse was weary, their provisions were gone with the pack-animal, and Ferrier’s strength was clearly failing.

As the first, grey light of dawn began to creep into the sky, Jefferson Hope rose to his feet. “We must be moving,” he said. “We cannot stay here. They will scour these hills as soon as it is light enough to see.”

The storm had passed, leaving a cold, clean world in its wake. They set out again, this time at a much slower pace. Hope led the horse, upon which Lucy now rode alone, while he and Ferrier walked beside it. The young hunter moved with an easy, tireless stride, but the old farmer struggled to keep up, his breath coming in ragged gasps.

Their flight now assumed a new and more terrible character. The desperate chase of the night was replaced by a grim and silent battle against nature itself. They were in a land of rock and scrub, a desolate wilderness that offered neither food nor shelter. By midday, the sun had burned away the clouds and beat down upon them with a fierce intensity. Thirst, a far more terrible enemy than the Avenging Angels, began to torment them.

Hope, with his hunter’s knowledge, led them to a small, hidden spring, where they drank deeply and refilled his flask. He shot a rock-rabbit with a small pistol he carried, and that night they roasted its meagre flesh over a tiny, smokeless fire of dry sagebrush. It was a poor meal, but it renewed their strength.

For three days they journeyed thus, pushing ever deeper into the wilderness. Jefferson Hope was their guide, their protector, their provider. He seemed to be a man made of iron and whipcord, impervious to fatigue or despair. His rifle, which had been strapped to his saddle, provided them with what little game the barren land had to offer. On the fourth day, he could walk no farther. He collapsed by the trail, and it was clear to both Hope and Lucy that he could not rise again. His face was flushed and his eyes were unnaturally bright.

He had no medicines, and their food was all but gone. He hunted ceaselessly, but game was scarce, and he dared not fire his rifle too often for fear of attracting notice. On the evening of the second day, he returned to their camp empty-handed and exhausted, to find Lucy weeping over her father. John Ferrier’s ordeal was over. Hope removed his hat and stood for a long moment in silence, his rugged face full of a sorrowful pity. Jefferson Hope came and stood before her. He took her unresisting hands in his. “Lucy,” he said, his voice hoarse with a depth of feeling he could no longer conceal. “He is at rest. But we must live. She looked up at him, her gaze full of a lost and desolate appeal. In the whole, wide, terrible world, he was all that she had left. She could only nod, unable to speak, and put her trust in the grim, devoted man who had already sacrificed so much for her.

He scanned the horizon, his gaze sweeping across the darkening plain. For a moment he saw nothing but the endless grey expanse. Then, far away to the west, he saw it. There, in the lee of a rocky outcrop, was a sight that made him halt in his tracks: the faint, unmistakable flicker of a campfire.

For a long moment he stood motionless, his mind racing. Who could it be? A band of Indians? Or, worse still, a party of the Elders’ men, the very pursuers from whom they were fleeing? The risk was enormous. Yet, to remain where they were was to accept certain death. He looked down at the pale, still form of the girl at the foot of the rock, and his decision was made. Whatever the danger, it must be faced. He scrambled down from his perch, his movements now infused with a renewed, if desperate, energy. He gently shook Lucy’s shoulder.

“Lucy,” he whispered, his voice a hoarse croak. “Lucy, wake up. There is a fire. We have a chance.”

Her eyes fluttered open, vacant and uncomprehending. He offered her his arm, and with his help she struggled to her feet, swaying with weakness. The horse, their faithful companion in misfortune, raised its head and gave a low whinny, as if it too sensed a change in their fortunes.

“We must be cautious,” Hope said, more to himself than to her. He unslung his rifle, checked its mechanism, and then, leading the horse with one hand and supporting Lucy with the other, he began to make his way slowly and silently towards the distant light.

Every shadow seemed to hold a menace, every rustle of the night wind sounded like the footfall of an enemy. He advanced with the stealth of a panther, placing each foot with infinite care, his eyes fixed upon the flickering light which now grew steadily brighter. As they drew nearer, he could make out the dark shape of a small, canvas-topped wagon and the figures of several mules picketed nearby. Crouching behind a line of chaparral bushes, he peered into the circle of firelight.

A single man sat beside the blaze, a grizzled and weather-beaten individual with a long, grey beard that covered his chest. He was dressed in the rough clothes of a prospector, and was occupied in stirring the contents of a small iron pot that hung over the flames. An air of quiet contentment hung about him, the air of a man at peace with himself and the world. There was nothing in his appearance to suggest the stern fanaticism of the Saints, nor the predatory savagery of the Indian. After a moment of intense scrutiny, Hope made his choice.

He stepped out from the shadows, holding his rifle loosely in the crook of his arm so that it should not appear as a threat.

“Good evening, stranger,” he called out, his voice steady despite the tremor of exhaustion that ran through him.

The old prospector started, his hand flying to a long-barrelled pistol that lay on the ground beside him. He peered into the darkness, his eyes narrowed. “Who goes there?” he demanded, his voice a gravelly rasp. “Speak up, or I’ll put a hole in ye.”

“A friend,” said Hope, advancing slowly into the light, pulling the horse and the stumbling girl behind him. “A traveller in need. We have lost our way, and are without food or water.”

The old man’s eyes widened as he took in the pitiful sight before him. He saw the gaunt, sun-blasted man with the burning eyes, and the pale, exhausted girl who clung to him for support. His hand relaxed its grip on his pistol, and a look of compassion softened his rugged features.

“Well, I’ll be,” he muttered, rising to his feet. “Lost is one word for it. Ye look half-starved, the both of ye. Come in, come in to the fire. Don’t stand there in the dark.”

He bustled about, his initial suspicion forgotten in a flood of rough hospitality. He ladled a steaming broth from the pot into two tin cups, and produced a slab of corn-bread and a side of bacon from a wooden chest in his wagon. He watched in silence as Hope and Lucy fell upon the food with an eagerness that told its own story. He unharnessed their horse and led it to a trough of water, patting its neck with a knowing hand.

“Poor beast’s seen some hard miles,” he remarked. “Same as you two, I reckon.”

When they had eaten their fill, and the blessed relief of a full stomach had begun to work its magic, the old man filled a blackened pipe and lit it with a brand from the fire. For a time he smoked in contemplative silence, his shrewd eyes moving from Hope’s grim, watchful face to Lucy’s downcast one.

“My name’s Silas,” he said at last. “Silas Kane. Been prospecting these hills for twenty years. And who might you be?”

Hope hesitated for a fraction of a second. “My name is Harrison,” he said. “And this is my sister, Mary. Our wagon broke an axle a week back, and we have been on foot since.”

It was a flimsy tale, and he knew it. But the prospector seemed to accept it without question. He merely nodded, puffing on his pipe. “Hard country to be stranded in,” he said. “Headed for the diggings in Carson, were ye?”

“That was our intention,” Hope replied.

“Well, ye’re in luck,” said Silas Kane. “I’m bound for Carson City myself, to sell my dust and take on supplies. It’s another three days’ journey, but my wagon’s sturdy and my mules are strong. Ye are welcome to travel with me, if ye’ve a mind to it.”

The offer was a godsend, a reprieve from the very jaws of death. A wave of profound relief washed over Jefferson Hope, so potent that it left him feeling weak and light-headed. He looked at Lucy. A faint touch of colour had returned to her cheeks, and the terrible vacancy in her eyes had been replaced by a look of utter weariness. She was safe, for the moment.

“We would be grateful for your kindness, sir,” Hope said, his voice thick with an emotion he could not entirely suppress. “We are in your debt.”

“Pshaw,” said the old man, waving a dismissive hand. “There’s no debt out here but the one we all owe to our maker. We’re all just poor devils trying to scratch a living from this hard old earth. Now, get some sleep, you two. You look as though you could sleep for a week.”

He pointed to a pile of blankets near the wagon. Hope spread them out for Lucy, and she sank down upon them without a word, falling almost instantly into the deep, dreamless sleep of utter exhaustion. He covered her gently, then took up his own position on the other side of the fire, his rifle across his knees. He did not intend to sleep. Despite the prospector’s apparent kindness, he could not afford to relax his guard. For hours he sat, a silent, wakeful sentinel, his mind a whirl of conflicting thoughts. He had cheated death, but for how long? Carson City was their destination, a place of new beginnings. But he knew, with a cold certainty that chilled him to the bone, that the shadows of Salt Lake City were long, and that they would stretch even as far as the silver mines of Nevada.

True to his word, Silas Kane proved to be a generous and undemanding companion. The three days to Carson City passed without incident, a period of blessed recovery for the two fugitives. Under the old prospector’s gruff but kindly care, a measure of strength returned to their wasted frames. Hope’s gauntness lessened, and the iron resolve returned to his jaw. Even Lucy seemed to rally. The colour deepened in her cheeks, and though she remained quiet and withdrawn, the dreadful, vacant stare was seen less often in her eyes. She would listen for hours to Silas’s rambling tales of boom and bust, of fortunes found and lost in the great Nevada silver rush, and sometimes a faint, ghost of a smile would touch her lips.

Carson City, when they reached it, was a raw and turbulent place, a sprawling settlement of wooden shanties, saloons, and assay offices, thrown up in a dusty canyon in the shadow of the Sierra Nevada. The streets teemed with a rough and motley crowd: hard-faced miners in flannel shirts and high boots, sleek gamblers in black broadcloth, merchants, teamsters, and all the human flotsam and jetsam that a rich mineral strike casts upon its shores. It was a city of noise, of energy, of desperate hopes and frequent disappointments; and for Jefferson Hope, it was the perfect hiding place. Here, in this swirling vortex of humanity, a man’s past was his own affair. No one asked questions.

They parted from Silas Kane with genuine regret. The old prospector refused any payment for his help, waving away the pouch of gold dust that Hope had earned by a stroke of luck years before.

“Keep it, son,” he said, clapping him on the shoulder. “Ye’ll need it to get started. Look after the little one. She has sad eyes.” With a final nod, he turned and disappeared into the throng of the main street, and they never saw him again.

With the small capital at his disposal, Hope was able to secure a small, clean cabin on the outskirts of the town. He found work at one of the larger mines, the Consolidated Virginia, where his immense strength and tireless capacity for labour quickly earned him the respect of his fellow workers and the favour of the foreman. The work was brutal and dangerous, a daily descent into the hot, dark bowels of the earth, but he welcomed it. The physical exhaustion it produced helped to quiet the turmoil in his mind, and the steady wages provided a security he had not known for years.

For a time, it seemed as if they had truly found a haven. A fragile peace settled over their small household. Lucy, in the clean and quiet cabin, began to show the first real signs of recovery. She took to tending a small garden, and the simple tasks of housekeeping seemed to restore a measure of purpose to her days. She began to speak more freely, to recall small, happy memories of her childhood in Illinois, before the long and fateful journey west. Hope, returning from the mine each evening, would find a warm meal waiting for him, and in her gentle presence he found a solace that soothed his own bruised and battered spirit. The love he bore for her, which had been forged in the crucible of shared danger and loss, deepened into a quiet, protective devotion. He did not press his suit, for he could see that she was not yet ready for it. It was enough for him that she was safe, and that he was there to watch over her.

And yet, the shadow of the past was never entirely absent. It lurked in the moments of silence that fell between them, in the sudden, haunted look that would sometimes cross her face, in the nightmares that caused him to start up in the dead of night, his hand reaching for the rifle that was never far from his side. He knew that their peace was a precarious one, a temporary truce in a war that was not yet over.

The blow, when it came, was as swift and unexpected as a flash of lightning from a clear sky. It was a Saturday evening, some eighteen months after their arrival in Carson City. Hope had just been paid, and was returning to the cabin, his heart light, for he had decided that the time was now right to speak to Lucy of marriage, to offer her the final protection of his name. As he walked down the dusty street, he saw a group of men gathered around the notice board outside the Wells Fargo office. Curiosity piqued, he paused to see what news had attracted their attention.

A stout, red-faced man was reading aloud from a printed notice in a booming voice. “A reward of five hundred dollars,” he read, “is hereby offered by the council of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints for information leading to the whereabouts of one Lucy Ferrier, an apostate and fugitive from the authority of the holy prophet, believed to be in the company of a gentile murderer known as Jefferson Hope.”

The world seemed to stop. The blood drained from Hope’s face, and the roar of the city faded to a dull hum in his ears. He stood as if turned to stone, the words echoing in his brain like a death knell. Five hundred dollars. They had put a price on her head. The long arm of the Salt Lake prophets had reached them, even here.

His first instinct was to tear the notice down, to scatter the men with his bare hands. But his second, colder thought restrained him. He turned away, pulling his hat down to hide his face, and moved into the shadows of an alleyway, his mind working with frantic speed. They had to leave. They had to leave at once. But where could they go? The notice would be in every town, on every telegraph pole from Nevada to the coast.

As he stood there, wrestling with his despair, he saw a familiar figure pass by on the opposite side of the street. It was Joseph Stangerson. He wore the broadcloth of a prosperous citizen, and walked with an air of arrogant self-importance, but there was no mistaking the cruel, thin-lipped mouth and the cold, calculating eyes. He was here. He was in Carson City.

At that moment, all thought of flight, of concealment, vanished from Jefferson Hope’s mind. A great and terrible change took place within him. The smouldering embers of his hatred, banked for so long beneath the ashes of a quiet life, burst into a raging, all-consuming flame. He had run from these men, had hidden from them, had tried to build a life out of the wreckage they had made. But there was no running. There was no hiding. They would hunt Lucy to the ends of the earth.

A cold, deadly calm descended upon him. His purpose, which had been to protect, was now transformed. It was no longer enough to save Lucy. He must destroy those who tormented her. The hunter must become the hunted. He watched as Stangerson entered a saloon, and a grim smile touched his lips, a smile that would have chilled the blood of any who saw it. He would not act now, not in a rash and foolish manner. His revenge would be a thing of patience and cunning, a dish served cold, as the saying goes.

He turned and strode back towards the cabin, his step firm and resolute. The long and bitter journey of which he had been dimly aware on the Alkali Plain now stretched before him, clear and terrible in its outlines. It was a road that would lead him through darkness and deceit, a path of vengeance that would twist and turn for many a year and across many a thousand miles. It would lead him, ultimately, to a foggy street in London, and to a final, bloody reckoning in a dusty, empty house. But of that, he knew nothing. He knew only that his life now had but one object: to be the instrument of justice, the avenging angel for the wrongs done to John Ferrier and the woman he loved. And as he walked through the deepening twilight, the weight of that terrible oath settled upon his soul, and Jefferson Hope, the gentle protector, was no more. In his place walked a grim and patient executioner.

When Jefferson Hope re-entered the small, crudely-built cabin which had served as their refuge, the change in his bearing was so palpable that the young woman who sat by the hearth looked up with an expression of sudden alarm. Her face, though still beautiful in its delicate outlines, was thin and worn, and a constant, nervous tremor afflicted her hands. It was the face of one who had looked upon horrors and who lived in their perpetual shadow.

“Jefferson,” she said, her voice a mere whisper, “what has occurred? Your face is as a mask of stone.”

He came to her side and took one of her trembling hands in his own, which was now as steady as the rock of the Sierra itself. He forced a softness into his eyes which he no longer felt in his heart, a tenderness reserved for her alone in all the world.

“Rest easy, my dear Lucy,” he said, his tone low and soothing. “I have but come to a resolution concerning our future. We have been adrift too long upon a sea of uncertainty; I mean now to chart a course.”

She searched his features, her large, fearful eyes seeking the truth behind his words. She saw the grim set of his jaw and the cold fire that had replaced the weary sadness in his gaze, and she shivered. This was not the man who had left the cabin an hour before. The gentle protector had been supplanted by something harder, something more dangerous.

“What course is it?” she asked. “Are we to flee again? To Nevada? To the coast?”

“There will be no more fleeing,” he answered, and the finality in his voice was like the closing of a tomb. “I have seen that to run is but to prolong the chase. No, we shall stay here, for a time. But you cannot remain in this place; it is too exposed, too near the town.”

He had already formed the beginnings of a plan. There was an old washerwoman, a widow named McCabe, who lived in a secluded shanty at the very edge of the settlement, a good woman whose silence he had purchased once before with a silver dollar for a small service. She was beholden to no man and had a reputation for minding her own affairs with a fierce and solitary pride. To her he would entrust Lucy’s care.

“I am making arrangements for you to stay with a person I can trust,” he explained, avoiding her questioning eyes. “You will be safe there, and I shall be able to visit you. It is a temporary measure, Lucy, until I have made this land safe for you forever.”

“Safe forever?” she repeated, the words holding a meaning she could not quite grasp. “But how, Jefferson? They are powerful. Their arms are long.”

“Their arms may be long,” he said, a grim smile touching his lips for an instant, “but they are not unbreakable. Trust in me, Lucy. You have trusted me across a thousand miles of desert and mountain. Trust me a little longer.”

He did not tell her that he had seen Joseph Stangerson, for he knew the name alone would be enough to send her into a paroxysm of terror. He spoke only of a new hope, a new strategy, and though his words were vague, his iron confidence had its effect. She allowed herself to be persuaded, her will being but the frail echo of his own. That very night, under the cloak of a moonless sky, he moved her and their few belongings to the widow McCabe’s hut, pressing into the old woman’s hand a sum that made her eyes widen. He gave her strict instructions, his voice holding a menace that guaranteed her compliance more surely than any gold. Lucy was to be seen by no one; she was a sick relative from the east, and her ailment was such that she required absolute quiet and seclusion.

With Lucy secured, Jefferson Hope was free to turn his full and undivided attention to the task he had set himself. He became a creature of the shadows, a phantom haunting the dusty streets of Carson City. He sold his rifle, the trusty companion of his wilderness years, and with the proceeds he purchased the drab and common attire of a labouring man. He let his beard grow more ragged and allowed the alkali dust to settle upon him, until he was indistinguishable from the scores of other forgotten men who drifted through the mining town, seeking work or whiskey.

His first object of study was the saloon where he had seen Stangerson. It was called “The Gilded Cage,” a garish establishment of false fronts and swinging doors, from which emanated the constant sounds of coarse laughter and tinkling pianos. For two days he watched it from across the street, his patience as boundless as the desert. He saw Stangerson come and go, always in the company of two or three other men, hard-faced individuals with watchful eyes who were clearly more than mere companions. Then, on the third evening, he saw the other.

Enoch Drebber came out of the saloon, his arm linked with Stangerson’s. He was a man of florid complexion, with a thick neck and a self-satisfied smirk that seemed permanently affixed to his features. He was dressed in expensive, if ill-fitting, clothes, and a large diamond pin glittered in his necktie. As Hope watched, Drebber laughed, a loud, braying sound that grated on the nerves, and slapped Stangerson upon the back with an air of gross familiarity. They were a pair, then, just as they had been in Utah: the lean, cruel wolf and the fat, preening jackal. The sight of them together, prosperous and arrogant, sent a surge of pure venom through Hope’s veins, yet his face remained a stony, impassive mask.

He needed to get closer, to hear their plans. To that end, he sought work at the livery stable connected with the hotel where, as he had discovered, the two men were lodging. His knowledge of horses, gleaned from his years on the plains, was second to none, and he was hired at once as a stable-hand. It was grimy, back-breaking work, but it afforded him the perfect vantage point. He was now a part of the scenery, a man to be looked through, not at. He mucked out stalls and polished harnesses, his ears ever open.

From the fragments of conversation he overheard, from the careless gossip of other grooms and the drunken boasts of the men Stangerson and Drebber employed, he began to piece together a picture of their lives. They had come from Salt Lake City not a fortnight since, on business for the church. They were buying up land and mining claims, using the immense wealth of the community to extend its temporal power into Nevada. They were figures of importance, surrounded by a retinue of their own people, the quiet, grim-faced men he had noted, whom the other townsfolk referred to in hushed tones as the “Avenging Angels.” They were a secret police, an instrument of the prophets’ will, and their presence made it clear that Drebber and Stangerson were not merely prosperous; they were protected.

A direct assault, he saw, was folly. To attack them here would be to bring the entire hornet’s nest down upon his head. He would be shot down in the street, and his death would be a nine days’ wonder, nothing more. His revenge had to be a thing of stealth and precision. It had to be personal. He wanted more than their deaths; he wanted them to know, in their final moments, from whence their doom had come. He wanted to see the name of John Ferrier and his daughter upon their lips as they drew their last, shuddering breaths.

For a week he laboured and listened. He learned that Drebber was a heavy drinker and a braggart, while Stangerson was the more cunning of the two, abstemious and watchful. It was Stangerson who held the purse-strings and directed their affairs; Drebber was but his coarse and swaggering subordinate. It was Stangerson, Hope deduced, who was the true power, and the true danger.

An opportunity, or what seemed to be one, presented itself one evening. He heard Drebber, his voice thick with liquor, arguing with Stangerson in the stable-yard as they waited for their horses.

“I am weary of this business, Joseph,” Drebber complained. “It is all ledgers and maps. I came for the excitement of a new territory, not to be a clerk. I mean to have some amusement tonight.”

“You will do as you are told, Enoch,” Stangerson’s voice was cold and sharp as ice. “We have an appointment with the agent from the land office in one hour. Your amusement can wait.”

“Let it wait, then!” Drebber retorted, his tone mutinous. “I am going to Madame Lebrun’s gambling house. You can meet with the agent yourself. You are better with figures than I.”

Before Stangerson could reply, Drebber had turned on his heel and staggered out of the stable-yard, heading down the street in the opposite direction from the hotel. Stangerson stared after him for a moment, his face a mask of cold fury. Then, with a muttered curse, he gestured to one of his guards.

“Follow him,” he commanded. “See that he does not get into any serious trouble. I will handle the agent.”

The guard nodded and set off after Drebber, while Stangerson went upon his own errand. Here was his chance. Drebber was separated from his master, with only a single guard, and his wits were clouded by drink. Madame Lebrun’s was a notorious den at the far end of the town, a place where a man might easily meet with a fatal accident.

Hope’s heart began to beat a slow, heavy drum against his ribs. He wiped his hands on a rag, murmured an excuse to the head groom about an errand, and slipped out of the stable. He moved through the back alleys, his stride long and silent. He knew a shortcut, a narrow, unlit passage that ran behind the gambling house. Drebber would likely leave by the back entrance if he won heavily, or was thrown out if he lost. In either case, he would be vulnerable.

In the passage, the darkness was almost absolute, thick with the stench of refuse and stale beer. Hope flattened himself against a rough wooden wall, his hand resting on the hilt of a heavy knife he had concealed in his belt. He was a patient man. He could wait for hours if need be. He heard the muffled sounds from the gambling house, a sudden shout, a burst of laughter, the clatter of a thrown bottle. Minutes stretched into what seemed like an hour.

Then he heard footsteps approaching the rear door. It creaked open, spilling a rectangle of yellow, smoky light into the alley. Two figures were framed in the doorway. One was Drebber, looking flushed and angry. The other was the guard who had been sent to follow him.

“You lost it all, then?” the guard was saying, his voice contemptuous. “Stangerson will not be pleased.”

“To hell with Stangerson!” Drebber snarled. “The cards were marked. The whole place is a cheat! I should have burned it to the ground.”

“Come,” the guard said, taking his arm. “Let us get you back before you cause more trouble.”

They stepped out into the alley. Hope tensed, his muscles coiling. He had only to take two strides. The guard was between him and Drebber. He would have to deal with the guard first, a quick, silent thrust. Then Drebber, the drunken, helpless fool, would be at his mercy.

He was about to spring when a third figure appeared, stepping out of the deeper shadows at the far end of the alley. For a wild instant, Hope thought it was one of his own tribe, another hunter. But the man’s bearing was familiar. He wore the plain, dark coat of the Elders, and he moved with a quiet, deadly authority. It was another of them.

“Brother Drebber,” the newcomer said, his voice soft yet carrying a clear note of command. “Brother Stangerson grows impatient. Your conduct brings discredit upon us. You are to return with me at once.”

Drebber, who had been about to argue with his guard, seemed to shrink. The drunken belligerence evaporated from him, replaced by a sullen obedience. Even the guard appeared deferential.

“Yes, Brother Cowper,” Drebber mumbled, his eyes on the ground.

The three men turned and walked away, their footsteps receding down the alley. Hope remained frozen in the darkness, his hand still clenched around the hilt of his knife. His chance had been stolen from him. They were never truly alone. The web of their organisation was everywhere, its strands invisible until one was about to strike. He realised with a fresh wave of cold fury that to kill these men here, in a town where their power was so entrenched, would be almost impossible. He had underestimated the discipline and reach of their secret brotherhood. They were not merely two men; they were the heads of a hydra, and for every guard he struck down, another would appear.

A profound change came over his strategy. His rage did not diminish, but it became colder, more calculating. If he could not strike them here, then he would follow them. He would follow them to the ends of the earth if he had to. He would become their shadow, a persistent, inescapable doom that they would not even know was there until the final moment of reckoning. The chase might take years. It might take him across continents and oceans. He did not care. His life had no other purpose.

He returned to the livery stable, his expression unreadable. For another week he worked and watched, gathering every scrap of information. His patience was rewarded when he heard Stangerson making arrangements for a journey. They were to travel to St. Louis, and from there, it was rumoured, to Cleveland, where the church had other business. They would be travelling by stagecoach, and then by the new railroad.

The day of their departure arrived. Jefferson Hope stood in the stable-yard, ostensibly polishing a bit of brass, and watched them go. Stangerson, Drebber, and their two ever-present guards climbed into a private coach. He saw Stangerson’s cold, commanding profile at the window, and Drebber’s fleshy, dissipated face beside it. He memorised their faces, burning the images into his brain until they were as much a part of him as his own heartbeat.

As the coach rattled away in a cloud of dust, a grim sense of satisfaction settled upon him. This was not an escape; it was but the next stage of the hunt. He had a small store of money, saved from his prospecting days. It would be enough. He went to the head groom, told him he was quitting, that he had a touch of the lung fever and needed to seek a better climate. No one questioned him. He was just another piece of human driftwood, floating away.

Before he left Carson City, he paid one last visit to the widow McCabe’s hut. He stood in the shadows of the doorway, looking at Lucy as she slept. Her breathing was shallow, and her face, in the pale light of the moon, was almost translucent. A great wave of pity and love washed over him, extinguishing for a moment the cold fire of his hatred. He was doing this for her. He was her avenger, the instrument of a justice that the world had denied her. He bent down and lightly kissed her forehead, a silent farewell. He knew, with a terrible certainty in his heart, that he would never see her alive again. Her frail constitution was no match for the slow poison of her memories. But he would keep his vow. He would pursue her destroyers until either they or he were in the grave.

He turned and walked away from the hut without a backward glance. He did not head for the stagecoach office. Instead, he sought out the driver of a freight wagon, a man he had spoken with at the livery. For a handful of silver, he bought passage east, to be just one more silent figure among the bales and barrels. He was a hunter, and the first rule of the hunt is not to let your prey know they are being stalked.

As the heavy wagon lumbered out of the sleeping town, Jefferson Hope looked east towards the vast, dark continent that stretched before him. Somewhere out there, his quarry was moving, feeling safe and powerful. They had no idea that behind them, patient and relentless as time itself, their doom was following. The long chase had begun.
