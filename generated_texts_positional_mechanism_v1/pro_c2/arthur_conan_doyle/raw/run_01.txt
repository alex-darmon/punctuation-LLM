“It was a particularly fine bird,” said Holmes smoothly, “sold to a small inn or public-house for a Christmas club. The inn, I believe, is called The Alpha.”

The poulterer, whose name we learned was Breckinridge, narrowed his eyes. “How would you know that?”

“I do not know it. I am deducing it,” Holmes replied calmly. “The owner of the hat is a man of modest but regular habits, the sort who would belong to a Christmas club. Such clubs are often run from public-houses. I am merely searching for a poulterer who supplies such an establishment.”

Mr. Breckinridge grunted, neither confirming nor denying the statement. His gaze fell upon the battered hat which Holmes was holding.

“Sold all my club geese to the landlord of The Alpha, Mr. Windigate,” he said, his tone softening slightly. “Had a book full of the names. Paid up weekly, they did. Got their bird on the 22nd.”

“And were all these geese of your own rearing?”

“Some. Others I buy in from breeders. Had a fine batch from a Mrs. Oakshott of Brixton this year. Twenty-four of ’em. The ones for The Alpha came from that lot.”

“Thank you, Mr. Breckinridge,” Holmes said, pressing a shilling into the man’s hand. “You have been most helpful.”

As we turned to leave, a small, rat-faced man with shifting eyes, who had been lurking near the stall, pushed his way forward.

“Here, guv’nor,” he whined at Breckinridge. “You said you’d sold all o’ them Oakshott geese. All of ’em. You’re sure about that?”

“I’m as sure as I’m standing here,” the poulterer snapped, his good humour evaporating. “I sold the lot to Windigate of The Alpha. Now clear off, Ryder, I’ve told you a dozen times. There’s none left.”

The man, Ryder, cast a desperate, hunted look around the market, his eyes meeting Holmes’s for a fleeting second before he scuttled away into the crowd.

“Did you see that, Watson?” Holmes murmured, his voice low and urgent. “That man is exceedingly interested in those particular geese.”

“He seemed quite distraught,” I observed.

“Distraught is the word. He is on the same trail as we are, but for a very different reason, I suspect. He is not searching for a lost dinner. He is searching for something that was *in* one of the birds. The plot thickens considerably. Come, let us make for The Alpha Inn. Our friend Ryder may well lead us there.”

The Alpha Inn was a modest public-house in a side-street near the British Museum. Its landlord, a portly and genial man named Windigate, remembered Henry Baker well.

“Oh yes, Mr. Baker’s one of our regulars,” he confirmed, polishing a glass. “A scholar, he is. Spends most of his days in the Reading Room. A very decent fellow, though fallen on hard times, I fear. Paid his shillings for the goose club all year. Was very pleased to collect his bird.”

“And you purchased these geese from a Mr. Breckinridge at the market?” Holmes asked.

“That’s right. A batch of two dozen from a farm in Brixton, he said.”

“Did you happen to notice anything unusual about one of the birds?”

The landlord laughed. “Unusual? Mr. Holmes, they are geese. They have feathers and beaks and they get roasted. What could be unusual?”

“Perhaps one was heavier than the others? Or had a mark upon it?”

“No, sir, not that I recall. All fine birds. Sold ’em all, and Mr. Baker’s was the last but one to be claimed.”

It seemed we had reached another dead end. As we prepared to take our leave, however, the inn door swung open and a new patron entered. It was a woman, dressed in the neat but plain uniform of a lady’s maid. She was pale and her hands trembled as she approached the bar.

“Mr. Windigate,” she began, her voice a nervous whisper. “I… I was sent by my cousin. James Ryder. He asked if you had any more of those special geese. He would pay double for one. Triple.”

Holmes and I exchanged a glance. Here was the other side of the conspiracy, standing before us.

The landlord shook his head. “I’m sorry, miss. They’re all gone. I told your cousin that myself this morning.”

The maid’s face fell, a mask of bitter disappointment. She seemed on the verge of tears. “Are you certain? There is not one left? It is a matter of the most terrible importance.”

“I am quite certain,” the landlord said, not unkindly. “Now, if you’ll excuse me, I have customers to attend to.”

The woman gave a little sob, turned, and fled from the inn as abruptly as she had arrived. Holmes watched her go, a grim line to his mouth.

“That, Watson,” he said quietly, “was Miss Catherine Cusack, maid to the Countess of Morcar. I recognise her from the newspaper engravings. She is the one who gave the primary evidence against John Horner. And she is the cousin of the man Ryder, who is so desperately seeking a goose from the Oakshott farm.”

“Holmes, this is incredible!” I exclaimed. “The thief and his accomplice are leading us right to the truth!”

“They are,” he agreed. “They believe the jewel is still lost within the flock. They do not know that a commissionaire’s wife is at this very moment preparing to cook the evidence. Their frantic search has provided us with the very links we were missing. The chain is complete: from the Countess’s jewel case, to the hands of Cusack and Ryder, into the throat of an Oakshott goose, through Breckinridge’s stall and Windigate’s inn, to the hands of Henry Baker, and finally, by a street brawl, to ours. All that remains is to close the net.”

“And how will you do that?”

“By waiting. It is nearly six o’clock. Our advertisement should have done its work. Let us return to Baker Street. I fancy we are about to receive the first of our visitors.”

We hurried back to our lodgings. Mrs. Hudson had lit the fire, and its cheerful blaze was a welcome sight. Holmes placed the battered hat on the centre of the table.

“Now, Watson,” he said, sinking into his armchair. “Let us see if my deductions about the owner are correct. A middle-aged, intellectual man, down on his luck, but retaining a shred of his former dignity.”

Punctually, as the clock on the mantelpiece struck six, there was a heavy, hesitant tread upon the stair, followed by a knock at our door.

“Here he is,” murmured Holmes. “The man who began it all. Enter!”

The door swung inward to admit a man of considerable stature, broad shouldered and stooping, with a large, intelligent head and a face that was clean shaven, kindly, and furrowed with the cares of a life of thought. He was clad in a respectable, if somewhat frayed, black frock coat, and he clutched a walking stick in a hand that was more accustomed, I should have guessed, to the pen than to the cudgel. His hair, which I could see was indeed grizzled about the temples, was neatly trimmed. As he stood blinking in the light of the lamp, his eyes fell upon the hat which lay upon our table, and a smile of recognition and relief spread across his features.

“Mr. Sherlock Holmes, I presume?” he said, his voice a deep and educated bass.

“Pray, sir, take a seat,” said Holmes, gesturing to the chair opposite his own. “You are, I believe, Mr. Henry Baker.”

“I am indeed, sir. I am here in response to your advertisement which I saw this afternoon in the Globe. I cannot express my gratitude. I had quite given up my poor old hat for lost, and the bird as well. It was a most unfortunate and somewhat undignified scuffle.”

“One finds that dignity is often the first casualty in such street encounters,” Holmes remarked. “We have, I am happy to say, recovered your hat.” He picked up the battered felt and handed it across. “As for the goose, I am afraid it has been eaten.”

Mr. Baker looked rather crestfallen at this news. “Eaten! Dear me, that is a pity. My wife was so looking forward to it. Still, one cannot have everything. I am most obliged to you for the trouble you have taken in returning my hat.”

“It was no trouble,” said Holmes. “However, we felt that as you had lost your dinner through this affair, it was only right that you should have a replacement. Peterson, my friend the commissionaire who found your belongings, purchased another fine bird for you this morning. It is in the kitchen, should you care to take it with you.”

A look of genuine delight shone in Mr. Baker’s eyes. “Now, that is truly a handsome offer!” he exclaimed. “You and your friend are most generous. I am a man of modest means, sir, and the loss of that goose was a heavier blow than you might imagine.”

“I imagine it was precisely as heavy a blow as the loss of a Christmas dinner would be to any honest gentleman,” said Holmes, watching our visitor with a keen, unwavering gaze. “We have retained the feathers, legs, and crop of your original bird, should you wish to have them back for any reason.”

Mr. Baker laughed a hearty, rumbling laugh. “The feathers and legs! My dear sir, what on earth should I do with such things? No, no, I am a simple scholar. It was the flesh of the bird I was after, not its anatomy. Keep them, by all means, or throw them upon the fire. The new goose is more than I could have hoped for. You have turned a most unfortunate incident into a happy one.”

He rose to his feet and bowed. “I do not know how to thank you, Mr. Holmes. If my small library of British birds can ever be of service to you, I should be delighted to offer it.”

“Thank you,” said Holmes, standing as well. “I shall remember the offer. Tell me, Mr. Baker, before you go, where did you procure the original goose? It was a rather remarkable specimen.”

“Ah, yes. It was from a little club I belong to, at the Alpha Inn near the Museum. The landlord, a good fellow named Windigate, has a scheme by which we pay a few pence a week and are assured of a goose at Christmas. It saves a lump sum payment, you see.”

“I see. A most provident arrangement,” said Holmes, his eyes twinkling. “I am acquainted with the Alpha. Well, Mr. Baker, I shall not detain you from your new dinner any longer.”

I accompanied our visitor downstairs and saw him off, a fine plump goose under his arm and his old hat restored to his head. When I returned to our rooms, Holmes was standing before the fire, his hands clasped behind his back.

“So much for Mr. Henry Baker,” said I. “He is precisely as you described him. Your deductions were flawless.”

“They were elementary, my dear Watson,” he replied. “He is clearly an innocent man, and a most deserving one. He knew nothing of what the bird contained.”

“And now we await the true culprit?”

“Precisely. He must come. He has exhausted every other avenue. The advertisements were his last hope. He will believe that Baker, upon finding the jewel, placed the notice to assuage his own conscience or perhaps to claim a reward. Ryder cannot afford to ignore it. He is a desperate man, and desperate men take desperate chances.”

Holmes had scarcely finished speaking when we heard a second footstep upon the stair. It was a complete contrast to the solid, measured tread of Mr. Baker. This was a quick, nervous, skittering sound, the footfall of a man in the grip of a powerful agitation. A timid and furtive knock sounded upon our door.

“This will be our man,” murmured Holmes. “Let us see what he has to say for himself. Come in!”

The man who entered was small and sharp-featured, with a receding chin and pale, watery eyes that darted about the room as if seeking a means of escape. He twisted a cloth cap in his hands, and his whole demeanour was one of abject terror.

“Mr. Holmes? The advertisement?” he stammered, his voice thin and reedy.

“Yes, I am Mr. Holmes. And who, may I ask, are you?”

“My name is John Robinson,” the man said, though he could not meet Holmes’s eye. “I have come about the goose. And the hat. Mr. Baker, he… he asked me to come for him.”

“Indeed?” said Holmes, raising an eyebrow. “That is most singular. Mr. Baker was here himself not ten minutes ago. He took his new goose and his old hat with him. I am afraid you are too late.”

The man’s jaw dropped. A ghastly pallor spread over his face, and he swayed as if he might faint. “He was here? He has the goose?” he whispered, his voice cracking. “Did he… did he find anything?”

“I think,” said Holmes, his voice suddenly as hard and cold as steel, “that we can dispense with this charade. You are not John Robinson. You are James Ryder, head attendant at the Hotel Cosmopolitan.”

Our visitor gave a strangled cry and staggered back against the wall, his hand to his throat. “No, sir! It is a mistake! I do not know the name!”

“It is useless to lie, Ryder,” Holmes continued relentlessly. “I know you, and I know why you are here. You are not interested in the hat, nor in the goose itself. You are interested in this.”

He walked to his desk, unlocked a small drawer, and took something out. He returned to the centre of the room and held out his hand. There, upon his open palm, lay the Blue Carbuncle, blazing with a cold, internal fire that seemed to mock the warm lamplight of our room.

Ryder stared at it, his eyes wide with a mixture of terror and ravenous desire. He gave a hoarse sob and took a stumbling step forward, his hands outstretched as if in prayer.

“You have it!” he gasped. “You have had it all along! Thank God! Oh, thank God!”

Holmes slipped the stone back into his pocket. “So much for your touching concern for Mr. Baker’s property,” he said dryly. “Now, Ryder, sit down in that chair and tell me the whole sordid story. I know most of it already, but I should like to have the details from your own lips. And be warned, if you do not tell me the truth, the whole truth, I will call a cab and we shall take a little journey to Scotland Yard together.”

The threat was enough. Ryder collapsed into the chair like a puppet whose strings had been cut. For a moment he was convulsed with sobs, his face buried in his trembling hands. Then, in a broken, miserable voice, he began his confession.

“I am not a bad man, Mr. Holmes, not by nature,” he began, wiping his eyes with the back of his hand. “I have never done a wrong thing before. But there was this man at the hotel, a valet to one of the guests, who had been in the diamond fields. He used to tell me of the fortunes to be made, of how a man could be rich for life with one lucky find. The stories, they got into my head, sir. I have debts. I saw no way out. And then I learned of the Countess of Morcar’s jewel, this Blue Carbuncle. I was in her suite every day, attending to her needs. I saw where she kept it. The temptation was too great. It preyed on my mind until I was half mad with it.”

“And so you framed the plumber, John Horner,” Holmes prompted.

Ryder flinched. “He had been in the room that morning to repair a grate. It was a chance, you see. I waited until he had left, then I forced the lock on the jewel case, took the stone, and ran downstairs to raise the alarm. I knew suspicion would fall on him. He had a previous conviction for theft, which made it all the easier. Everyone believed it was him. I thought I was safe.”

“And what did you do with the jewel?”

“I fled the hotel at once. I did not dare keep it on my person. I have a sister, sir, a Mrs. Oakshott, who lives in Brixton and raises geese for the London market. I ran to her house. I thought I would hide it until the hue and cry had died down, and then I would make my way to the continent and sell it in Amsterdam or Paris.”

“A fool’s errand,” Holmes murmured. “A stone of this fame is almost impossible to sell.”

“I know that now, sir,” Ryder wailed. “I was out of my mind. When I got to my sister’s, I went out to the yard behind the house. There was a great flock of geese there, all white with black-barred tails. My sister had promised me one for Christmas. An idea came to me. A mad idea. I caught the largest and finest bird I could see, prized its beak open, and pushed the stone down its throat. I felt it go down into its crop. I thought, who would ever think to look for it there? I could kill the bird at my leisure and recover it. But as I was holding the goose, it gave a great squawk and a flutter, and at that very moment, my sister came out into the yard.”

“‘What are you doing with that bird, Jem?’ she asks. I was so startled, sir, that the goose tore itself from my grasp and flapped away into the middle of the flock. ‘I was just choosing my Christmas goose,’ I said. ‘Well,’ she says, ‘your goose is the one I’ve put aside for you, it’s a fine fat one with a marked leg. But if you’d rather have one of these, you can choose.’ Sir, I could not tell her what I had done. And in my panic, I could not tell one goose from another. They were all identical! The one I had chosen had vanished among its fellows. I grabbed another bird, the one she had marked for me, and I took it away. I was in a fever of fear. I hoped, I prayed, that I had by some miracle picked the right one.”

He paused, breathing heavily, the memory of his frantic hope still vivid in his face.

“I took the bird to a friend of mine to be killed and dressed that very night,” he continued. “I could not bear the suspense. I tore it open myself. But there was nothing. The stone was gone. Then I understood the horror of my situation. The jewel was inside another goose, one of a flock of two dozen, which my sister was sending to a dealer in Covent Garden named Breckinridge. I raced back to my sister’s house, but it was too late. The geese were gone. I spent all of Christmas Day in an agony of mind. Yesterday morning, I went to this Breckinridge and tried to find out where the geese had gone. He was a coarse, abusive man. He told me he had sold the lot to the Alpha Inn. I went to the inn. The landlord told me he had sold all but one of his birds, and that one had gone to a Mr. Henry Baker.”

“And you tried to find Mr. Baker?”

“Yes, sir! I searched all day. I followed him from the Museum. I saw the scuffle at the corner of Tottenham Court Road. I saw his hat and the goose fall in the street. I tried to get to them, but that big commissionaire, Peterson, he got there first and drove me off. Then there was nothing. My last hope was gone. I sent my cousin, Catherine Cusack, to the inn to try and buy another goose, but it was no use. I was at the end of my rope. And then, today, I saw your advertisement. I thought perhaps Baker had found the stone and this was his way of trying to find the owner. It was my only chance. And so I came here.”

He finished his tale and sat slumped in his chair, a picture of utter dejection and despair, his face streaked with tears. There was a long silence in the room, broken only by the soft hiss of the gaslight and the crackle of the fire.

“The case is complete,” said Holmes at length, turning to me. “We know the thief, the motive, and the method. The only thing left is to decide his fate.” He turned his piercing gaze back upon the trembling man. “Horner, the plumber, is still in custody. His trial is due to begin within the week. If I hand you over to the police, the case against him will collapse. You will go to prison, and he will go free.”

Ryder flung himself from the chair and grovelled upon the carpet at Holmes’s feet. “For God’s sake, have mercy, sir!” he shrieked. “Think of my father! Think of my mother! It would break their hearts. I have never done wrong before! I will never do wrong again! I swear it on the Bible. Don’t send me to gaol! For the love of Christ, don’t send me to gaol!”

“Get up,” said Holmes sharply. The man scrambled back into his chair, his sobs shaking his small frame.

My friend walked over to the window and stood for a moment, looking out at the dark, swirling fog that blanketed the city. When he turned back, his face was thoughtful.

“It is a season of forgiveness,” he said, more to himself than to us. “Chance has put this pathetic creature in my power. He is not a hardened criminal. The fear of the last few days has been a more terrible punishment than any the law could devise. If he were sent to prison now, he would likely become a gaoler’s bird for life. Besides, it is a private matter. I have saved an innocent man from prosecution, and I have restored the Countess’s property. Why should I do the work of the police?”

He looked at Ryder with an expression of profound contempt. “James Ryder,” he said. “You are one of the sorriest scoundrels I have ever had the misfortune to meet, but I believe you are too frightened to ever transgress again. We are going to let you go.”

Ryder stared at him, unable to comprehend. “You mean… I am free?”

“Yes. But you are to get out of London, and out of England, for good. If you are ever seen in this city again, the bargain is off. Now go!”

He flung open the door. Ryder did not need a second telling. He gave a cry of disbelief and relief, snatched his cap, and fled from the room. We heard his feet clattering frantically down the stairs and the slam of the street door.

I stared at my friend in amazement. “Holmes!” I cried. “You cannot be serious! You have let him go! After all that, you have connived at a felony!”

“I suppose I am,” he said, a strange smile playing upon his lips. He walked over to the armchair and sank into it, picking up his clay pipe. “Perhaps I am committing a wrong, but I think I am saving a soul. This fellow will not go wrong again; he is too terribly frightened. Send him to gaol now, and you make him a gaol-bird for life. Besides, it is Christmas, Watson. And if I can spare a man from the clutches of the law and give him a second chance at a time of goodwill, I am not sure that it is not a greater service than handing him over to justice.”

He filled his pipe and lit it, the sharp aroma of his strong shag tobacco filling the room.

“My primary object, after all, was to solve the little problem of how a precious stone could find its way into the crop of a goose. That has been accomplished. The fate of the thief is a secondary consideration. Horner will be released, the Countess has her jewel, and we have had a most singular and instructive little adventure to brighten a foggy winter’s day. I fancy that when I am an old man, and I sit by the fire with you, my dear fellow, recounting the cases of our past, this business of the Blue Carbuncle will stand out as one of the most bizarre. And now, Watson, the night is still young. What do you say to a little music? I feel in a contemplative mood.”

And so, as the London fog pressed thick against our window-panes, the strange case of the Christmas goose came to its end with the low, melancholy wail of Sherlock Holmes’s violin.

Precious stone, sir? Or some sort of crystal?” “It is the Blue Carbuncle,” said Holmes, his voice hushed with a certain reverence, “the very stone which has been the talk of London for the past week. Its value can scarcely be estimated, but the reward of a thousand pounds offered by the Countess of Morcar gives some small indication of its worth.” He looked from the glittering gem to the commissionaire’s dumbfounded face. “Peterson, you have stumbled upon a fortune. But the question is, how did this stone, stolen from the Countess’s suite at the Hotel Cosmopolitan, find its way into the crop of a goose sold at a stall in Covent Garden?”

Before I could so much as venture a theory, or before Peterson could fully grasp the magnitude of his discovery, the air of quiet contemplation in our rooms was shattered by a heavy and insistent pounding upon our street door. It was not the tentative knock of a prospective client, but the authoritative summons of one who would not be denied.

Holmes raised an eyebrow, a flicker of annoyance crossing his features. “If I am not mistaken, that is the tread of our friend Lestrade upon the stair,” he remarked, carefully placing the Blue Carbuncle upon the mantelpiece. “He seems in a considerable hurry. I wonder what fresh crisis has sent him scurrying to Baker Street on Christmas night.”

The door flew open, just as it had for Peterson, to admit the wiry, ferret-like figure of the Scotland Yard detective. His face was flushed, not with astonishment, but with a sort of righteous fury, and he waved a folded evening paper in his hand as if it were a writ.

“Holmes!” he cried, his voice sharp and accusing. “I knew I should find you at the bottom of this! Meddling, always meddling! I told them at the Yard that if there was some devilry afoot that defied common sense, your hand would be in it somewhere!”

“My dear Lestrade, do calm yourself and have a chair,” said Holmes coolly, gesturing with his pipe-stem towards the armchair opposite. “To what do we owe the pleasure of this tempestuous visit? And might I suggest you lower your voice? You will alarm Mrs. Hudson.”

“Alarm her!” Lestrade scoffed, though he did lower his voice to a strained hiss. “It is I who am alarmed, Holmes! You have been questioning James Ryder, the head attendant from the Hotel Cosmopolitan. Do not deny it; my man saw him leaving this very address not an hour ago, looking like he’d seen a ghost.”

“I do not deny it,” Holmes replied placidly. “He was indeed here. He proved most helpful in resolving a small matter concerning a Christmas goose.”

Lestrade threw his hands up in exasperation. “A goose! Is that what you call it? Holmes, you have interfered with a capital investigation! We had our case against the plumber, Horner, all but sealed. Now, thanks to you, it is in ruins. And for what? To let the true culprit slip through your fingers!”

“What makes you so certain he has slipped anywhere?” asked Holmes, his eyes glinting with interest.

“Because he is dead, Holmes! That’s what!” Lestrade snapped, his moment of triumph soured by the circumstances. “James Ryder was found not twenty minutes ago in a back alley off the Strand. His head was bashed in with a life-preserver or some such instrument. Murdered, Holmes! And the man you let walk out of your door is now lying on a slab at the morgue.”

I confess that a chill went down my spine, and I shot a look at my friend. His decision, which had seemed so magnanimous and filled with the spirit of Christmas, now appeared to be a catastrophic miscalculation. “Holmes!” I murmured, a note of reproach in my voice.

He, however, appeared utterly unmoved by the news, save for a quickening of his attention. His languor vanished in an instant, and he leaned forward, his grey eyes bright and sharp as steel points.

“Murdered, you say? How very interesting,” he said, as if commenting upon a curious chemical reaction. “And the jewel, Lestrade? I presume you did not find the Blue Carbuncle upon his person?”

Lestrade stared. “How in blazes could you know that? No, we did not. Which proves my theory. Ryder had an accomplice, someone he was to meet to divide the spoils. The accomplice decided he wanted all of the spoils for himself, lay in wait for Ryder, and did away with him. A simple, brutal case of greed. And it would never have happened if you had not taken the law into your own hands!”

“Perhaps,” said Holmes, rising to his feet and beginning to pace the hearth-rug. “Or perhaps it would have happened in Paris, or New York, or wherever this poor wretch managed to flee. The outcome for Ryder would be the same; only the geography would differ. Tell me everything, Lestrade. The precise location of the alley? What was found upon the body? The time of death, as near as the police surgeon can guess?”

“It was in Falcon Court, a narrow passage behind the Vaudeville Theatre,” the detective recited, mollified somewhat by Holmes’s sudden professional intensity. “The surgeon puts the time of death within the last hour. As for his possessions, his pockets were turned out. Empty, save for a few shillings, a handkerchief, and this.” He drew from his own pocket a small, pathetic object and held it out. It was a little wooden bird, crudely carved from a piece of pine, with a loop of twine around its neck. “Meaningless, I should say. Probably a trinket for a child.”

Holmes took the object and examined it closely, turning it over and over under the gas-light. “You see, but you do not observe, Lestrade,” he murmured, his familiar refrain. “This is not a child’s toy. The wood is unvarnished, the carving hasty. It is a token, a pass-key of a sort. It is the most significant clue you have.”

“A clue to what?” Lestrade asked, bewildered. “It’s a scrap of wood.”

“It is a clue to where Ryder was going,” Holmes said, his voice dropping into its low, resonant, lecturing tone. “He was not fleeing the country, Lestrade, not yet. He had one last stop to make. He left my rooms in a state of terror and relief. He believed he had a second chance. But he still had a loose end to tie up; he had the jewel, but he also had an accomplice who knew he had it. Your theory is correct in that respect, but your conclusion is flawed. He was not going to divide the spoils. He was going to secure his escape.”

He turned to me, his face alight with the thrill of the chase. “Watson, this is no longer a time for violins and contemplation. The game is afoot once more! Fetch your hat and your stoutest walking-stick. Lestrade, you may accompany us if you wish to see how this 'simple case of greed' unravels. I fancy it is a trifle more complex than you imagine.”

Within minutes, the three of us were descending the seventeen steps to the street and plunging into the thick, swirling fog. It was a raw, damp night, the gas-lamps casting fuzzy halos of light that did little to penetrate the gloom. A hansom cab rattled past, its lamps like the eyes of some nocturnal beast.

“Where are we bound, Holmes?” I asked, pulling my collar up against the chill.

“We are in search of a man who carves birds,” he answered, striding purposefully eastward. “A man who deals in more than just woodwork. This carving is of a specific style, Watson. Notice the deep, angular cuts on the wings, and this peculiar notch at the base of the tail. It is not English work. It has the flavour of the Baltic ports, of Riga or Danzig. Ryder was an attendant at a grand hotel; he would have encountered all manner of men. He needed passage on a ship, passage that could be bought without questions being asked. He was on his way to arrange it when his past caught up with him.”

Our journey took us far from the respectable environs of Baker Street, into the labyrinthine alleys that cluster near the Thames. The fog grew thicker here, tasting of coal smoke and river mud. The sounds of the city became muffled, punctuated by the distant, mournful hoot of a ship’s horn.

At last, Holmes stopped before a dingy little shopfront, its windows so grimy as to be nearly opaque. Above the door, a barely legible sign read, “Erik Kristiansen, Curios & Carvings.” The air of decay was palpable.

“This should be the place,” Holmes muttered, and pushed open the door, which announced our arrival with the jangling of a rusty bell.

The interior was even more cluttered and dim than the outside suggested. It was a cavern of forgotten things: ship’s compasses, dusty sextants, scrimshaw, and everywhere, on every available surface, were small wooden carvings of animals, ships, and gnomes. The air was thick with the scent of pine-wood, turpentine, and something else, something acrid and unpleasant.

From the shadows at the back of the shop, a figure emerged. He was a stooped, elderly man with a great shock of white hair and pale blue eyes that seemed to have been washed out by a lifetime of staring at the sea.

“What do you want?” he asked, his voice a gravelly rumble with a thick Nordic accent.

Holmes stepped forward into the narrow pool of light cast by a single oil-lamp. He held up the small wooden bird. “We are here about this,” he said simply.

The old man’s eyes widened for a fraction of a second before his face settled back into a mask of cautious indifference. “I make many birds. I sell many birds. It is nothing to me.”

“Many men come here. Many men have fear in their eyes. This is not a place for happy men,” the carver replied, turning away as if to end the conversation.

Her composure shattered. She gave a single, guttural cry of fury and despair, and lunged, not at Holmes, but at the window, as if she might throw herself out into the abyss of the city. But the constables were too quick for her. They seized her, and her struggle was brief and futile.
