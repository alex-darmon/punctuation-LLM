Near a line of trees at the edge of the field sat a small, sleek looking monoplane painted in a drab camouflage pattern. It was a liaison plane, built for speed, not for a fight. Standing beside it, polishing a pair of goggles with a silk handkerchief, was a man who could only be their pilot.

He was tall and lanky, with a long, drooping mustache that gave his face a perpetually mournful look. He wore a stained leather flying jacket and breeches, but no hat, and his dark hair was whipped about by the wind. He watched them approach with an air of detached amusement.

"Ah," he said as they came to a halt. His voice was a dry, raspy baritone with a thick French accent. "The special delivery packages. I am Captain du Bois." He did not offer to shake hands. Instead, he finished polishing his goggles and fitted them over his eyes. "The General tells me you must go to England. A very popular destination these days."

"Yes, sir," Dave said. "We have to deliver a message to the British."

Captain du Bois grunted and patted the fuselage of his plane. "And I am the postman. A very dangerous job, being a postman in this war. The competition, they shoot at you." He gave them a thin, humorless smile. "I hope you are not prone to airsickness. It will be a bumpy ride."

"We'll be all right," Freddy said, his chin jutting out.

The Captain's eyes, hidden behind the dark glass of his goggles, seemed to study them for a long moment. "Brave boys," he murmured, almost to himself. "The world is full of brave boys today. Soon it will be full of dead ones." He shook himself, as if casting off a dark thought. "Enough talk! The Boche will not wait for us. Get in. You," he pointed a long finger at Freddy. "You are smaller. You will sit behind me. You," he pointed at Dave, "you will sit next to me. Do not touch anything. Especially not the pretty red levers. They make things fall off. Now, in!"

They clambered into the cramped cockpit. It smelled of oil and gasoline and something else, the faint, metallic scent of fear. The space was tight, with Freddy squeezed in a small seat behind the pilot's. Dave settled into the co-pilot's seat, his knees practically touching the instrument panel. It was a maze of dials and switches, far more complex than the simple controls of the training plane they had stolen.

Du Bois swung himself into his seat with practiced ease. He buckled himself in, his hands moving with a swift, sure confidence. He flipped a series of switches on the panel. "We fly on the deck, you see!" he shouted over his shoulder to Freddy. "Right on the treetops! It is harder for the Messerschmitts to see us. And if they do see us, it is harder for them to shoot us. Du Bois gave a thumbs up to a mechanic standing near the wingtip, who pulled the wheel chocks away. The captain eased the throttle forward, and the little plane began to roll across the bumpy pasture.

But at the last possible second, Du Bois pulled back on the stick, and the plane leaped into the air. It climbed steeply for a few hundred feet, just enough to clear the trees, and then Du Bois pushed the nose down sharply.

Dave's stomach lurched into his throat. The ground rushed up to meet them again. They leveled out so low that he could see individual leaves on the branches whipping past their wingtips. The world became a terrifying, high speed blur of green and brown.

"See?" Du Bois yelled, a wild grin splitting his face. "This is flying! Not like a goose in the sky, waiting to be plucked!"

Dave could only nod, his knuckles white as he gripped the sides of his seat. He risked a glance behind him. Freddy was pale, but he gave Dave a shaky grin and a thumbs up.

The landscape below was a panorama of chaos. They flashed over roads clogged with refugees, endless columns of men, women, and children trudging westward with carts and wheelbarrows piled high with their worldly possessions. They looked like ants fleeing a disturbed hill. Here and there were the dark, smoldering hulks of military trucks and civilian cars, strafed by German planes.

Then they saw the other side of the war. They crossed a wide, straight road, and Dave's heart leaped into his throat. It was choked with German military vehicles. Tanks, armored cars, and trucks packed with soldiers in field gray uniforms. A column of steel and men that stretched for miles, moving relentlessly forward. It was the tidal wave he had imagined, and it was even more terrible in reality.

A few soldiers looked up and pointed. A machine gun on an armored car began to chatter, and streams of tracer bullets stitched the air behind them, harmlessly out of range.

Du Bois laughed, a harsh, barking sound. "Too slow, you square heads! Too slow!"

He banked the plane sharply, following the contour of a small river valley. They flew so low that their shadow danced on the surface of the water. For twenty minutes they flew like this, a desperate, hedge hopping race across a dying country. The constant roar of the engine and the rush of the wind was hypnotic. Dave felt a strange sense of detachment, as though he were watching a film instead of living it.

Then Freddy's voice, sharp with alarm, crackled in his ears. "Up there! To the right!"

Dave looked up and his blood ran cold. High above them, circling like a bird of prey, was a single aircraft. Its distinctive gull wings and fixed landing gear were unmistakable. A Stuka. It was flying a lazy patrol, its pilot no doubt searching for targets of opportunity on the roads below.

"I see him," Du Bois said, his voice suddenly grim. The wild grin was gone. "He has not seen us. Not yet. We will stay in the valley. With luck, he will pass."

"Sacre bleu!" Du Bois swore. "He is looking for us! Somebody on the ground must have radioed our position!"

There was no more hiding. The Stuka pilot had clearly spotted them now. The dive bomber tilted and began to drop toward them, growing larger in their windscreen with terrifying speed.

"Hold on!" Du Bois yelled.

He didn't try to climb or outrun the German plane. Instead, he did the last thing Dave expected. He pushed the stick forward and dove even lower, aiming for a dense patch of forest ahead. The trees rushed toward them like a solid green wall. Dave braced himself for the inevitable, final impact.

But Du Bois was a master of his craft. He leveled out at what seemed like inches above the treetops, the propeller wash churning the leaves into a frenzy. The Stuka, a much heavier and less nimble aircraft, could not follow such a maneuver. Its pilot was forced to pull up to avoid crashing.

As the German plane roared overhead, its machine guns opened up. A stream of bullets ripped through the trees just behind them, sending splinters of wood flying. Dave heard a series of loud thumps from the rear of their own plane.

"He has hit us!" Freddy shouted.

"It is nothing!" Du Bois snapped back, his eyes darting from the sky to the trees ahead. "Just a few love taps! He is coming around for another pass!"

The Stuka was climbing, preparing for a second attack. But Du Bois had used the few seconds he had gained. He spotted a narrow firebreak cutting through the forest, a thin brown ribbon in the sea of green. He threw the plane into a stomach wrenching bank and dove for it. They shot down the narrow corridor, the wingtips seeming to scrape the trees on either side. It was like flying down a tunnel.

The Stuka pilot tried to follow, but the angle was too steep and the target too narrow. He overshot them, roaring past overhead. By the time he could circle back, Du Bois had already shot out of the far end of the forest and was skimming low over a series of rolling hills. He weaved the little plane between them, using the terrain as cover.

After five more minutes of this nerve wracking flight, Freddy's voice came again. "I think we lost him! I can't see him anymore!"

Du Bois grunted and eased back on the throttle slightly. He banked and climbed a few hundred feet, circling cautiously. The sky was empty.

"Good," the pilot said, the tension draining from his voice. "He has gone to find an easier target. A truck, perhaps. Or a school." He spat the last word out with venom. Then he glanced at his instrument panel. His face tightened. "But he left us a souvenir."

Dave followed his gaze. One of the fuel gauges was dropping at an alarming rate. A bullet must have punctured a fuel line in the wing tank.

"Are we hit bad?" Dave asked, his mouth suddenly dry.

"We are losing fuel," Du Bois stated flatly. "The main tank is fine, but the port wing tank is draining away. We will not have enough to reach England."

"What are we going to do?" Freddy asked from the back, his voice small.

Captain du Bois was silent for a moment, his eyes scanning the horizon ahead. To the west, the sky was beginning to turn a pale gray. The English Channel. It was close, but not close enough.

"We have one chance," the pilot said at last. "There is a French airfield near Dunkirk. A forward base. If we can make it there, we can refuel. It is our only hope." He looked at Dave, and even through the goggles, Dave could see the grim fatalism in his eyes. "But it is in the heart of the fighting. The Germans may already be there. It is a gamble."

"We don't have a choice," Dave said.

"No," Du Bois agreed. "We do not."

He pushed the nose of the plane down again, and once more they were racing just above the shattered landscape of northern France. The signs of battle were everywhere now. The smoke was thicker, the sound of explosions more frequent. They passed over hastily dug trench lines and abandoned artillery positions. The ground was pockmarked with shell craters, so many that it looked like the surface of the moon.

The little plane's engine began to sputter. Du Bois switched over to the main tank, and the engine caught again, running smoothly for now. But the gauge for that tank was already worryingly low.

"Not much farther," the pilot muttered, his knuckles white on the control stick. "Not much farther."

Ahead, through the haze, Dave could just make out the flat expanse of an airfield. But something was wrong. There was no activity. No planes taking off or landing. No men scurrying about. A pall of black smoke hung over one of the hangars at the far end of the field.

"It looks deserted," Freddy said.

"It is too quiet," Du Bois said, his voice tight. "This is a trap."

Even as he spoke, flashes of light erupted from the woods bordering the airfield. The air around them was suddenly filled with the angry buzz of machine gun bullets. A sound like a fist punching tin foil came from the wing beside Dave, and he saw a neat row of holes appear in the metal skin.

"Germans!" Du Bois yelled. "They have already taken the field! Hold on!"

He jammed the throttle to the firewall. The engine screamed in protest, but the little plane surged forward. Instead of turning away, Du Bois flew straight for the airfield, staying as low as he could. Bullets from the ground zipped all around them. The windscreen suddenly starred with a spiderweb of cracks as a round struck it, but miraculously did not shatter it completely.

They flashed across the airfield in a matter of seconds. Dave caught a glimpse of German soldiers in the trees, and the snout of a Panzer tank poking out from behind the burning hangar. Then they were past, and Du Bois was throwing the plane into a series of violent, evasive turns.

"The engine!" Freddy screamed.

The steady roar had changed to a ragged, coughing sputter. Black smoke was now pouring from the cowling. One of the rounds from the ground had found its mark. The engine coughed one last time, then died completely.

The silence was terrifying. All Dave could hear was the rush of the wind over the wings. They were a glider now, a wounded bird falling from the sky.

"We are going down!" Du Bois shouted, his voice strangely calm. He was fighting the controls, trying to keep the plane level. "There is a beach ahead! I will try to put us down there!"

Through the cracked windscreen, Dave could see it. A wide stretch of pale sand, and beyond it, the gray, choppy waters of the English Channel. They were so close. The plane was losing altitude fast. The sand rushed up at them.

"Brace yourselves!" Du Bois commanded.

"Well," Captain du Bois said, pulling off his cracked goggles and wiping his face. "The landing was not my best. But, any landing you can walk away from, eh?" He looked back toward the airfield, then down the long, empty stretch of beach. "This is Dunkirk."

Their mission was not over. It had just landed them in the biggest trap of all.

"Yes, sir," Freddy said, snapping to a stiff, if rumpled, attention.
"Tell me what you saw," the General commanded, his voice raspy with fatigue.
Once again, Freddy launched into their story, his French fluent and precise. He recounted their escape, the stolen plane, and the map they had seen in the German command post. When he finished, the General and the other officers were silent for a long moment.
The General then unrolled a fresh chart on the table. "Show me," he said simply.
Dave stepped forward. His French was clumsy, but the language of maps was universal. With a steady finger, he began to point out the locations of the German armored divisions, the supply depots, the pontoon bridges he had committed to memory. He traced the route of the main panzer thrust, a deadly arrow aimed straight at the coast, designed to cut the Allied armies in two.
As he pointed, a low murmur went through the room. The officers exchanged grim looks. An air of cold, hard reality seemed to settle over them. The information from these two boys was confirming the scattered and confusing reports from the front. The picture it painted was one of impending disaster.
"They are moving faster than we believed possible," the General said, his voice barely a whisper. He straightened up and his eyes, though tired, held a spark of decision. "This changes everything. This information must get to Lord Gort and General Blanchard immediately."
He barked a series of orders. A communications officer rushed to a radio set. Another officer began marking the new enemy positions on the main map with red pins. Dave and Freddy found themselves suddenly at the center of a whirlwind of activity, their firsthand intelligence acting as a catalyst.
"You have done the Allies a great service today, young men," the General said, placing a hand on each of their shoulders. "A very great service. But you are not safe here. The enemy is closing in. I have arranged for you to be evacuated. You will be taken to the coast, to Dunkirk. From there, you will get a ship to England. You must deliver this information in person to the War Office in London."
"Dunkirk, sir?" Freddy asked, his brow furrowed. "Is it still open?"
The General's face was grim. "For now. The British are holding a perimeter. The Royal Navy is attempting to take men off the beaches. It is our only hope. Now go. A Major du Bois will escort you."
A stout, mustached officer stepped forward and saluted. "This way, gentlemen. We must not delay."
They were hustled out of the chateau and into another staff car. As they sped away, Dave looked back at the chateau, now a hive of desperate, last minute planning. He felt a surge of pride that they had been able to help, but it was quickly overshadowed by the chilling finality of the General's words. It is our only hope.
The drive to Dunkirk was even worse than the one before. The roads were a tangled mess of military and civilian traffic. The sky was a constant source of terror. The Luftwaffe seemed to own the air, strafing the roads at will. More than once, Major du Bois had to expertly swerve the car off the road to avoid a column of burning trucks. Smoke from distant fires stained the horizon, and the sound of artillery grew into a continuous, deafening roar.
"They are shelling the perimeter," the Major shouted over the noise. "We are getting close."
And then they saw it. The outskirts of Dunkirk were a landscape from a madman's dream. Buildings were shattered skeletons, fires burned unchecked, and a thick pall of oily black smoke hung over everything, turning the afternoon sun into a sickly orange disk. The closer they got to the coast, the more men they saw. Not in neat columns, but in vast, sprawling masses, covering the dunes and the beaches, all facing the sea. An entire army, trapped and waiting.
Major du Bois navigated the cratered streets with grim skill, finally bringing the car to a screeching halt near the waterfront. "This is as far as I can go," he yelled. "We must proceed on foot. To the East Mole! That is our best chance!"
They scrambled out of the car and were immediately engulfed by the chaos. The noise was overwhelming. The crash of shells, the rattle of distant machine guns, the drone of aircraft, and the deep, concussive whump of bombs exploding in the water. They followed the Major, pushing their way through the throngs of exhausted soldiers.
The East Mole was a long, narrow wooden breakwater stretching over a mile out into the sea. It was their pier, their gangplank to salvation. A long, orderly queue of thousands of men snaked its way along the mole, waiting patiently to board the ships that came alongside. Destroyers, minesweepers, passenger ferries, and a motley collection of smaller vessels, from fishing trawlers to private yachts, were nosing up to the wooden pilings, taking on as many men as they could hold. These were the famous little ships, come to pluck an army from the jaws of defeat.
As they hurried down the long stretch of the mole, another air raid siren began to wail from the town. Far out at sea, the guns of the destroyers began to bark, throwing up puffs of black smoke into the gray sky.
"They're coming again!" Freddy shouted over the din.
"Keep moving!" the Major yelled. "The ship's guns will keep them busy!"
They broke into a run, dodging around a line of walking wounded. The mole seemed to stretch on forever. Dave could see the destroyer now, HMS Wakeful, her sleek gray hull a symbol of safety and power. A gangplank was down, and a steady stream of soldiers was clambering aboard.
They reached the gangplank just as the Stukas began their dives. This time, their targets were the ships. The noise was deafening. The destroyer's pom-pom guns opened up with a furious, hammering roar, spitting streams of tracers into the sky. Bombs fell all around them, sending up massive plumes of water that drenched the mole and everyone on it. A small fishing boat just a few hundred yards away took a direct hit and vanished in a flash of orange flame and black smoke.
"Go, go, go!" the Major screamed, shoving them toward the gangplank.
Dave, Freddy, and du Bois scrambled up the ramp and onto the steel deck of the destroyer. They were immediately swallowed up by the crowd of soldiers already on board. The Major was right behind them. He grabbed a young naval lieutenant by the arm. "Take these three to the bridge! The Admiral is expecting them! It's a Gort-level priority!"
The lieutenant's eyes widened. He nodded and gestured for them to follow. "This way, sirs!"
As they were hustled toward the ship's superstructure, Dave chanced a look back. The beach was wreathed in smoke from the bombing. The long, patient lines were still there, a testament to a courage he could barely comprehend. The town of Dunkirk was burning. The sky was a crisscross of shell bursts and diving planes. The ship's engines thrummed beneath his feet, and he felt a lurch as the destroyer began to pull away from the mole. They were safe. They had made it. But as the ship turned and gathered speed, heading for the open sea and the white cliffs of England, Dave felt no sense of victory. He looked at the thousands upon thousands of men still standing on that strip of sand, waiting under a sky full of enemies. Their mission was not over. It had just landed them in the biggest rescue in history, and they were only the first load out. He felt very tired, and old, and for the first time since their wild escape began, he felt the true, crushing weight of the war.

The naval lieutenant, a young man whose face was smudged with cordite but whose eyes were clear and alert, pushed a path for them through the packed ranks of soldiers. "Mind your heads, sirs. Watch the lines." The deck was a tangle of ropes, hoses, and ammunition boxes, made treacherous by the exhausted bodies of men slumped against every available surface. The air was thick with the smell of salt, sweat, and hot oil.
"Quite a full house," Freddy muttered, squeezing past a burly sergeant who was field stripping a Bren gun.
"We're taking on nearly a thousand this trip," the lieutenant shouted over the din of the engines and the distant crump of bombs. "She's designed for three hundred."
Dave looked around at the faces of the soldiers. They were caked with dirt and stubble, their uniforms torn, their eyes hollow with a fatigue so deep it seemed to have settled in their very bones. Yet as the ship pulled further from the coast, he saw small signs of life returning. A few men were talking quietly, another was sharing a piece of hardtack with his mate, and one soldier was even attempting to clean his rifle with a rag. They were beaten, but they were not broken.
Suddenly, a new sound cut through the air, a high pitched, menacing whine that was different from the Stukas. "E-boats!" a lookout screamed from a platform above them. "Two points off the starboard bow!"
An alarm bell clanged furiously. "All hands to action stations! Repel torpedo attack!" The ship's intercom blared. The sailors around them who had been assisting with the evacuation now moved with a swift, practiced urgency, manning the smaller deck guns and torpedo tubes.
"Get down!" the lieutenant yelled, shoving Dave and Freddy toward the bulkhead of the superstructure.
Dave flattened himself against the cold steel, his heart pounding against his ribs. He peered around the edge of the doorway. He could see them now, two long, sleek shapes cutting through the choppy waves at incredible speed, leaving white wakes behind them. They looked like gray predators of the sea.
"They're in range!" someone shouted.
The destroyer's forward four inch gun roared, a deafening blast that shook the entire ship. A huge splash erupted in the water, just short of the lead E-boat. The German boats did not slow down. They zig-zagged wildly, making them difficult targets. Puffs of white smoke appeared from their decks.
"Torpedoes in the water!" the lookout shrieked. "Two of them! Starboard side!"
"Hard to port!" came a calm, authoritative voice from the ship's bridge, amplified by a loudspeaker. The destroyer heeled over sharply, its deck tilting at a crazy angle. Dave had to grab onto a railing to keep from sliding across the deck. He saw two thin white lines racing through the water, straight for them. For a heart stopping second, it looked as though they would hit amidships. Then, with agonizing slowness, the ship's turn took effect. The first torpedo shot past their stern, so close Dave felt he could have reached out and touched it. The second one passed a few dozen yards off their bow.
"They missed!" Freddy breathed, a grin of pure relief spreading across his face.
But the fight was not over. The pom-pom guns, silent during the high altitude attacks of the Stukas, now opened up with their characteristic hammering roar, spitting a hail of explosive shells at the nearest E-boat. Tracers lit up the gray afternoon, converging on the small craft. Dave saw flashes and sparks erupt all over the German boat's hull. It swerved violently, then seemed to slow. The destroyer's main gun fired again. This time the shell was a direct hit. The E-boat disappeared in a blinding flash of orange and a geyser of black smoke and splintered wood.
"One down!" a gunner near Dave yelled with savage satisfaction.
The second E-boat, seeing its companion destroyed and now facing the full fury of the destroyer's guns, turned sharply and sped away, disappearing into the haze.
A collective cheer went up from the soldiers and sailors on the deck. The tension broke as quickly as it had built. The lieutenant wiped a grimy hand across his forehead. "That was too close," he said. "Nasty pieces of work, those E-boats. Now, let's get you to the Admiral before something else tries to sink us."
He led them up a steep metal ladder to the ship's bridge. The contrast with the chaotic deck below was stark. Here, all was calm, quiet, and professional. Helmsmen stood at the wheel, their eyes fixed on the compass. A navigator bent over a chart table under a hooded lamp. And in the center of it all, staring out through the armored glass at the sea ahead, stood a tall, lean man in a crisp naval uniform. His face was etched with lines of exhaustion, but his posture was ramrod straight. This was Vice Admiral Ramsay, the man in charge of Operation Dynamo.
"Sirs," the lieutenant said, saluting crisply. "The three men with the Gort-level priority."
The Admiral turned, and his piercing blue eyes fixed on them. They were the eyes of a man carrying an impossible burden. "Major du Bois. And you must be the two lads who came out of Germany with a stolen airplane. I've had a signal about you. Tell me your story."
Major du Bois spoke first, giving a quick, formal report of their journey from the Belgian G.H.Q. to the mole. Then Freddy, his voice steady despite their ordeal, recounted the information about the German map. The Admiral listened intently, his expression unreadable. When Freddy finished, the Admiral walked over to the chart table.
"Show me," he said, his voice quiet but commanding.
The navigator moved aside, and the Admiral spread a large scale chart of Northern France and Belgium on the table. "Point out what you saw, son. Exactly as you remember it."
Dave, his confidence bolstered by the Admiral's calm demeanor, stepped forward. He put his finger on the chart. "Here, sir," he began, his voice surprisingly firm. "At this crossroads near Gembloux. A full panzer division, refitting. And another one here, moving southwest." He moved his finger across the map, tracing the lines of advance, pointing out the supply dumps, the headquarters, the river crossings. He and Freddy worked together, one remembering a name, the other a location, piecing together the mosaic of German strategy they had glimpsed.
The Admiral and the other officers on the bridge watched in silence. As Dave's finger finally stopped near Abbeville on the coast, the Admiral drew a long, slow breath.
"It's the pincer," he said to his captain. "Just as we feared, but faster. They're not just trying to trap the B.E.F., they're trying to annihilate it." He looked at Dave and Freddy. "Your information confirms that the window to get these men out is closing far more rapidly than London believes. You two may have just bought us a few more precious hours."
He turned to his communications officer. "Signal the Admiralty. Top secret, top priority. New intelligence confirms speed of enemy southern pincer. Evacuation must be accelerated by all possible means. Repeat, all possible means."
The officer nodded and hurried to his station. The Admiral turned back to Dave and Freddy. A flicker of something that might have been a smile touched his tired lips. "You've had a long day, boys. I think you've earned a cup of tea and a patch of deck that isn't being shelled. Lieutenant, find these men a place to rest. And see they get some food. Real food."
"Aye, aye, sir," the lieutenant said.
As they were led from the bridge, Dave felt a profound sense of relief. Their wild, desperate journey had meant something. The information had been delivered. Now, all they had to do was get home. He looked out at the sea. The gray shores of France were sinking below the horizon, and ahead, barely visible through the mist, he thought he could just make out the faint, white line of the cliffs of Dover.

The rest of the crossing passed without incident. The German air force, it seemed, was concentrating its fury on the beaches they had left behind. HMS Wakeful, now part of a loose convoy of returning ships, steamed at full speed for England. Dave and Freddy were given a space in a small officers' cabin, where they devoured a meal of corned beef sandwiches and hot, sweet tea that tasted like the finest banquet they had ever had. Exhaustion finally overtook them, and they fell into a deep, dreamless sleep on narrow bunks, lulled by the steady thrum of the destroyer's engines.
They were woken by a change in that sound. The engines were slowing, and they could hear the shouts of men and the clang of lines on the deck above. Freddy sat up, rubbing his eyes. "Are we there?"
Dave was already at the porthole. "We're here," he said, his voice filled with awe. They had arrived at Dover. But the port was not the calm, peaceful harbor he had imagined. It was as busy as Dunkirk, but with a different kind of energy. An energy of welcome and relief, not of desperation. Tugs nudged the big ships into place. The quays were crowded with nurses in starched white aprons, Women's Voluntary Service ladies with steaming urns of tea, and an army of officials trying to bring order to the chaotic arrival of thousands upon thousands of men.
Their ship docked, and the long process of disembarkation began. The walking wounded went first, then the rest of the men, shuffling down the gangplanks onto the solid ground of England. As Dave and Freddy waited their turn, the same naval lieutenant who had guided them before found them in the crowd.
"The Admiral sends his compliments," he said. "And instructions. You're not to get lost in the shuffle. There's someone waiting for you."
He led them to the gangplank, and they were given priority passage. As they stepped onto the quay, a military policeman saluted and directed them toward a black staff car parked away from the main bustle. Standing beside it was a man who seemed a world away from the dirt and grime of the evacuation. He was tall and impeccably dressed in the uniform of an RAF Group Captain. His shoes were polished to a mirror shine, and his officer's cap sat at a precise, regulation angle.
"Mr. Miller? Mr. Armstrong?" the Group Captain asked. His voice was crisp and upper class, without a trace of warmth.
"Yes, sir," Freddy said.
"I am Group Captain Mallory," the officer stated. "I've been sent to collect you. Get in the car."
They climbed into the back of the car, which was as clean and orderly inside as Mallory was on the outside. The driver, an equally neat and tidy airman, closed the door, got in, and pulled smoothly away from the docks. They drove in silence through the streets of Dover, then out into the green countryside of Kent. The peaceful, rolling hills and tidy cottages were such a stark contrast to the burning hell of Northern France that Dave felt a sense of disorientation, as though he had traveled to another planet.
"Your information was received by the War Office an hour ago," Mallory said abruptly, without turning around. "It has caused, shall we say, a considerable stir."
"We just told them what we saw, sir," Dave said.
"Quite," Mallory said dryly. "We will be going to RAF Kenley. You will be given a proper debriefing. You will tell my people everything you saw, everything you heard, and everything you can remember from the moment you were captured until you stepped onto that destroyer. Is that clear?"
"Yes, sir," they both replied.
They drove for another hour, the silence in the car broken only by the hum of the engine. They arrived at a sprawling airbase. Rows of Hawker Hurricanes and Supermarine Spitfires were lined up on the grass, their engines being worked on by crews of mechanics. The air buzzed with the familiar, comforting sound of powerful aero engines being tested. It was the sound of defiance.
They were taken to a small, secure building at the edge of the field and shown into a sparse office. Group Captain Mallory sat behind a large desk, and two other officers, one from the RAF and one in a civilian suit who had the unmistakable look of an intelligence man, sat to the side with notebooks.
For the next three hours, they were questioned. The questions were sharp, precise, and relentless. Mallory led the interrogation, drilling down into every detail. What was the exact insignia on the German tanks? What type of anti-aircraft guns were protecting the bridges? What was the rank of the officer in charge of the command post? What was the mood of the German soldiers they saw?
Dave and Freddy answered as best they could, their memories stimulated by the sharp questioning. They drew maps, sketched aircraft markings, and tried to recall snippets of conversations they had overheard. It was mentally exhausting, but they understood the importance of it. When it was finally over, Mallory sent the other two men away. He leaned back in his chair and steepled his fingers, studying Dave and Freddy for a long moment.
"You've done well," he said, and for the first time, there was a hint of approval in his voice. "Exceptionally well. Your report from inside a German Corps H.Q. is the most valuable piece of human intelligence we've received since this affair began."
He paused, his eyes glinting. "Which brings us to the question of what to do with you now."
"I'd like to get back to my squadron, sir," Freddy said eagerly. "Or what's left of it."
Mallory shook his head. "Your squadron was wiped out at Lille. As far as the RAF is concerned, Pilot Officer Frederick Armstrong is officially listed as 'missing, believed killed in action'. It would be administratively inconvenient to bring you back from the dead just to send you into the regular pilot pool."
He then turned his gaze to Dave. "And you, Mr. Miller. You are an American citizen. A civilian. Technically, you should be handed over to your embassy and sent home on the next boat. We are, I must remind you, not yet allies in this conflict."
Dave's heart sank. After everything they had been through, was he just going to be sent home? "Sir," he said, leaning forward. "I'm a pilot. A good one. I was flying for the Finns against the Russians. I can't just go home and sit this out. There must be something I can do."

"This is Professor Dubois," Jeanine answered. "And his daughter, Elise. The Professor was working at the university in Liège. On… special projects. The Germans were very keen to have him continue his work for them. We have been hiding him since the invasion."

"What kind of projects?" Dave asked.

A dry cough came from the back. "Projects involving the theoretical application of gyroscopic stabilization to rocket propelled projectiles," the Professor said, his voice stronger now. "They believe my work can solve the accuracy problems with their new long range weapons."

Dave and Freddy exchanged a look in the dim light of the dashboard. This was bigger than just getting a couple of downed airmen out of the country. This was vital.

"Good grief," Freddy murmured. "No wonder they were tearing the town apart for you."

"They do not know we are gone yet," Elise's voice cut in, sharp and clear. "They think we are still hiding in the cellar. But they will find the tunnel soon. And then they will hunt us with everything they have."

"She is right," the Professor added. "They will not let me go easily. The information in my head is… valuable."

Dave's jaw was set. "Then we've got to get you to England. All of you."

They drove on in silence for another ten miles, the French countryside rolling past them in monochrome shades of grey and black. The initial adrenaline of the escape was wearing off, replaced by a bone deep weariness. The van was performing better than Dave had any right to expect, but he kept his ear tuned to every clatter and knock from the engine.

"Roadblock ahead," Freddy said suddenly, pointing.

Dave squinted. Far down the straight stretch of road, a single red lamp glowed. As they drew closer, they could make out the shape of a striped barrier pole across the road, and the silhouettes of several steel helmeted soldiers.

"Pull over," Elise said urgently from the back. "Turn off the lights."

Dave swung the van onto the muddy shoulder of the road and killed the engine and the lights. The sudden silence was deafening, broken only by the chirping of crickets. The red lamp of the roadblock seemed to burn with a malevolent intensity in the darkness.

"We can't go back," Dave said in a low voice. "They'll be looking for a van just like this. We can't go forward. What do we do?"

"There must be a way around," Freddy whispered, peering out the window. "A farm track, maybe?"

"No," Elise said firmly. "They will have the side roads watched. We must go through."

"Go through?" Dave stared back into the darkness of the van. "Are you crazy? We have no papers. I'm an American, Freddy's English. Your father is the most wanted man in Belgium right now! They'll shoot us on the spot!"

"Let me speak to them," she said, her voice devoid of any fear. "I speak German fluently. My mother was from Berlin. They will be looking for fugitives, for soldiers. They will not be looking for a girl taking her sick father to a specialist in Amiens. It is a risk, but it is a better risk than turning back or trying to run."

Dave hesitated. It was a wild plan. It was insane. But she was right. Going back was a death sentence. Hiding in a ditch and waiting for morning was no better.

"All right," he said finally. "But the moment it looks like it's going wrong, I'm putting my foot down and we're crashing that barrier. Got it?"

"Understood," Elise replied calmly. "Jeanine, you and the Professor lie down on the floor. Cover yourselves with those sacks. Be silent. My friend," she said, her voice now directed at Freddy, "you will have to hide as well. There is no explaining you."

Freddy didn't argue. He squeezed himself over the seat and into the back, disappearing into the darkness. "Good luck," came his muffled voice.

"Okay," Dave breathed, his heart starting to pound again. "What's my story?"

"You are my fiancé," Elise said simply. "You do not speak much German. You are worried about my father. Look anxious. It should not be difficult." She moved forward, squeezing between the front seats until she was sitting beside Dave. In the faint moonlight, he could see her face was pale but composed. She had undone the top buttons of her blouse and mussed her hair. She looked tired and frantic.

"Ready?" she asked.

Dave just nodded, his throat too dry to speak. He started the engine, turned on the lights, and drove slowly toward the checkpoint.

A German soldier stepped out into the beam of the headlights, holding up a hand. Dave brought the van to a halt a few feet from the barrier. The soldier, a young corporal with a weary face, ambled over to the driver's side window. Another soldier with a submachine gun stood warily a few feet away.

"Your papers," the corporal said in German, his voice flat.

Before Dave could do more than stare blankly, Elise leaned across him, her expression a perfect blend of desperation and impatience. "Officer, please, we must get through!" she said in flawless, unaccented German. "My father is terribly ill. He is a senior administrator with the Kommandantur in Brussels, an important man! He has had a seizure. We are taking him to the specialist at the military hospital in Amiens. Doctor Schmidt. You must let us pass!"

The corporal was taken aback by the torrent of words. He shone his flashlight into the van, the beam sweeping over Elise's distraught face and then onto Dave. "And who is this?" he asked, his eyes narrowing.

"This is my fiancé, Franz," Elise snapped. "He is a mechanic from Alsace. He speaks very little German, only the dialect. He is a good driver, that is all. We are losing precious time!"

The corporal seemed to consider this. The story was plausible. An important official, a frantic daughter. It had the ring of panicked truth. He lowered his flashlight slightly. "You have papers for the vehicle? A travel permit?"

"Of course not, you fool!" Elise cried, her voice rising with convincing hysteria. "We did not have time to get papers! It was an emergency! Do you want to be the one who explains to the Gestapo why an important man died because you were checking paperwork for a baker's van? His name is Herr Müller! Check with your superiors if you must, but do it quickly!"

The mention of the Gestapo made the corporal flinch. He looked uncertainly back at the small guard hut beside the barrier. An older sergeant was now standing in the doorway, watching them. The corporal gestured for him to come over.

Dave's hands were sweating on the wheel. He could feel the tension coiling in his stomach like a snake. This was it. If the sergeant was smarter than the corporal, they were finished.

The sergeant, a grizzled veteran with a sour expression, walked slowly to the van and peered in. His gaze was sharp and missed nothing. He looked at Elise, then at Dave, then he shone his light into the back of the van. The beam passed over the jumble of dirty burlap sacks on the floor. One of them moved slightly.

Dave's blood ran cold. He saw the sergeant's eyes fix on the spot.

"What is under the sacks?" the sergeant asked, his voice a low growl.

Elise didn't miss a beat. "Our luggage! And some blankets for my father! Must we unpack the entire van for you while he lies dying? I demand you let us pass!"

The sergeant ignored her. "Get out," he said to Dave in broken French. "_Sortez._" He motioned with his thumb. Then he barked an order in German, and two more soldiers came jogging up, their rifles at the ready.

It was over. The bluff had failed. Dave's mind raced. He could try to ram the barrier, but the soldiers were ready for them. They'd be cut down in a hail of bullets before they got fifty feet.

He was just about to open his door when Elise did something extraordinary. She let out a heart-wrenching sob, buried her face in her hands, and began to cry, her whole body shaking. It wasn't the angry, demanding act from before. This was the sound of utter, hopeless despair.

"Please," she wept in German. "Please, I am begging you. He is my father. He is all I have. Please."

The raw, desperate grief in her voice seemed to affect the soldiers. The young corporal looked uncomfortable. Even the hard faced sergeant's expression softened for a fraction of a second. He hesitated.

And in that moment of hesitation, a sound began to build in the distance. A low, ominous drone that grew rapidly louder.

"Aircraft!" one of the soldiers shouted, pointing to the sky.

Everyone looked up. A lone, twin engined plane was flying low and fast, heading straight for them. It wasn't one of theirs. It was a British Blenheim bomber, lost or on a solitary night raid.

The sergeant shouted an order. "Take cover! Douse the lights!"

The roadblock was thrown into chaos. The red lamp was kicked over. The soldiers scrambled for the ditch by the side of the road. For a few seconds, everyone had forgotten about the baker's van.

"Dave, now!" Freddy's voice yelled from the back.

Dave needed no further encouragement. He slammed the van into gear and stomped on the accelerator. The van leaped forward, smashing straight through the flimsy wooden barrier pole. It splintered with a loud crack. He swerved to avoid the guard hut and roared back onto the road.

A machine gun opened up from the ditch, a frantic, angry chatter. Bullets hammered against the rear of the van, a sound like a fistful of gravel being thrown against a tin roof. One of the back windows shattered. But they were moving, picking up speed, a dark shape disappearing into the night.

The drone of the Blenheim grew to a deafening roar as it passed directly overhead, its engines thundering. Then it was gone, its sound fading into the distance as quickly as it had come. They were alone on the road again, the shattered roadblock already far behind them.

Dave drove like a madman for a solid ten minutes, not daring to slow down, expecting to see the headlights of a pursuing vehicle at any moment. But nothing appeared. The British plane had been their salvation.

Finally, he eased his foot off the gas, his entire body trembling with the aftershock of the escape. He pulled the van over into a thicket of trees, cutting the engine.

Silence descended again, thick and heavy.

"Is everyone all right?" he asked, his voice hoarse.

A series of affirmations came from the back.

"That was too close," Freddy said, his voice shaky. "I thought my heart was going to pound its way out of my chest."

Elise was no longer crying. She sat beside Dave, staring straight ahead, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. "Thank you," she said softly, not looking at him. "You were very brave."

"You were the brave one," Dave replied, meaning it. "I've never seen anything like it. You had them believing you."

"Not the sergeant," she said bitterly. "He knew. He saw the sack move." She shuddered. "It was Jeanine. I felt her shift."

From the back, Jeanine's voice was small. "I am sorry. A cramp in my leg."

"It does not matter now," the Professor said, his voice weak but steady. "We are through. But we cannot continue on the main roads. They will be looking for us everywhere now. They will have our description."

He was right. Their luck had run out. Dave got out of the van and leaned against its dented side, taking deep gulgasps of the cool night air. Freddy joined him, handing him a map they had taken from the bakery.

"The Professor is right," Freddy said, spreading the map on the hood of the van and shining a small pocket torch on it. "We're here," he said, pointing to a spot. "Amiens is this way. But the roads will be crawling with them. The coast… Dunkirk… is up here. To the northwest."

Dave studied the map. It was a tangle of red main roads and smaller, yellow secondary roads. Crisscrossing them all were faint white lines. Farm tracks. Footpaths.

"We have to get off the road," Dave said, tracing a line with his finger. "Go cross country. We'll use these small tracks where we can, but mostly we stick to the fields and woods. It'll be slow. It'll be miserable. But it's our only chance."

"Through the fields? In this?" Freddy gestured to the rickety van. "It'll get stuck in the first patch of mud."

"Then we walk," Dave said, his voice hard. "We have to get the Professor and his information to the Allies. That's all that matters now. We'll drive as far as we can, and then we walk the rest of the way to the coast." He looked up from the map, his eyes meeting Freddy's in the dim light. "It looks like our war isn't over yet, Freddy."

"No," Freddy agreed, a grim smile touching his lips. "It looks like it's just getting started."
