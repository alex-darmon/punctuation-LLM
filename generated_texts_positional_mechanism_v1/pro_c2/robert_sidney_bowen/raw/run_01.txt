For the next hour, they bounced and jolted across open fields and through small patches of woods. The ride was brutal, but they were making better time. The sounds of the battle for Gembloux slowly faded behind them, replaced by an unnerving quiet.

Finally, the Major brought the car to a halt in a grove of trees overlooking a small, peaceful-looking valley with a river running through it. "We rest here for a moment," he said, cutting the engine. "We must check the map and be sure of our direction."

He got out of the car and unfolded a map on the hood. Dave and Freddy climbed out, stretching their bruised and aching limbs. The air here was clean and fresh, filled with the scent of grass and wildflowers. It felt like a different planet from the one they had just left.

"According to the General, the British sector should begin just beyond this river, the Dyle," the Major said, tracing a line with his finger. "If we can find a bridge that is not yet blown, we can cross and find a British command post."

Freddy looked across the valley. "It looks so quiet," he said wistfully.

As if his words were a cue, a shape appeared in the sky to the east. A single, small plane.

"Get down!" Dave yelled, his instincts screaming. He grabbed Freddy and pulled him down behind the car. Major Dubois dove for cover beside them.

The plane was not a Stuka. It was smaller, a Fieseler Storch, a German reconnaissance plane. It flew low over the valley, its engine making a lazy popping sound. It circled once, twice, like a hawk spotting its prey.

"He has seen the car," Dubois whispered, his face tight. "He will report our position."

"Maybe not," Dave said, his eyes still glued to the plane. "He's just one guy. Maybe he's just looking for the front line."

The Storch completed its second circle and then, instead of flying off, it dipped its wings and began to descend, heading for a large, flat field on their side of the river.

"What is he doing?" Freddy breathed. "He's landing!"

"It is a trap," the Major said, his hand moving to the pistol at his hip. "He is trying to draw us out. He will have radioed for troops."

"No," Dave said slowly, his mind racing. "I don't think so. Look at him. He's landing like he owns the place. He's arrogant. He thinks there are no enemy soldiers for fifty miles." He looked at the Major, then at Freddy. A crazy, desperate idea was forming in his head. "And one thing I know about German reconnaissance pilots is that they carry the latest maps. The very latest."

The Major's eyes widened as he understood. "You cannot be serious! To attack a German pilot, just the three of us?"

"We don't have to attack him," Dave said, his voice low and urgent. "We just have to surprise him. He's one man. We're three. If we can get his plane, his maps... we could fly straight to British headquarters! It's faster than the car, and we'd have proof!"

Freddy's eyes lit up with the familiar fire. The exhaustion was gone, replaced by the thrill of the gamble. "He's right, Major! It's a mad idea, but it's a good one! Do we look like Germans? Of course not! But we could be! It's the only way!"

Major Dubois looked from the landing plane to the two wild-eyed boys. He saw the burning wreckage of Gembloux in his mind, heard the General's desperate voice. "Get them to General Gort." He was a soldier. He had his orders. This mad plan was a better chance than driving through a collapsing front.

He drew his pistol, the click of the safety being released unnaturally loud in the quiet grove.

"Alright," he said, his voice firm. "But we do it my way. We wait until he is out of the cockpit. Then we move. No shooting unless I give the order. We need him alive, if possible. Now, follow me, and do not make a sound."

I would feel honored to die for Belgium, but not for nothing! Not for a cause that is already lost!" He shook his head, a gesture of infinite weariness, and gestured for a soldier to come forward. "Corporal Henri will take you to the car. My orders from General Blanchard were to get you to the British, to General Gort. I will follow my orders. Perhaps your information can yet do some good. Go now. And may God go with you."

Major Dubois turned away without another word, his shoulders slumped as he walked back toward the frantic work in the woods. His duty was here, to stand and to die with his men. Dave and Freddy felt a lump rise in their throats. There were no more words to be said. They followed the silent Corporal Henri back through the trees, the sounds of digging and the clink of military hardware fading behind them.

The little Citroen was where they had left it, tucked under the lip of the wooded rise. Corporal Henri gave them a single, solemn nod, saluted, and then melted back into the woods, a ghost returning to his own grave. For a moment, the two boys and the Major simply stood by the car, the silence of the valley pressing in on them. They were alone again, three insignificant figures in a war that was swallowing nations whole.

"We must go," Major Dubois said finally, his voice raspy. "The road south is our only chance. We will drive toward Charleroi. The French Ninth Army should be there. From there, we can try to get across to the British sector."

Dave slid behind the wheel and started the engine. It coughed to life with a reassuring rumble. He glanced at the Major, whose face was a gray mask of strain. Freddy was huddled in the back, for once silent, his youthful face pinched with a sorrow that was too old for his years. Dave put the car in gear and eased it out from the cover of the trees, down the gentle slope and on to a narrow track that ran parallel to the river. He kept the speed down, his eyes scanning the road ahead and the sky above. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, every distant sound was the possible approach of a German patrol.

They drove for nearly an hour, the valley winding before them. The sounds of battle had receded to a constant, dull thunder behind them, but the signs of its passing were everywhere. A burned out farm, its stone walls blackened and gaping. A dead horse by the side of the road, already beginning to swell. And once, a column of refugees, a pitiful trail of men, women, and children with carts and bicycles, their faces blank with shock. Major Dubois had them pull over and hide in a copse of trees until the sad procession had passed. He did not want to answer their questions. He had no answers to give.

They were just starting to feel a sliver of hope, a fragile sense of having put some distance between themselves and the inferno, when Freddy's voice came sharp from the back seat.

"A plane! Look!"

Dave's head snapped up. His heart leaped into his throat. He saw it at once, a black cross against the pale blue sky. But it was not a bomber. It was small, with long, spindly landing gear and a high wing.

"Reconnaissance," Major Dubois said, his voice grim. "A Storch. He'll be spotting for their artillery, or looking for troop movements."

"He's seen us!" Dave grunted, pushing the little car as fast as it would go. The road was twisting, offering intermittent cover from trees, but the valley was wide here. They were horribly exposed. The plane banked lazily, easily keeping pace with them. It was like a cat toying with a mouse.

"He's not shooting," Freddy observed, his head craned out the window. "He's just watching us."

"He does not have to shoot," the Major explained, his knuckles white as he gripped the dashboard. "He has a radio. He is calling in our position to the nearest panzer column. We have maybe ten minutes, perhaps less."

An idea, born of sheer desperation, exploded in Dave's mind. "The river! If we can get across the river, we might have a chance. The main German thrust is on this side. The other side might be clearer!"

Ahead of them, a small stone bridge arched over the water. It looked ancient and sturdy. It was their only hope. Dave jammed his foot down on the accelerator. The little Citroen leaped forward, bouncing and rattling over the uneven road. The Storch followed, dropping lower now, its engine a menacing buzz. Dave could almost feel the German pilot's eyes on the back of his neck.

They were a hundred yards from the bridge. Fifty. Then, with a roar that shook the very air, the world erupted in fire and stone. An artillery shell, perfectly aimed, struck the far end of the bridge. For a heart-stopping second the ancient structure seemed to hang in the air, a silhouette of flying rock and dust, before it collapsed into the churning water below. Dave slammed on the brakes, skidding the car to a halt just yards from the newly made chasm. They were trapped. The Storch pilot, his work done, dipped his wings in a mocking salute and climbed away to the east.

"So," Major Dubois said into the ringing silence. "That is that." He slowly drew his service pistol and checked the magazine. "We will not be taken alive."

"Wait!" Dave said, his eyes darting around, refusing to accept defeat. The river was not wide, perhaps forty yards, but the current was swift. On the far side, the terrain was hilly and thick with woods. It was safety. "We can swim for it!"

"And the car? The information?" the Major countered, though the fire had gone out of his eyes.

"The information is in our heads!" Freddy piped up from the back. "What's more important, a car or getting the news to General Gort? Dave's right! We have to try!"

Before the Major could argue further, a new sound reached them. It was a low, grinding rumble, interspersed with a metallic clanking. It was coming from the direction they had just fled.

"Tanks," Dave said, his voice barely a whisper. "They're coming."

That decided it. There was no more time for debate. "Out of the car!" Major Dubois ordered. "Take nothing but yourselves! To the river bank!"

They tumbled out of the Citroen and scrambled down the steep, muddy bank to the water's edge. The rumble of the approaching armor was growing louder with every second. Dave looked at the swirling brown water, then back at the road. He could see the lead tank now, a Panzer II, its machine gun already traversing toward the abandoned car.

"We go on my count!" the Major yelled over the noise. "One! Two! Three! Go!"

They plunged into the shockingly cold water. The current grabbed Dave immediately, trying to pull him under and drag him downstream. He fought it, his arms and legs pumping with frantic energy. He was a strong swimmer, but the river was a powerful, living thing. A few yards to his right, he could see Freddy's head bobbing, his face a mask of fierce determination. Just ahead, Major Dubois was cutting through the water with clean, efficient strokes.

Machine gun fire suddenly ripped across the water behind them, kicking up angry spurts of spray. The Germans had seen them. Bullets whizzed past Dave's head with a sound like angry hornets. He ducked his head under the surface, holding his breath until his lungs burned, and swam blindly forward. When he came up for air, gasping, the far bank was closer. He could hear the Major shouting encouragement.

"Almost there! Keep going, boys! Keep going!"

Another burst of fire stitched the water, this time closer. Dave felt a sharp, stinging pain in his left shoulder, as though a hot poker had been jabbed into him. He cried out, his arm going numb. The current immediately took advantage, spinning him around. Panic seized him. He was losing strength, the cold seeping into his bones.

"Dave!" Freddy yelled, his voice filled with alarm. He had reached the shallows near the bank and turned back.

Just as Dave felt his head start to go under, a strong hand gripped the collar of his jacket. It was Major Dubois. The Frenchman's face was grim, his teeth clenched with effort. "I have you, my boy!" he grunted. "Do not fight! Just kick your legs!"

With the Major hauling him, and Freddy reaching out a hand from the bank, they dragged Dave out of the river's greedy grasp. He collapsed onto the muddy ground, gasping for breath, the world spinning around him. The machine gun on the far bank chattered one last time, the bullets thudding harmlessly into the earth bank above their heads, and then it stopped.

"They are not crossing," the Major panted, collapsing beside him. "They will not risk getting their tanks stuck in the mud. For now, we are safe."

Freddy was already ripping a strip of cloth from the bottom of his own shirt. "You're hit, Dave!" he said, his voice trembling slightly. "Let me see!"

Dave sat up, his head clearing. The pain in his shoulder was a dull, throbbing ache now. He looked down. A bullet had torn through the fleshy part of his shoulder, leaving a nasty looking furrow. It was bleeding freely, but it didn't seem to have hit the bone. "I'm okay," he mumbled, though he felt dizzy and weak. "It's just a graze."

"A very lucky graze," Major Dubois corrected him, examining the wound with a professional eye. "Another inch to the right and it would have shattered your clavicle." He helped Freddy fashion a crude but tight pressure bandage. "This will have to do. We must keep moving. We cannot stay here."

With the Major on one side and Freddy on the other, they helped Dave to his feet. He staggered, but his head was clearing. The adrenaline was pumping through him again, overriding the pain and exhaustion. They half carried, half dragged him up the steep bank and into the sanctuary of the woods.

They pushed deeper into the trees, putting as much distance as possible between themselves and the river. The woods were dense and quiet, a world away from the mechanized slaughter they had just escaped. After what felt like an eternity, Major Dubois called a halt. They had found a small, hidden hollow, screened by thick undergrowth.

"We will rest here," he announced, his breath coming in ragged gasps. He was not a young man, and the swim had taken its toll. "We must check our position and make a new plan."

While Dave rested his aching shoulder against the trunk of a large oak, the Major took out a small compass. Freddy, ever restless, explored the edge of their little sanctuary.

"The sun is to the west," the Major murmured, more to himself than to them. "The river runs roughly north to south here. So we must continue west. But on foot... it could take days to reach the British lines. And we have no food, no water."

The grim reality of their situation settled upon them. They had escaped, but they were wounded, exhausted, and lost in enemy-occupied territory. The information they carried seemed a useless burden now, a secret that would die with them in these Belgian woods.

"Major!" Freddy's voice was a sharp, excited whisper. "Over here! You have to see this!"

Dave and the Major exchanged a look, then pushed their weary bodies up and followed Freddy through the bushes. He was standing at the edge of the hollow, pointing down a gentle, wooded slope. At the bottom of the slope, nestled in a small, natural clearing, was a cottage. It was a simple woodsman's cottage, made of stone and timber, with a plume of white smoke curling lazily from its chimney.

"Someone is home," Freddy breathed.

"And that someone could be German," the Major cautioned, his hand instinctively going to his pistol. "Or a collaborator."

"Or they could be a Belgian who hates the Germans as much as we do," Dave countered, his hope rekindling. "We need help, Major. We need food, water, a place to rest. My shoulder isn't going to get better out here in the dirt."

"He is right," the Major conceded after a long moment. "It is a risk. But it is a risk we must take. Staying here is not an option." He looked at the two boys, his eyes filled with a weary sort of pride. "You boys have the courage of lions. Let us see if our luck has truly run out."

His mind made up, the Major took the lead. "I will approach alone first," he said. "You two stay here, under cover. If you hear shooting, run. Head west. Do you understand?"

"No," Dave said firmly. "We stick together. They see a lone soldier, they might shoot. They see two boys with a wounded man, they might show pity."

Freddy nodded vigorously. "We're a team! All for one, and one for all!"

The Major looked at their determined faces and a faint smile touched his lips. "Very well. All for one. But stay behind me, and let me do the talking. And be ready to run."

They made their way down the slope as quietly as they could, using the trees for cover. As they drew closer, they could hear the sound of an axe splitting wood. They peered through the last line of bushes. A man was in the yard behind the cottage. He was old, with a wiry frame, a thick white beard, and clear, steady eyes. He was methodically splitting logs and stacking them against the cottage wall. He wore simple peasant's clothes. There were no soldiers, no vehicles, nothing to suggest a German presence.

Major Dubois took a deep breath, holstered his pistol, and stepped out into the open, with Dave and Freddy close behind him.

"Bonjour, monsieur," the Major called out, his voice calm and steady.

The old man stopped his work instantly, his hand resting on the handle of the axe. He did not look startled, but wary, like a forest animal that has sensed a change in the wind. His eyes took in the Major's tattered but still recognizable uniform, Dave's bandaged shoulder, and Freddy's mud-caked form. He looked at them for a long, silent moment.

"You are soldiers," he said finally. It was not a question. His French was thick with a local Walloon accent.

"We are," the Major confirmed. "An officer of the Belgian army, and these two brave boys, one American, one English. We have... run into some trouble. As you can see, my young friend is wounded. We need water, and perhaps a place to rest for a short while, if you would be so kind."

The old man’s eyes lingered on Dave's shoulder, then moved back to the Major’s face. His expression was unreadable. "The Boche are just across the river," he said flatly. "They have patrols everywhere. To help you is to risk death. My death, and my wife's."

"We understand that," the Major said softly. "We will not endanger you. If you will just give us a drink of water, we will be on our way."

The old man stared at them, his gaze unwavering. Dave’s heart sank. He had been a fool to hope. This man was just trying to survive. He wasn't going to risk his life for three strangers. Dave braced himself for the refusal, for the command to leave.

Then the old man sighed, a deep, weary sound. He leaned the axe against the woodpile. "My son was killed at Fort Eben-Emael," he said, his voice suddenly thick with grief. "He was twenty years old. He would have called you brothers." He turned and gestured toward the cottage door with a calloused hand. "Come inside. The wife has soup on the fire. You will not go thirsty or hungry in my house."

Relief washed over Dave in a warm flood, so potent it nearly buckled his knees. He felt Freddy shift beside him, and Major Dubois let out a long, slow breath he seemed to have been holding since they first saw the cottage. The old man, who had spoken of his own son with such profound, simple sorrow, seemed to them in that moment like a saint.

Inside, the cottage was just one room, but it was a haven of peace against the raging storm of the war outside. A woman, as old and wiry as her husband, looked up from a large black pot bubbling over the hearth. Her face was a roadmap of wrinkles and care, but her eyes, when they met theirs, were not filled with fear, but a deep, weary kindness. She said nothing, but simply took three bowls from a wooden cupboard and began to ladle out a thick, steaming vegetable soup.

"My wife, Marie," the old man said, his voice softer now that they were inside. "And I am Jean."

"We are deeply in your debt, Jean, Marie," Major Dubois said, removing his cap. "I am Major Dubois. This is Dave, and this is Freddy."

The three of them sat on rough-hewn stools around a small wooden table. The soup was the most delicious thing Dave had ever tasted. It warmed him from the inside out, chasing away some of the deep, biting chill of fear and exhaustion. For a few minutes, the only sound was the scrape of spoons against earthenware bowls and the gentle crackle of the fire. It was a pocket of impossible normalcy in a world gone mad.

"The Boche, they crossed the Meuse at Dinant two days ago," Jean said, breaking the silence. He had not taken any soup for himself, but sat watching them, his gnarled hands resting on his knees. "The news we hear is not good. It comes from travelers, and it is always of retreat. The French Ninth Army was supposed to hold the river." He shook his head slowly. "They say the Panzers just... drove right through them."

Major Dubois put his spoon down. His face was grim. "That matches the intelligence we carry. Their main thrust is not where we expected it. They are moving west, toward the sea, trying to cut us off."

"Cut who off?" Freddy asked, his voice sharp with anxiety.

"The British Expeditionary Force. The French First Army. The Belgians. All of us in the north," the Major replied gravely. "If they reach the coast at Abbeville or Boulogne, they will have us in a pocket."

The image was stark and terrifying. A giant steel trap snapping shut. Dave looked from the Major’s set face to Freddy’s wide, alarmed eyes. He thought of the Belgian soldiers they had left, digging in with such futile desperation. A picket fence against a tidal wave, he had thought. It seemed he was right.

"You must get your information to the British," Jean said, his gaze fixed on Major Dubois. "They are your best hope now."

"That is our intention," the Major agreed. "But we are on the wrong side of the river, and my young friend here needs his wound tended."

Marie, who had been listening silently, rose and came over to Dave. She gestured toward his shoulder with a gentle hand. "Let me see," she said, her voice a low murmur.

Hesitantly, Dave allowed the Major to help him slip out of his jacket. The makeshift bandage was stiff with dried blood. Marie clicked her tongue softly and went to the hearth. She returned with a bowl of warm water and clean strips of linen. With hands that were surprisingly gentle and deft, she cleaned the ugly gash on his shoulder. It was a deep graze, torn by a piece of shrapnel from the Messerschmitt's cannon shell. It stung fiercely as she washed it, but Dave bit his lip and did not make a sound. When she was finished, she applied a thick, sweet-smelling salve from a small jar and bound his shoulder with the fresh linen.

"There," she said, patting his good arm. "It is clean now. You must rest."

"Thank you," Dave said, his voice thick. "Thank you very much."

Suddenly, Jean held up a hand, his head cocked. "Listen."

In the sudden silence, Dave could hear it. A low, grinding rumble that was not the distant thunder of artillery. It was mechanical, and it was getting closer.

"A vehicle," Major Dubois whispered, his hand instantly going to the pistol at his belt.

Jean was already on his feet, his face a mask of urgent concern. He peered through a tiny crack in the wooden shutters covering the single window. His body went rigid. "A patrol. Germans. A Kübelwagen. It is slowing down."

Marie gasped and put a hand to her mouth. The cozy haven was instantly transformed into a deadly trap.

"Quickly!" Jean hissed, moving toward a large, worn rug in the center of the floor. "The cellar. It is the only place." He bent down and tugged, revealing the edges of a wooden trapdoor. "Hurry! They will be here in a moment."

Major Dubois did not hesitate. "You first, Freddy. Then Dave. Go on!"

Freddy scrambled toward the opening. Jean heaved the heavy door up, revealing a set of steep wooden steps leading down into blackness. The smell of damp earth and potatoes rose up to meet them. Freddy disappeared into the hole like a rabbit.

"Dave, now!" the Major urged.

Dave slid off the stool, his shoulder throbbing with a fresh ache, and moved as quickly as he could. He lowered himself onto the steps, his feet searching for a hold in the dark.

"There is no time to be a gentleman, Major," Jean said, his voice strained. "You must go."

Major Dubois gave the old man a look of profound gratitude and apology. "God keep you, Jean," he said, and then he too was descending into the cellar.

Jean lowered the trapdoor over them, and the world went completely black. A moment later, they heard the scrape of the rug being pulled back into place. Then, the heavy thud of boot steps on the floorboards just above their heads. They were so close. A German voice, harsh and guttural, barked a question.

Dave held his breath. He could feel Freddy pressed against him in the tight space, could hear the Major's shallow, controlled breathing. The cellar was small and cramped. Sacks of potatoes and carrots were piled against one wall, and shelves of preserves lined another. The air was cool and close.

Above them, they heard Jean's voice, answering in his thick Walloon French. His tone was slow, perhaps a bit dull, the voice of a simple old peasant who did not understand the ways of the world. Then Marie spoke, her voice thin and wavering, a perfect imitation of a frightened old woman.

Heavy boots stomped around the small room. Dave’s heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird. What if they had been seen coming to the cottage? What if someone had informed on them? Every creak of the floorboards above was a jolt of pure terror. He imagined a German soldier standing directly over the trapdoor, his rifle held at the ready.

A drawer was pulled open, its contents scattered on the floor. A German voice shouted again, this time with impatience. Jean answered, his tone unchanging. Dave squeezed his eyes shut, praying. He prayed for the safety of the old couple who were risking their lives for them.

Then there was a crash. It sounded like a bowl being smashed against the wall. Marie cried out, a small, sharp sound of distress. Dave flinched in the darkness, his fists clenching. He felt a wave of helpless fury. The Major put a steadying hand on his arm, a silent command to remain still.

More guttural conversation followed. It seemed to go on for an eternity. Dave tried to follow the sounds. The boots moved toward the hearth. Then toward the door. Was it over? Were they leaving?

The front door of the cottage creaked open. There was another short exchange of words, and then the door slammed shut. The rumble of the Kübelwagen's engine, which had been idling outside, grew louder as it pulled away. The sound faded into the distance, until once again the only noise was the faint, ever-present growl of the war.

They waited in the suffocating darkness, not daring to move. It could be a trick. The soldiers could be waiting outside, listening. Minutes stretched into an agony of suspense. Finally, they heard the rug being pulled back. Light streamed in as Jean lifted the trapdoor. His face, weary and pale, looked down at them.

"They are gone," he said. "It is safe to come up."

They climbed out of the cellar, blinking in the firelight. The room was in disarray. The three soup bowls lay shattered on the floor where one of the soldiers had swept them from the table. Marie was quietly picking up the pieces, her face expressionless.

"They took the last of our bread," Jean said, his voice flat. "And two jars of preserves." He looked at the broken pottery on the floor. "They wanted to know if we had seen any soldiers. Belgian or French. I told them we see nothing. We are old and we stay inside."

"You were magnificent," Major Dubois said, his voice filled with admiration. "Both of you. You saved our lives."

Jean shrugged, but a flicker of pride showed in his tired eyes. "They are arrogant. They do not believe a simple peasant could have the courage to lie to them. But you cannot stay. They said they would be patrolling this road regularly. It is no longer safe for you here, or for us."

He went to the wall and took down a large, rolled-up oilcloth. He spread it on the table. It was a detailed hand-drawn map of the area.

"You must cross the river tonight," Jean said, his finger tracing a line. "Here. About two kilometers downstream. The banks are steep, but there is a small cove on this side, hidden by willows. I have a boat there. A small one, for fishing. I have kept it hidden from them."

Dave stared at the map. A boat. It was a chance. A real chance.

"You would give us your boat?" Freddy asked, his voice full of wonder.

"My son cannot use it anymore," Jean said simply. The words hung in the air, heavy with a grief that needed no further explanation. He tapped another spot on the far side of the river. "Here is the French side. The land is marshy, but if you go inland one kilometer, you will find a road. It leads south, toward Rethel. The French should be there. If they are anywhere."

He began to give the Major detailed instructions, pointing out landmarks. A ruined church tower, a distinctive bend in the river, a line of tall poplar trees. Major Dubois listened intently, memorizing every detail.

As they prepared to leave, Marie pressed a bundle into Freddy's hands. It was a loaf of dark, heavy bread she must have had hidden away, and a chunk of hard cheese. "For the journey," she whispered. "Eat when you can."

"We can't take your food," Dave protested. "After they..."

"Go," she insisted, her eyes firm. "It is more important that you get through. Go and tell your generals what is happening to us."

Outside, the moon was hidden by thick clouds, cloaking the world in a deep, inky blackness. It was perfect for their purpose. Jean led them away from the cottage, not by the road, but through a tangled patch of woods that ran along the ridge above the river. The going was slow and difficult. Dave’s shoulder ached with a dull, insistent rhythm, and he stumbled more than once, saved from falling by the Major's steadying arm.

After what felt like an hour of silent, careful progress, Jean stopped. "Down there," he whispered, pointing through a gap in the trees. "The willows. The boat is tied up just beyond them. Be careful. The path is steep."

Major Dubois gripped the old man's arm. "I have no words to thank you, Jean. The Allies will not forget this."

Jean nodded, his face a pale blur in the darkness. "Just win the war," he said, his voice rough. "For my son."

He turned and melted back into the shadows without another word, a ghost returning to his haunted home.

The three of them scrambled down the muddy, treacherous bank. The air grew damp and thick with the smell of river water and decaying leaves. They found the cove, a small bite taken out of the riverbank, shrouded by the drooping branches of willow trees. And there, just as Jean had said, was the boat. It was a small, flat-bottomed rowboat, barely big enough for the three of them.

"All right, lads," the Major whispered, his voice taut with tension. "Let's be quick about it. Freddy, you get in the middle. Dave, you and I will take the oars. Try not to make a sound."

They untied the rope and carefully pushed the boat into the dark, swirling water. It bobbed precariously as they climbed in. The current was stronger than Dave had expected, tugging at them immediately, trying to pull them downstream. He and the Major dipped their oars into the water as gently as they could, pulling with long, slow strokes.

The boat moved out from under the cover of the willows into the open river. The far bank was an invisible line somewhere in the darkness ahead. The only sounds were the soft dip and pull of the oars and the gurgle of water against the hull. Every sound seemed magnified a hundred times. A snapping twig on the bank, the distant hoot of an owl, each made Dave's muscles tense.

They were about a third of the way across when it happened.

A brilliant white beam of light slashed through the darkness, sweeping across the surface of the river from the far bank.

"Searchlight!" Freddy hissed.

"Don't move!" the Major commanded in a low, urgent tone. "Freeze!"

Dave held his oar motionless in the water, his body rigid. The light was moving systematically, back and forth, getting closer. He could feel his heart pounding a frantic, terrified rhythm. The beam swept over a spot just twenty yards upstream from them, turning the black water into a sheet of glittering silver. It paused, then continued its slow, methodical sweep. It was coming right for them. Dave held his breath, waiting for the inevitable moment of discovery, the shout, the burst of machine gun fire.

The light touched the water just in front of their bow. For one blinding, heart-stopping second, Dave was sure they were caught. He could see the Major's face, a pale, grim statue opposite him. Then, miraculously, the beam lifted slightly and swept over them, illuminating the treetops on the bank they had just left. It continued its arc and then swung back toward the other direction.

"Now!" the Major breathed. "Row! For your lives!"

The shriek of another Stuka attack echoed behind them, followed by the dull crump of explosions. Dave clutched the dispatch case to his chest as if it were his own heart. He glanced at Freddy, who was jogging to keep up. "Well, here we go again," Dave breathed. A ghost of a grin touched Freddy's lips. "At least this time we might have a proper plane!" he shot back. But his grin faded as he looked at the western sky, stained orange and black with the smoke of burning towns and the dark, circling specks of enemy aircraft. The picket fence was long gone, and the tidal wave was rolling over everything. And they were about to try and fly right into the heart of it.
