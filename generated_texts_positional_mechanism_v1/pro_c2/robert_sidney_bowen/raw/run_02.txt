The waist guns, which had been covered, were swung out and pointed, the heavy clack of bolts being slammed home echoing across the water. The Portuguese refueling crew dropped their hoses and scattered like rats. The man on the pier, seeing the situation had turned against him, slowly raised his hands. The fishing boat, its element of surprise lost, suddenly revved its engine and sped away into the darkness of the harbor. Just then, the embassy launch came roaring back, with Colonel Welsh standing in the bow like a furious figurehead. "What in blazes is going on here?" he demanded as he leaped from the launch onto the pontoon. "We had some uninvited guests, sir," Dawson said, his pistol still trained on the man on the pier. "He was signaling that boat." Colonel Welsh took in the scene at a glance, his eyes resting on the man with the scar. "Major von Richter," the colonel said, his voice dangerously soft. "I thought I smelled the Gestapo. It's been a long time since we crossed paths in Berlin." The man, von Richter, gave a stiff, formal bow. "Colonel Welsh. Always a pleasure. I see your luck has not run out. We received a report that you had been lost at sea. Imagine my surprise." "I'm a difficult man to lose, Major," Welsh retorted. "Now, I suggest you return to the shadows you crawled out of before you cause a diplomatic incident that even your friends in the Portuguese secret police can't smooth over." Von Richter smiled that cold smile again. "This is not over, Welsh," he said. He turned and walked away, melting back into the shadows of the dock as if he had never been there. The trio stood in silence for a moment, the tension slowly draining away. "So much for a quiet stop," Freddy said, letting out a long breath. Colonel Welsh clapped Dawson on the shoulder. "Good work, both of you. Your instincts were right, Dawson. That was von Richter. The most dangerous Abwehr man in Western Europe. He wouldn't be here unless he suspected something. He doesn't believe the President is dead." "You mean he saw through the plan, sir?" Dawson asked, stunned. "Not entirely," the colonel said, a thoughtful frown on his face. "But he knows something is wrong. The official story has a hole in it, and he's worrying at it like a dog with a bone. He knows we're up to something, and he's not going to stop until he finds out what." He turned to Lieutenant Marks. "Is the plane ready?" "Fueled and ready, sir." "Then let's go. The big Catalina flying boat lifted off the glassy surface of the Tagus estuary, its twin engines beating a powerful, reassuring rhythm against the night. Below them, the lights of Lisbon dwindled to a faint, jeweled smear against the black velvet of the land. Soon, there was nothing but the vast, empty darkness of the Atlantic Ocean.

Inside the cockpit, Lieutenant Marks held the plane steady on its new southerly course. Behind him, in the navigator's compartment, Colonel Welsh, Dawson, and Freddy Farmer were gathered around the small chart table. The single dim bulb overhead cast their faces in sharp relief.

"So that was the famous von Richter," Freddy said, breaking the silence. "He didn't look like much. Just another chap in a gray coat."

"That's what makes him dangerous," Colonel Welsh replied, his voice low and serious. "He's not the heel-clicking, shouting type. He's quiet, he watches, and he's brilliant. If he's in Lisbon, it's not by accident. He was here waiting for us."

"But how could he know, sir?" Dawson asked, a frown creasing his forehead. "Our flight plan was secret. Nobody knew we were switching to a Catalina in England, let alone coming to Lisbon."

"He didn't know," the colonel corrected. "He suspected. The official report said your B-25 went down. The wreckage was found. Two bodies, burned beyond recognition. The mission was a tragic failure. The President's trip was indefinitely postponed. That was the story we fed the press, and by extension, the enemy."

"So what's the problem?" Freddy chimed in. "Sounds like a perfectly good story to me."

"It's too perfect," Welsh said grimly. "Von Richter is a student of human nature and of his opponents. He knows me. He knows I don't give up. He knows that if a mission this important fails, I don't just file a report and go home. I find another way. He probably reasoned that the loss of the B-25 might be a feint, a deliberate deception to make the Nazis lower their guard."

The weight of the colonel's words settled heavily in the small compartment. Dawson felt a familiar knot of tension tighten in his stomach. This was no longer a simple delivery run with a bit of risk. This was a personal duel between his commanding officer and one of the Third Reich's most feared intelligence agents. And he and Freddy were the pawns on the board.

"So when he heard whispers of an American flying boat making an unscheduled stop," the colonel continued, "he put two and two together. He came to the docks to see for himself. And he saw us. Now he doesn't just suspect. He knows. He knows the decoy mission is still active."

"And if the decoy mission is active," Dawson finished the thought aloud, "it means the real mission is still on schedule. He'll be trying to figure out what we're really up to."

"Exactly," Colonel Welsh confirmed. He tapped a long, thin finger on the chart. "He can't be sure what our role is now. But he knows this plane is important. He will assume, as the original plan intended, that this plane is a key part of the President's journey. Which means he will do everything in his power to stop us."

A shiver, not entirely from the high-altitude chill, ran down Dawson's spine. "So we can expect trouble, sir?"

The colonel's smile was thin and without humor. "I would be very surprised if we didn't, Dawson. Very surprised indeed. Von Richter isn't the type to watch his quarry fly away and do nothing. He'll have something planned."

The hours crawled by. The steady drone of the engines was the only sound, a monotonous hum that seemed to magnify the immense emptiness of the ocean around them. Dawson and Freddy took turns on watch, scanning the dark sky and the star-dusted water from the big teardrop-shaped blisters on the Catalina's flanks. The rest of the crew, two sergeants who manned the guns and the radio, remained at their posts, their faces tense.

It was Dawson's turn in the port blister. His eyes, accustomed to the darkness, moved in a slow, steady sweep. The moon had risen, a silver crescent that cast a pale, ghostly light on the scattered clouds below. It was a beautiful, peaceful scene, but Dawson felt no peace. He felt exposed, a solitary target moving across an endless shooting gallery.

He thought he saw it first as a flicker, a momentary break in a star's light. He blinked, telling himself it was just his tired eyes playing tricks on him. He stared at the spot, holding his breath. Nothing. He was about to relax when he saw it again. This time it was unmistakable. A dark shape, moving fast, silhouetted against a bank of high, thin clouds.

"Colonel!" he called, his voice sharp and urgent. "Something at ten o'clock high!"

Colonel Welsh was at his side in an instant, binoculars already pressed to his eyes. Freddy scrambled into the starboard blister, his own eyes scanning the sky.

"What is it, sir?" Dawson asked, his hands gripping the handles of the .50-caliber machine gun.

"Can't tell yet," the colonel said, his voice tight. "It's keeping its distance. But it's matching our course."

For several minutes, the silent, deadly game of cat and mouse continued. The dark shape shadowed them, a predator stalking its prey, waiting for the right moment to strike. Then it began a long, slow descent, banking so the moonlight caught the angles of its wings and the distinctive shape of its long, greenhouse-like cockpit.

"Condor," Welsh breathed, lowering his binoculars. The name hung in the air like a death sentence. "Focke-Wulf 200. The Scourge of the Atlantic."

Dawson's blood ran cold. He knew all about the Condor. A four-engine bomber and maritime patrol aircraft, it was fast, heavily armed, and had a massive range. It was designed specifically to hunt and kill things in the middle of the ocean. Things exactly like them.

"This is it, then," Freddy's voice came over the intercom, surprisingly steady. "Von Richter's little surprise party."

"Looks that way," the colonel's voice was grim. "Marks, give it everything you've got! Gunners, stand by! This is going to be rough!"

Lieutenant Marks pushed the throttles to the firewall. The Catalina's engines roared in protest, the whole airframe vibrating as it strained for every last knot of speed. But it was no use. The sleek Condor was faster, more powerful. It closed the distance with contemptuous ease.

"Here he comes!" Dawson yelled. "Banking for a pass from the port side!"

The German plane grew with terrifying speed, its four engines spitting flame in the darkness. Dawson swung his big machine gun around, lining up the sights. He waited, his heart pounding against his ribs. He had to wait until the last possible second. The Catalina was a big, slow target. Every bullet had to count.

The night exploded in a chaos of noise and light. The Condor's cannons opened up, sending brilliant lines of green and white tracer shells streaking past the Catalina. Dawson squeezed his triggers, and his own gun answered with a deafening roar, the heavy .50-caliber slugs arcing out into the night. He saw his tracers walk across the Condor's fuselage, but the German plane was built tough. It barely seemed to notice.

The Condor flashed past them, its engines thundering. The Catalina shuddered as cannon shells found their mark, a sound like a giant tearing sheet metal.

"Damage report!" Welsh snapped.

"A few holes in the wing, sir!" Marks called from the cockpit. "Nothing critical yet! He's climbing for another run!"

"Freddy, you see him?" Dawson shouted.

"I've got him!" came the reply. "He's coming around to my side!"

The whole terrifying sequence repeated itself. The Condor dove, its guns blazing. This time it was Freddy's turn to answer, his machine gun chattering defiance into the night. The air inside the Catalina filled with the sharp, acrid smell of cordite. Dawson could hear the ping of shrapnel hitting the hull around him. He felt utterly helpless, able to do nothing but wait for the attacker to come back into his own field of fire.

Suddenly, the starboard engine coughed. It sputtered once, twice, then caught again, but its steady roar had changed to a ragged, uneven beat.

"We've taken a hit in the starboard engine!" Marks yelled over the intercom. "Losing oil pressure!"

"Keep it running as long as you can!" the colonel ordered.

Dawson gritted his teeth. This was bad. Very bad. The Catalina could fly on one engine, but not well. They would lose speed and altitude. They would be a sitting duck.

The Condor, seeing its prey was wounded, circled lazily, taking its time. The German pilot knew he had them. He was just deciding how to finish the job.

"Sir," Dawson said, his mind racing. "Down there! Those clouds!"

Below them was a thick, churning layer of storm clouds, a squall line moving across the ocean. It was their only chance.

"Marks, can you get us down there?" Welsh asked.

"I can try, sir! It's going to be a rough ride!"

"Do it!"

The nose of the Catalina dipped, and they began a desperate dive toward the churning gray mass. The Condor pilot saw their intent immediately and dove after them, his guns opening up again. Tracers zipped past the cockpit windows. The plane bucked as more shells slammed into the fuselage. The port blister window next to Dawson crazed, a spiderweb of cracks appearing in the thick plexiglass.

Then they were in it. The world outside dissolved into a gray, turbulent chaos. The big flying boat was tossed about like a leaf in a gale. Rain hammered against the hull. Lightning flashed, momentarily illuminating the terrifying, violent motion of the clouds.

"He won't follow us in here," Dawson said, half to himself. "It's too dangerous."

"Don't count on it," Welsh said, his knuckles white as he gripped a bulkhead for support. "A German pilot in a Condor is a confident man."

As if on cue, the starboard engine gave a final, shuddering cough and died. A terrible silence fell on that side of the plane, replaced by the howling of the wind and the groaning of the strained airframe.

"Engine's gone, sir!" Marks reported, his voice strained as he fought the controls. The loss of power on one side was trying to pull them into a deadly spiral. "I can't hold her for long like this! We're losing altitude fast!"

"We have to get below the storm," the colonel said. "Get us down to the water!"

They broke through the bottom of the cloud layer barely five hundred feet above the ocean. The sea was a nightmare of huge, white-capped waves. Landing in that would be suicide; the plane would be smashed to pieces in seconds.

And then Freddy's voice, sharp with alarm, came from the other side of the plane. "He's still there! Off the starboard quarter!"

Dawson looked. The Condor had followed them through. It was circling, waiting for them to clear the storm, ready to pounce. But the German pilot seemed to be having his own troubles. The squall was battering his plane as well, and he was fighting to keep it stable.

"He's staying out of our range," Dawson noted. "He's waiting for us to crash."

"We need a miracle," Colonel Welsh said softly.

It was Freddy who provided it. The young English pilot had been a champion marksman before the war. He had an uncanny eye for deflection shooting. As the Condor made a wide, banking turn, its broad belly was momentarily exposed. It was a long shot, almost an impossible one.

"Cover me, Dave!" Freddy yelled.

Dawson opened up, sending a stream of tracers toward the Condor, forcing the German pilot to focus on him. At the same moment, Freddy fired a long, sustained burst from his own gun. For a heart-stopping second, it looked like he had missed. Then, a thin trail of black smoke began to leak from the Condor's inboard port engine.

The German pilot seemed to realize his peril at the same instant. The smoke thickened, turning into a greasy black plume. The Condor wobbled, then broke off its attack, turning away and beginning a slow, labored climb, heading back the way it had come.

A ragged cheer went up inside the Catalina. They had done it. They had driven him off.

But their celebration was short-lived.

"Colonel, we're in real trouble," Lieutenant Marks said, his voice grim. "The port engine is overheating from the strain. I've got a fire warning light. And we're still losing altitude. We've got maybe ten minutes of flight left, if we're lucky. We have to set down."

Colonel Welsh looked out at the raging sea. "We can't land in this."

"We don't have a choice, sir," Marks replied. "Either we try it, or we go into the drink nose-first."

Dawson scanned the horizon, his heart sinking. There was nothing. Just miles and miles of angry, gray water. They had survived the hunter only to be claimed by the sea.

And then he saw it. A dark smudge on the horizon, almost lost in the spray and the gloom.

"Land!" he shouted, pointing. "Straight ahead! An island!"

All eyes strained to see it. It was a small, volcanic-looking peak, jutting sharply out of the ocean. A desolate, forsaken-looking place. But it was land.

"Can we make it?" Welsh asked Marks.

"It'll be close, sir," the pilot said, his jaw set. "But it's our only shot. There might be a sheltered cove or a lagoon on the lee side."

The next few minutes were the longest of Dawson's life. The smoking port engine screamed in protest as Marks nursed every last ounce of power from it. The island grew slowly larger. It was a single, barren volcanic cone, wreathed in mist. On the far side, away from the wind, the water was calmer, a stretch of relatively protected sea inside a crescent-shaped bay.

"I'm taking her in," Marks announced. "Brace for impact!"

With the one good engine throttled back, the Catalina glided toward the water. It was a masterful piece of flying. Marks stalled the big plane just feet above the surface, letting it settle into the water with a jarring splash and a long, skidding hiss. For a moment, it felt as though the impact would tear them apart. The plane groaned and shuddered, but the hull held. They slowed, and finally, came to a stop, bobbing in the choppy waters of the bay.

Silence descended, broken only by the lapping of water against the fuselage and the sigh of the wind. They were down. They were alive. They were stranded.

Colonel Welsh was the first to speak. "Damage report. Everyone all right?"

Miraculously, there were no serious injuries. They were bruised and shaken, but alive. The plane itself was another story. Lieutenant Marks made a quick inspection.

"The starboard engine is a total loss, sir," he reported grimly. "Looks like an oil line was severed. It's seized up solid. The port engine is cooked. We were lucky the whole thing didn't catch fire. The hull has a dozen or more holes, but none below the waterline, thank God. We're floating, but we're not going anywhere."

Dawson looked out the blister at the desolate island. Gray rock, black sand, and a few patches of tough, scrubby grass. No sign of life. "Where are we, sir?"

Colonel Welsh was already at the chart table, making calculations. "Based on our last known position and our estimated speed and drift... my guess is we're somewhere in the Azores. One of the smaller, uninhabited western islands. We're hundreds of miles off course." He looked up, his face a mask of frustration. "Hundreds of miles from where we need to be."

The mission was a shambles. The President's plane was still flying on its original schedule. The new orders, the ones in Welsh's briefcase that were meant to alert the bases of the real flight, were here, in a crippled plane in the middle of nowhere. The safety of the President of the United States depended on them, and they had failed.

"We have to get a message out, sir," Dawson said.

"And how do you propose we do that?" the colonel asked tiredly. "The Condor's first pass took out our main radio antenna. The emergency radio has a range of maybe fifty miles, if we're lucky. Broadcasting blind is more likely to bring von Richter's friends down on us than our own. He'll have U-boats combing this area, looking for the Condor and for us."

Freddy Farmer, ever the optimist, tried to find a silver lining. "Well, at least we have plenty of tins of bully beef, sir."

The attempt at humor fell flat. The reality of their situation was too stark. They were alone, cut off, with a mission of world-shaking importance resting on their shoulders.

"First things first," Welsh said, his voice regaining its command. "We need to get the plane secured and hidden as best we can. Then we'll assess the damage more thoroughly. There has to be a way. There's always a way."

They managed to taxi the crippled Catalina using its one faltering engine toward a narrow inlet, beaching it on a strip of black sand where it was partially hidden from the sea by an outcropping of volcanic rock. As the sun began to set, casting long, eerie shadows across the island, they gathered their tools to begin the near-impossible task of trying to repair a ruined engine with nothing but hand tools and sheer determination.

Dawson was working with Freddy to remove the cowling from the dead starboard engine when he paused and looked up at the rocky slopes of the volcano that dominated the island. Something had caught his eye. A faint, unnatural glint of light, high up near the summit. It was there for a second, then gone.

"Did you see that, Freddy?" he asked quietly.

"See what, old boy? All I see is a lot of rock and a ruined engine."

Dawson kept staring. He was sure he had seen it. A reflection. Like sunlight glinting off a piece of glass. Or a binocular lens.

"Colonel," he said, walking over to where Welsh was studying the damaged oil lines. "I think we may have company."

He explained what he saw. The colonel immediately grabbed his binoculars and scanned the high ridges. For a long time, he saw nothing. Then he stiffened.

"You're right, Dawson," he said, his voice barely a whisper. "Look there. Just to the right of that jagged peak. A radio mast. A very German-looking radio mast."

Dawson and Freddy looked. And now they saw it too. A thin, skeletal metal pole, almost invisible against the gray rock. It was a cleverly camouflaged antenna.

The horrible truth dawned on them all at once. Their desolate, deserted island refuge was nothing of the sort. They had, by a one-in-a-million stroke of bad luck, landed on the doorstep of a secret German weather station and observation post. They weren't alone. They were sharing the island with the enemy.

Colonel Welsh lowered his binoculars, a new, hard light in his eyes. "It seems," he said, his voice dangerously calm, "that von Richter had more than one trap set for us. He didn't just send a plane. He had his people waiting." He looked from Dawson to Freddy, then at the two sergeants who had joined them. "They'll have seen us land. They'll be reporting our position to U-boat command right now."

"So what do we do, sir?" Freddy asked, his hand instinctively going to the pistol on his belt.

The colonel's thin lips curved into that grim, humorless smile again. "The mission is to deliver those orders, gentlemen. Our plane can't fly. Therefore, the first step is to acquire a working radio. A long-range radio." He nodded toward the peak. "And it appears our neighbors have one we can borrow."

Dawson looked from the crippled plane to the forbidding, enemy-held peak. Their two-way hop across the ocean had just turned into a commando raid.

"Well, sir," Dawson said, a slow grin spreading across his face in spite of the danger. "At least we won't get bored waiting for a rescue."

Colonel Welsh turned his gaze from the distant peak to Dawson, and the ghost of a smile touched his lips for a fraction of a second. "That's one way of putting it, Dawson. Boredom is not on the agenda."

He turned to the two non-commissioned officers, who had been listening with grave expressions. "Sergeant Miller, Sergeant Kowalski. You heard the situation. We are on an enemy-occupied island. That mast up there means they have a long-range transmitter. They will have already reported a B-25 Mitchell crash-landing on their doorstep. We can assume that a reception party, likely a U-boat, is already on its way."

Sergeant Miller, the pilot, a stocky man with steady eyes, wiped a smear of grease from his cheek. "What are your orders, sir?"

"Our primary mission has failed," the colonel stated flatly. "The enemy knows a plane went down here. The element of deception is gone. Our new mission is threefold. First, survival. Second, neutralization of the enemy threat on this island. Third, and most important, reestablishing contact with Allied command to inform them that the entire Casablanca conference plan is compromised. The only way to do that is with their radio."

He pointed a long, thin finger toward the summit, now turning a deep purple in the fading light. "We will take that post tonight. As soon as it's fully dark."

"What have we got for weapons, sir?" Freddy Farmer asked, his hand still resting on the butt of his service pistol.

"My sidearm," the colonel said. "Yours and Dawson's. Sergeant Miller, you and Kowalski have pistols in the cockpit survival kit?"

"Yes, sir," Kowalski, the younger mechanic, confirmed. "Two .45s, and a flare gun. There's also a machete and two survival knives in the fuselage pack."

"Get them," Welsh commanded. "Check the ammunition. We have five pistols, then. Not a lot. This will have to be a quiet affair. We can't afford a firefight. We go in silent, and we go in fast. Our greatest weapon is surprise. They think we're helpless survivors of a crash. They don't know who is on this plane. They'll be arrogant. We will use that."

Dawson looked up at the volcanic slope. The climb looked treacherous even in daylight. In darkness, it would be a nightmare. "What's the plan of attack, Colonel?"

"Simple and direct," Welsh answered, his eyes already tracing a path up the dark rock. "You, me, and Farmer will make the climb. You two are young, and I've done this sort of thing before. We'll find a covered approach to the installation. Sergeant Miller, you're the best shot among us?"

The pilot nodded slowly. "I qualified as an expert marksman, sir."

"Good. You will stay here. Find a position among these rocks that gives you a clear view of the path leading down from the summit. If anyone comes down, you are to stop them. But only as a last resort. Noise is our enemy. Sergeant Kowalski, you will remain with the aircraft. If you hear prolonged gunfire from the summit, you are to destroy what's left of this plane. Use the fuel from the emergency tank and that flare gun. Make sure nothing is left. Understood?"

"Yes, sir," both sergeants replied in unison, their faces grim.

The colonel turned back to the two aces. "We move in one hour. Eat whatever rations we salvaged. Drink some water. We won't get another chance until this is over. We'll take one canteen apiece, our pistols, and the knives. Travel light, move quiet."

There were no more questions. The orders were clear, the stakes absolute. As the last sliver of the sun bled away below the horizon, plunging the island into an inky, star-dusted blackness, the five men made their final preparations. The only sounds were the whisper of the wind over the rocks and the distant, rhythmic crash of waves on the black sand beach. Dawson checked the action on his Colt .45, the metallic click sounding unnaturally loud in the silence. He felt the familiar thrum of nervous energy, the heightened awareness that came before a dogfight. This was just a different kind of cockpit, he told himself, and a different kind of enemy.

"Ready, Dave?" Freddy asked, his voice a low murmur beside him.

"As I'll ever be, Freddy," Dawson replied, holstering his weapon. "Just like a night scramble, only with more rocks."

Colonel Welsh appeared out of the gloom, a lean, predatory shadow. "It's time," he said, his voice no louder than the rustle of the wind. "Let's go borrow a radio."

The climb was worse than Dawson could have imagined. The volcanic rock was sharp as broken glass in some places and crumbled into treacherous, noisy scree in others. They moved in single file, with Colonel Welsh in the lead, picking his way through the darkness with an uncanny sense of direction. He seemed to flow over the terrain, a ghost in the night. Dawson followed, with Freddy bringing up the rear. They communicated with hand signals, a tap on the shoulder to halt, a low wave to advance.

For more than two hours they struggled upward, their lungs burning, their muscles screaming in protest. The air grew thinner and colder. Several times, one of them would send a shower of small stones rattling down the slope, and they would freeze, hearts hammering against their ribs, listening for any sound from above. But there was only the mournful howl of the wind whipping around the peak. They were climbing up the side of the volcano opposite the beach, using a massive spine of rock as cover from anyone who might be watching from the summit.

Finally, the colonel halted, pressing himself flat against a rock outcropping. He gestured for Dawson and Freddy to crawl up beside him. Below them, not more than two hundred yards away, was the German post.

It was a small, efficient setup. A single, long wooden hut with a low-pitched roof, built to withstand the high winds. A small shed, likely for a generator, hummed softly nearby. And rising up from the center of the small, flattened plateau was the radio mast they had seen from the beach, its guy wires singing in the wind. A faint yellow light glowed from a single window in the hut. A lone figure, bundled in a heavy coat with a rifle slung over his shoulder, paced a short, steady path back and forth in front of the door.

Colonel Welsh pulled out his binoculars, the same pair he had used on the beach, and studied the scene for a full five minutes. He lowered them and turned to the two pilots, his mouth so close to their ears that they could feel his breath.

"One sentry," he whispered. "The light is coming from the main barracks room. The radio shack will be at one end of the hut. I count four men in total, based on the size of the installation. The sentry, and three inside. One of them will be the radio operator."

"How do we do this, sir?" Dawson breathed.

"We take the sentry first. Silently," the colonel said, his voice like chipping ice. "Farmer, you're the lightest on your feet. You and I will circle around to the left, behind that cluster of boulders. There's a blind spot in his patrol route there. We'll wait for him to turn his back. Dawson, you will stay here. When we take him, that's your signal. You move directly for the hut door. Your job is to be the first one through it. Create chaos. I'll be right behind you. Farmer, you will follow and secure the door behind us. We can't have anyone getting out."

"And the radio operator?" Freddy whispered.

"He's the prize," Welsh said grimly. "When we go in, he'll be our first target. We have to get to him before he can send a signal or destroy his equipment. Move on my signal."
