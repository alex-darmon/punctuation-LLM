A German non-commissioned officer, a feldwebel, stood in the doorway, a machine pistol held at the ready. Two soldiers flanked him. "Was machst du hier?" he demanded. "What are you doing here?"

Jean-Luc mumbled something in rapid, provincial French, gesturing around the cellar as if to say he was just a poor man looking for shelter from the night.

The dogs, however, were not interested in Jean-Luc. They scrambled into the cellar, their noses to the ground, and went straight for the corner where Jean-Luc had crumbled the sausage and cheese. They began gobbling it up, their tails wagging.

The feldwebel grunted in annoyance. He kicked at one of the dogs. "Dummköpfe! Stupid heads! I thought you smelled the airmen." He shone his light around the small cellar, the beam passing over Dawson and Freddy, who were huddled against the far wall, trying to make themselves as small as possible. The beam lingered on them for a heart-stopping moment.

"And these two?" the German snapped, gesturing with his weapon.

"My sons," Jean-Luc said quickly in broken German. "They are simple. Not right in the head. We are looking for work. We are from the south."

The feldwebel looked them over with contempt. Their torn clothes, their dirt-smeared faces, their exhausted and vacant expressions, which were not entirely an act, seemed to convince him. He sniffed the air. "What is that smell?"

"Vinegar, sir," Jean-Luc said, holding up his flask. "For the bad water."

The German made a face. He seemed to have lost interest. The downed airmen he was looking for were soldiers, pilots. These three looked like nothing more than miserable vagrants. The dogs, having finished their snack, were now sniffing lazily at the door where Jean-Luc had sprinkled the vinegar, their earlier excitement completely gone.

"Get out," the feldwebel ordered, waving his machine pistol toward the exit. "This whole area is forbidden. If we see you again, we will shoot you. Verstanden?"

"Ja, ja, verstanden," Jean-Luc said, bowing his head obsequiously. He herded Dawson and Freddy toward the door, pushing them ahead of him. They stumbled out into the cool night air, blinking at the assembled German soldiers. The patrol leader gave them one last disgusted look, then turned to his men and issued a string of new orders. The patrol began to move off, the dogs trotting listlessly at their heels.

They waited, frozen in place, until the sounds of the patrol had faded completely into the forest. Only then did Jean-Luc allow himself to relax. He went back into the cellar and retrieved the briefcase and the pistol.

"That was too close," Dawson breathed, his voice shaky. "I thought we were done for."

"The sausage was a good touch," Freddy remarked, a slight tremor in his own voice. "They seemed to enjoy it."

"A hungry dog is a fine hunter," Jean-Luc said, his face grim. "A fed dog is lazy. We cannot stay here. They will be back at dawn to search more thoroughly. We must go now."

He handed the briefcase back to Dawson. The weight of it felt even heavier now. They had survived, but by the thinnest of margins. It was a stark reminder of what Jean-Luc had said. This was a different kind of war. It was not a war of machines and altitude, but of wits and nerve, of sausage and vinegar.

"Where to now?" Dawson asked, his resolve hardening. The close call had not broken his spirit. It had tempered it.

"South," Jean-Luc said, pointing into the darkness. "Always south. We have a long walk before the sun rises."

He turned and melted back into the trees. Dawson shared a look with Freddy, a look of grim determination. They were no longer Dave Dawson and Freddy Farmer, air aces. They were fugitives, couriers, and the last hope of a mission that could not be allowed to fail. Dawson clutched the handle of the briefcase, and together, they followed the silent Frenchman into the deep, dangerous heart of the French night. The war, for them, had started all over again.

The roar of the bomber's engines was suddenly joined by the higher, angrier scream of diving fighters and the furious, chattering reply of the bomber's own .50 caliber machine guns.

"Hold on!" the pilot's voice yelled over the intercom, no longer bothering with military formality. "They're coming around again!"

Dawson and Freddy were thrown against their seats as the big bomber banked sharply to the right, the pilot trying to present the narrowest possible target. Through the small compartment window, Dawson caught a fleeting glimpse of a dark, twin-tailed shape flashing past, its wings spitting angry orange fire. The compartment filled with the acrid smell of cordite from the top turret gun hammering away just above their heads.

"To be stuck here like a sitting duck!" Dawson exclaimed through gritted teeth, his hands clenching into useless fists. "If I was in my Spitfire, I'd show them a thing or two!"

"Easy, Dave," Freddy cautioned, his face pale but his voice steady. "We're just passengers on this trip."

"Passengers on a flying coffin, if we don't do something!" Dawson shot back. He looked at Colonel Welsh, who was gripping the edge of the small table, his knuckles white. The senior officer's face was a mask of concentration as he listened to the sounds of the battle raging around them.

Another tremendous jolt rocked the plane, this one followed by a grinding, sickening sound from the starboard side. The steady thrum of the Wright Cyclone engine faltered, catching, then sputtering into an uneven, coughing rhythm. A red light began to flash on an instrument panel near the forward bulkhead.

"Starboard engine is hit!" the co-pilot's voice shouted, the transmission laced with panic. "Fire warning! I'm hitting the extinguisher!"

"They've crippled us, sir," Dawson said, his voice urgent as he turned to the colonel. "At this rate, they'll pick us apart piece by piece. We can't outrun them and we can't outfight them in this bus."

Colonel Welsh nodded grimly. "I know, Dawson. But the pilot knows his job."

"With all due respect, sir," Dawson pressed, his fighter pilot's instincts screaming for action, "he's a bomber pilot. He's thinking about a bombing run and a steady platform. I'm a fighter pilot. I think about staying alive. There are things we can do! Tricks they won't expect from a B-25!"

The colonel stared at him for a long, hard moment, the roar of the air battle filling the silence between them. The hard light Dawson had seen before glittered in his eyes again. He was weighing the situation, calculating odds just as he had with the envelopes and the agents.

"What do you have in mind?" he asked at last.

"Let me get on the intercom with the pilot, sir," Dawson pleaded. "I can't fly the plane from here, but I can be his eyes. I can tell him what they're going to do before they do it."

Before the colonel could give his assent, the intercom crackled again. "Top gunner is hit! I need a man up here!"

"I'll go, sir!" Freddy Farmer spoke up instantly, already pushing himself out of his chair. "I've handled a fifty-caliber before!"

Colonel Welsh gave the English youth a sharp, appraising look and then nodded once. "Go," he said. "Be careful."

Freddy needed no further encouragement. He scrambled toward the narrow companionway that led to the flight deck and the turret ladder.

"And you, Dawson," the colonel said, turning his full attention back to the American ace. "Get on that handset. Tell the pilot you're his new tactical officer. Let's see if all that experience is good for something besides shooting down Huns over the Channel."

Dawson grinned, a flash of the old, confident ace returning to his face. "Yes, sir!" he said, grabbing the handset. He pressed the transmit button. "Dawson to pilot! Colonel's orders. I'm your spotter. What's our situation?"

"The situation is we're getting shot to pieces!" the pilot retorted, his voice ragged with strain. "One of them is lining up for a pass from below. My belly gunner can't get a clear shot!"

Dawson pressed his face to the small window, craning his neck. He couldn't see below, but he knew the tactic. The German would try to come up into the bomber's blind spot.

"Tell your co-pilot to drop flaps and gear, just for a second!" Dawson commanded into the mouthpiece. "It'll make him overshoot!"

"What? Are you crazy? That'll stall us!"

"Just for a second!" Dawson insisted. "It will throw his aim completely off! He's expecting a steady target! Do it now!"

There was a moment's hesitation, then Dawson felt a lurch and a sudden, dramatic deceleration as the landing gear and flaps deployed into the slipstream. The whole airframe groaned in protest. Outside, the pursuing Messerschmitt, which had been perfectly lined up for a killing blow on the bomber's soft underbelly, suddenly found its target ballooning and slowing. The German pilot, caught by surprise, flashed past them, his cannon shells stitching empty air a hundred yards ahead.

As the Me-110 swept by, it exposed its full broadside to the B-25's waist gunner.

"Now! He's a perfect target for the waist gun!" Dawson yelled.

A new voice, with a distinct English accent, came over the wire. "Right you are! Tally-ho!" It was Freddy. A split second later, the satisfying, heavy thud-thud-thud of the top turret's guns joined the chorus. Dawson saw a stream of tracers arc out from the bomber and converge on the German fighter. Black smoke began to pour from the Messerschmitt's port engine. The fighter wobbled, then peeled away, losing altitude in a long, dark plume.

"Good show, Freddy!" Dawson shouted.

"Pull it up! Pull it up!" the pilot was screaming. The B-25, dangerously close to a stall, shuddered and threatened to fall out of the sky. The co-pilot retracted the gear and flaps, and the plane surged forward again, the one good engine roaring in protest as it took the full strain.

"One down, one to go," the pilot said, a note of newfound respect in his voice. "Where's the other one?"

"He's circling, staying out of range," Dawson reported, scanning the vast emptiness of the sky. "He's probably calling for his friends. We have to get out of here."

"Can't do much with this starboard engine, Dawson," the pilot replied grimly. "We're losing altitude, and I'm fighting the rudder to keep her straight. We'll never make Casablanca now. We're a thousand miles from anywhere."

"What about clouds?" Dawson asked. "Any cover we can dive into?"

"There's a squall line about fifty miles ahead, but I don't know if we can stay in the air that long. That other Hun is just waiting for us to lose speed."

"Then we don't lose speed," Dawson said, his mind racing. "We go down. On purpose." He looked over at Colonel Welsh, who was listening intently, his face unreadable. "Sir, we have to convince him we're finished. That he's already won."

"Explain," the colonel said quietly.

"We let the starboard engine die completely," Dawson said into the handset. "Feather the prop. Start a slow, controlled spiral toward the water, as if we're going in. He'll follow us down, get cocky. He'll want to make sure, maybe even take pictures of the crash for his superiors. When he comes in for a final look, we give him everything we've got left."

It was a desperate gamble, the kind of bluff a cornered fighter pilot might try. For a heavy bomber, it was almost suicidal.

"I don't know," the pilot began, but Colonel Welsh cut him off.

"Do it, Lieutenant," the colonel ordered, his voice ringing with authority. "Do as Dawson says. It's our only chance."

A moment of static-filled silence followed. "Roger that, Colonel," the pilot said finally. "Feathering starboard engine. Here we go."

The coughing of the damaged engine stopped abruptly. The change in sound was terrifying. The bomber, now running on only one engine, yawed hard to the right. The nose dipped, and the vast, gray expanse of the Atlantic Ocean began to wheel slowly up to meet them.

"Maintain control," Dawson spoke calmly into the handset. "Keep the spiral wide. Make it look like you're fighting it, but losing. Freddy, you and the other gunners, hold your fire until I give the word. Hold it, no matter how close he gets."

"Understood, Dave," came Freddy's voice, tight with tension.

Down they went. Five thousand feet. Four thousand. The remaining Messerschmitt, a black cross in the sky, followed them, circling like a patient vulture. Dawson could imagine the German pilot's thoughts. The prize was his. The American President's plane, crippled and falling. It was a victory beyond his wildest dreams.

"He's coming in," the pilot reported, his voice a low murmur. "Closing from six o'clock low."

Two thousand feet. The German fighter was growing larger, bolder. He was no longer circling, but making a direct, slow approach to observe the final moments of the stricken bomber. He was probably getting his camera ready.

"He's almost on top of us," the pilot whispered.

"Everybody get ready," Dawson commanded. "When I say now, pilot, you're going to give that port engine everything it's got and kick full left rudder. Swing the tail into his face. Gunners, the moment we swing, you open up."

The Messerschmitt drew level with their tail. It was the perfect position for a spectator. It was a fatal position for an attacker.

The German pilot was taken completely by surprise. One moment he was observing a dying whale, the next the whale had turned into a porcupine, presenting its entire battery of tail and top turret guns directly in his path, less than a hundred yards away.

Every remaining gun on the B-25 erupted in a single, deafening roar. Freddy in the top turret, the tail gunner, and the waist gunner all poured streams of .50 caliber slugs into the Messerschmitt. It was like hitting a tin can with a shotgun at point-blank range.

The German fighter's canopy shattered. Pieces flew off its fuselage. Its nose dipped, and without making any attempt to pull up, it plunged directly into the sea, disappearing in a huge geyser of white water and black smoke.

For a moment, the only sound in the compartment was the strained whine of the single engine and the rushing of the wind. Then the intercom crackled.

"Scratch two," the pilot said, his voice shaking with a mixture of fear and elation. "Dawson... you ought to transfer to the bomber command."

"I think I'll stick to fighters, thanks," Dawson replied, slumping back into his chair, the handset falling from his limp fingers. He was drenched in sweat.

Colonel Welsh let out a long, slow breath. He ran a hand over his face, and for the first time, he looked his age. "Well," he said, his voice raspy. "That was... instructive. We have our answer. The enemy knows. They were expecting the President."

Freddy Farmer climbed down from the turret, a smudge of grease on his cheek. "The top gunner will be all right, sir," he reported. "Flesh wound in the shoulder. But the plane is another story."

Just then, the pilot came on the intercom again, his tone all business now. "Colonel, I've done a damage assessment. The starboard engine is a complete loss. We're leaking fuel from the port wing tanks, and the fuselage looks like a sieve in places. We've lost most of our hydraulic pressure. I can keep her in the air, but I can't get any altitude, and I can't say for how long. We will not make Casablanca. We will not make Africa, period."

The brief moment of victory vanished, replaced by the grim reality of their situation. They were alive, but they were stranded in a crippled aircraft, hundreds of miles from friendly territory, with the most vital intelligence of the war.

"What's our position, Lieutenant?" Colonel Welsh asked, his mind already working on the next problem.

"About six hundred miles west of the coast of Portugal, sir. My navigator suggests we try for a landing in the Azores. It's the closest land, about four hundred miles. It's neutral territory, but the Portuguese might be friendly."

"No," the colonel said immediately. "Too risky. We'd be interned, and this plane and its contents would become a diplomatic incident. The Germans have influence there. We can't take that chance." He stared out the window at the endless gray water. The mission had been compromised. The President was still in danger. His carefully laid plan was in tatters.

"So what do we do, sir?" Dawson asked, leaning forward again. The exhaustion was gone, replaced by the burning question. "What's the plan now?"

Colonel Welsh turned from the window. His expression was grim, but the hesitation was gone. A new, harder resolve had settled on his features.

"The plan has changed," he said, his voice as cold and hard as steel. "Our first priority was to test the line. We've done that. It's broken. Our second priority now is to get a warning to Washington. The President's flight must be scrubbed, immediately. Our third priority is to get these revised orders to Natal." He tapped the briefcase still sitting on the table.

"But how, sir?" Freddy asked. "We're practically swimming."

"The lieutenant will fly east," the colonel commanded. "Not toward the Azores, but toward the coast of France."

Dawson and Farmer stared at him as though he had lost his mind.

"France, sir?" Dawson gasped. "That's enemy territory! We'd be shot down or captured before we even crossed the coastline!"

"We will not fly to the coast," Colonel Welsh corrected him. "We will fly as far as this plane can take us. And when it can fly no further, we will put it down in the water. We will take to the life raft with that briefcase and my radio. I will send a coded flash message to Washington before the batteries die. Then, we will row."

"Row where, sir?" Freddy asked, bewildered.

The colonel looked at the two young aces, his eyes burning with a fierce intensity. "Toward France," he said quietly. "We've run out of friendly airfields. So we're going to have to find a friendly beach. The mission, gentlemen, is not over. It's just getting started."
