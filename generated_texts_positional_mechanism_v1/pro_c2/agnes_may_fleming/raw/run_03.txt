The air in the chamber was thick as grave-dirt, heavy with the scent of old paper and unlit tallow, and Elara Vane, standing solitary upon the bare stone floor, could feel the weight of it pressing upon her like a physical shroud. She was quite resolved that her end was a matter of minutes, a certainty as solid as the stone beneath her feet, and this knowledge lent her a strange and reckless calm. For this, she had come to be grateful; the frantic terror of her capture had subsided into a placid sort of impatience, and she wished only that they would dispense with the theatrics and get on with the business of her undoing. The council, it seemed, was at last prepared to grant her wish; the three figures behind the great oak table stirred, their faces emerging from the gloom like pale, malevolent moons. The Tribune, a man whose piety was matched only by his cruelty, was polishing a small silver locket with a scrap of silk, and his two companions watched him with the rapt attention of hounds awaiting a command.

“The accused has been given time for reflection,” the Tribune began, his voice a low purr that did not quite conceal its sharp edges. “Let us see if it has borne any fruit. Your name, you say, is Celeste Dubois?”

“I have said nothing of the sort,” Elara replied, her tone light, almost conversational; “you are the one who has said it, and I should hate to contradict a man of your esteemed position, especially when he is so plainly in error.”

A flicker of annoyance crossed the Tribune’s face, and he set his locket down with a soft click. “Insolence will not serve you, woman. You were found in the Archives, in possession of documents pertaining to the city’s water treaties, a clear act of espionage.”

“I was admiring the calligraphy, which is quite exquisite. My only regret is that I did not have time to appreciate it fully before your very loud and ill-mannered soldiers interrupted my study.”

“She mocks us!” hissed the councilor to the Tribune’s right, a thin, weasel-faced man named Malvol. “She admits her guilt with every word!”

“Indeed, she does,” the Tribune agreed, his good humor seemingly restored by the certainty of the outcome. “You understand, then, that such a crime carries but one penalty?”

“I have a passing familiarity with your laws, yes. They are rather simple, which is to say, they are uniformly fatal. What comes next? Do you read from a scroll?”

“You anticipate the ceremony with an unseemly eagerness! You confess, then, that you are a spy for the Western Conclave?”

“I confess only to a deep and abiding appreciation for fine penmanship. You are welcome to interpret that as you will,” said Elara, who knew perfectly well that her fate was sealed the moment she had been taken, and saw no reason to grant them the satisfaction of a cringing confession.

“This is tiresome sophistry! You were in the Archives, you had the documents, and you are, therefore, a spy. The syllogism is perfect.”

“Flawless, I admit. Shall we proceed to the conclusion?”

“With the greatest pleasure!” the Tribune said, taking up a quill and a sheet of heavy parchment that had been lying in wait. “I have rarely signed a warrant with a lighter heart.” He scribbled his name with a flourish that was almost a caress, and then pushed the document toward Malvol, who signed it with a vicious stab of the pen. The third councilor, a silent, hulking brute of a man, merely pressed his inked thumb to the bottom, his part in the proceedings more manual than intellectual.

“It is done,” the Tribune announced, a beatific smile spreading across his face. “You are condemned for high treason, and the sentence of this council is that you be taken from this place to the Red Vault, and there be purified by fire, so that your perfidy may be scoured from this world!”

“Fire?” Elara remarked, raising an eyebrow. “How very dramatic. I had rather expected a simple hanging. It is so much more efficient.”

The Tribune’s smile tightened into a grimace, and he made a sharp, peremptory gesture to the guards. “Take her away! Let the pyre be lit at once!”

As the guards stepped forward, a dry, rasping voice cut through the air, and everyone turned to see Madame Corveau, the council’s treasurer, emerge from the shadows near the doorway. She had been observing the entire affair, her face a mask of weary calculation.

“A moment of your precious time, Tribune? Must this be done with such haste?”

The Tribune glared at her, his performance having been so rudely interrupted. “The sentence is passed, Madame. The matter is closed.”

“A quick death is a tidy solution, I grant you,” she said, stepping into the dim light, “but a dead woman tells no tales. The schematics she stole are still missing, are they not? Her associates have not been identified.”

“She will tell us nothing! Her spirit is obdurate!”

“Perhaps,” Madame Corveau conceded, fixing her gaze upon Elara with an unnerving intensity, “but fire is so very final. A few days in the lower cells, with the proper encouragement, might prove more fruitful than a bonfire. Her information is worth more to us than the fleeting satisfaction of her screams. Let the execution wait.”

The Tribune’s face was a study in thwarted indignation, his jaw working silently as he stared at the old woman. The guards, who a moment before had moved with such purpose, now stood frozen, their eyes flicking between the furious Tribune and the implacable old woman, uncertain whose command held the truest promise of death should it be disobeyed.

The Tribune, whose beatific smile had curdled into something resembling a mask of tragedy, stared at Madame Corveau as if she had just proposed they all take up folk dancing. The very air in the chamber, which moments before had crackled with the promise of imminent and righteous violence, now grew thick with the frost of her pragmatism. The guards, caught between the furnace of the Tribune’s wrath and the iceberg of the treasurer’s logic, did not so much as breathe, their gazes fixed on some neutral point on the stone floor between the two powers. For a long moment, the only sound was the faint sputter of a torch in a wall sconce, its flame seeming to shrink from the chill in the room.

“Very well, Madame,” the Tribune said at last, his voice a low growl of capitulation, each word costing him a piece of his precious dignity. “You shall have your time. But I warn you, if your delicate methods fail to bear fruit, the pyre will seem a mercy. Its hunger will only have grown in the waiting.”

Madame Corveau offered a slight, almost imperceptible inclination of her head, a gesture that was less an acknowledgement of his authority and more a dismissal of his theatrics. “I am quite confident in my methods, Tribune. They are, if nothing else, economical. Now, if you will excuse me, I have ledgers to attend to. The cost of kindling has risen dreadfully.” With another dry rustle of her dark silks, she was gone, melting back into the shadows from which she had emerged, leaving the Tribune to glare at the empty space she had occupied.

He turned his furious gaze back upon Elara, but the victory had been snatched from him, and the fire in his eyes was now more petulant than powerful. “To the lower cells with her!” he snapped at the guards, who, grateful for a clear directive, sprang into motion at once. “And see that she is well secured. Madame Corveau may enjoy her games, but this council’s sentence stands.”

Elara offered him a smile that was all cool insolence. “Do try not to pout, Tribune. It ruins the noble line of your jaw.”

For her impertinence, she received a rough shove from the nearest guard, and then she was being marched from the chamber, her arms gripped in hands like iron vises. They did not return the way she had come, but were led through a narrow archway concealed behind a tapestry depicting some long-forgotten battle. A winding stair of rough-hewn stone plunged down into the very bowels of the fortress, the air growing thick and cold with every step, carrying the scent of damp earth and something older, like forgotten sorrows. The torchlight of her escort cast long, dancing shadows that writhed upon the walls, and the silence was broken only by the scuff of their boots and the faint, constant drip of water from the weeping stone.

The journey ended before a door of heavy, iron-banded oak. One of the guards produced a key the size of a man’s hand and wrestled with a lock that protested with a groan of tortured rust. The door swung inward on a final shriek of its hinges, revealing a space that was less a cell and more a neatly carved hole in the foundations of the world. It was small, no more than eight feet square, and contained nothing but a thin pallet of straw in one corner and a ring of rusted iron bolted to the far wall, a heavy chain depending from it.

Without ceremony, one guard pushed Elara inside while the other fastened the manacle around her ankle. The cold of the iron was a shocking, intimate touch, a final seal on her captivity. They said nothing, their faces as expressive as the stone walls, and then they were gone, the great door booming shut with a sound of utter finality. The bolt shot home with a noise like a breaking bone, and Elara was plunged into a darkness so complete it felt like a physical weight pressing upon her eyes.

For a time, she simply stood there, listening to the frantic beating of her own heart and the slow, methodical drip of water somewhere in the blackness. The bravado that had served her so well in the council chamber, that armor of wit and scorn, began to fray at the edges in the suffocating solitude. It was one thing to face a panel of pompous fools with defiance; it was quite another to be alone with the creeping certainty of one’s own doom. It was not death she feared, not precisely; it was the slow, ignominious chipping away of herself that was to precede it, the "proper encouragement" that Madame Corveau had so bloodlessly promised. She sank down onto the damp straw, the chain giving a short, mocking rattle, and drew her knees to her chest, fighting back a tremor that had nothing to do with the subterranean chill.

She did not know how long she sat there, adrift in the sightless, soundless void. It might have been hours, or a day. Time had ceased to be a river and was now a stagnant pool. At last, a sound broke the stillness: the scrape of the bolt being drawn back. Elara got to her feet, her senses instantly alert, her brief moment of despair locked away once more. The door opened, not with the previous violence, but with a quiet, well-oiled swing, and a rectangle of pale lantern light fell across the floor.

Silhouetted in the doorway was the thin, severe figure of Madame Corveau. She held the lantern aloft in one hand, its light carving harsh shadows on her hawk-like features. She was alone. She stepped inside, placing the lantern on the floor, and pulled the door almost closed behind her, leaving it open just a crack. The light threw their two shadows, monstrously large, against the stone.

“I trust your accommodations are to your liking,” she began, her voice as dry as dust. “They are, I confess, spartan. But then, we are not here to discuss decor.”

“I was beginning to feel neglected,” Elara replied, her own voice steady, though it cost her an effort. “I had imagined you would be more eager to begin your… encouragement.”

Madame Corveau’s thin lips twitched, a gesture that might have been the ghost of a smile. “Haste is the mark of the amateur, child. And I am no amateur. I have found that comfort, or the prospect of it, is a most persuasive tool. A warm meal, a soft bed, the simple pleasure of light; these things become infinitely precious when they are denied.” She paused, her gaze sweeping over Elara as if she were an entry in a ledger. “But I suspect you are not the sort to be swayed by such simple appetites.”

“And I have found that those who offer comfort are often the keenest merchants of pain.”

“A cynic. How refreshing,” Madame Corveau said, without a trace of refreshment in her tone. “Let us dispense with the preliminaries, then. You were sent to acquire the schematics for the Heliostat. You were successful. The documents, however, are no longer on your person. This suggests two possibilities: either you have hidden them, or you have passed them to a confederate. I am inclined to believe the latter. My question is simple. To whom?”

Elara remained silent, her expression a careful blank. The Heliostat. So they knew exactly what she had taken. Her mission had been even more compromised than she had feared.

“Silence is an answer, of course,” Corveau continued, her voice patient, “but not a profitable one. Not for you. Let me be perfectly clear, so that there can be no misunderstanding between us. You will not leave this fortress alive. That is a certainty, as fixed as the rising of the sun. Your sentence has been pronounced. What remains to be determined is merely the manner and the swiftness of your end. A quick, clean flame, as the Tribune desires. Or a slow, painstaking process in this room, and others less pleasant, until you have told me everything I wish to know. Your associates, their networks, your entire perfidious little enterprise. When I am satisfied, you will still burn. The choice before you is not life or death; it is the nature of the path you take to the same destination.”

She let the words hang in the cold air. It was a brutal calculus, stripped of all emotion, and for that reason, far more terrifying than the Tribune’s histrionics. Elara’s mind raced. Corveau was not bluffing; her every syllable rang with the cold iron of truth.

“You place a great deal of faith in your own skills,” Elara said, finally.

“It is not faith; it is experience,” the old woman corrected her. “I have dismantled men and women far more resolute than you. Everyone has a key. One must simply find the right lock. A loved one, a deeply held principle, the simple, animal terror of unending pain. I will find yours.”

She picked up the lantern. “I will leave you to contemplate my offer. I will return when the sun next sets, though of course, you will not see it. I expect you to have made a sensible decision by then.”

She turned and left, as quietly as she had come. The door closed, the bolt slammed home, and the absolute darkness returned, now heavier than before, freighted with the weight of Corveau’s promises. Elara stood for a long time, the image of the old woman’s merciless face burned into her vision. She had faced death, but this was something else. This was the threat of being unmade.

Another indeterminate stretch of time passed. Sleep was impossible. Her thoughts were a frantic whirl of desperate plans and hopeless realities. She had one secret, one small advantage that Madame Corveau did not suspect. She had, in fact, hidden the schematics, not passed them on. In the frantic moments before her capture in the Archives, she had managed to slip the rolled cylinder of parchment into a hollow space behind a loose stone in the wall, a flaw she had noted on a previous reconnaissance. It was a desperate gamble, but it meant the information was not yet lost. It also meant she was the only one who knew its location. This made her more valuable, but also a greater target for Corveau’s attentions.

She was tracing the mortar lines of the wall with her fingertips, a mindless exercise to occupy her hands, when she heard it. A sound, so faint she at first thought it was a trick of her own straining ears. Not the heavy, authoritative sound of the bolt on her door, but a soft, metallic click from another direction. A scratching.

She froze, holding her breath. The sound came again, from the wall to her left. A scrape, then a soft thud, as if a small, heavy object had been set down. Silence. Elara pressed her ear to the cold stone. Nothing. Had she imagined it?

Then a voice, a harsh, sibilant whisper that seemed to travel through the very rock. “Prisoner. Can you hear me?”

Elara’s heart leaped into her throat. She did not reply. A trap. It had to be a trap. Another of Corveau’s games.

“Answer me, you fool,” the whisper came again, laced with impatience. “Do you want to rot in here forever? I have not much time.”

The voice was familiar. She strained to place it, and then it came to her. It was Malvol, the sycophantic councilor who had signed her death warrant with such vicious pleasure. What could he possibly want?

“What do you want, Malvol?” she whispered back, her lips brushing the stone.

A sigh of relief hissed through the wall. “So you are not a complete idiot. Listen closely. There is a loose stone at the base of this wall. My cellars border this dungeon. The mortar is old. Pry it loose.”

Elara’s mind reeled. Malvol? The Tribune’s creature? This made no sense. Yet, what did she have to lose? She felt along the base of the wall where the whisper had originated. Her fingers found it: a block whose edges felt crumbly and loose. Using the heel of her boot, she kicked at it, once, twice. On the third kick, it gave way with a soft crunch and tumbled inward, revealing a dark, narrow opening. A small, cloth-wrapped bundle lay just inside.

“Take it,” Malvol’s whisper urged.

Her hand trembling slightly, Elara reached through the hole and retrieved the bundle. It was heavy. Unwrapping the oilcloth, her fingers closed around the hilt of a knife; a short, wicked-looking blade, its steel dark and non-reflective. Tucked beside it was a hard crust of bread and a small flask.

“A weapon?” she whispered, her voice filled with disbelief.

“A tool,” the voice corrected. “Or a way out, if you lack the stomach for what is to come. The Tribune is a fool, ruled by his vanity. But Corveau… she is a serpent. She supplants him. She will have what she wants from you, and in doing so, she will gain yet more power in the council. I do not wish to see that happen.”

Here, then, was the truth of it. Not mercy, but politics. A grubby power struggle, and she was the fulcrum.

“And what do you ask in return for this… gift?” Elara asked, her grip tightening on the knife. It felt solid, real, a sliver of hope in the suffocating dark.

“Information,” Malvol hissed. “Corveau wants your secrets to use for her own advancement. Give them to me instead. The name of your chief confederate in the city. Tell me who you passed the schematics to. I can use that information to ruin her. To show the council that her methods are no better than mine. In return…”

“In return, you will what? Set me free?” Elara asked, a note of scorn in her whisper.

A dry, mirthless chuckle. “Do not be naive. Your fate is sealed. But I can make it… cleaner. If you cooperate, I can arrange for a swift end. A poison in your water, perhaps. You would be found in the morning. Dead, but whole. Corveau would be denied her prize, and I would have the leverage I need. It is a better bargain than the one she offers you.”

Elara was silent for a long moment, the cold hilt of the knife in her hand, the cold logic of Malvol’s proposal in her mind. To trust him was madness; not to use him was suicide. He believed she had already passed the schematics on. He did not know that they were still within the fortress walls. An idea, wild and desperate, began to form.

“The information is valuable,” she whispered back, her voice taking on a calculating edge. “Far more valuable than a quick death.”

“What are you proposing?” Malvol asked, his tone suspicious.

“I will not give you a name,” she said. “Names can be denied. I will give you something better. Something tangible. I will tell you where the schematics are hidden. You can retrieve them yourself. Proof of your own competence. A triumph you can lay at the council’s feet, making Corveau look like a bungling amateur.”

There was a long pause from the other side of the wall. She could almost hear the gears turning in his ambitious, treacherous mind. The prize was far greater than he had hoped for.

“And your price?” he finally whispered, his voice tight with greed.

“Not a knife,” Elara said, her voice dropping so low it was barely audible. “A key. The key to this manacle. And a five minute head start before you ‘discover’ the empty cell and the missing documents. I will take my own chances in the fortress corridors. If I am caught, I am caught. But you will have what you want, free and clear.”

Another silence, longer this time. It was an audacious demand, a fool’s bargain. But she was betting on the depth of his ambition, and his hatred for Madame Corveau.

Finally, the whisper came back, laden with avarice and reluctant agreement.

“Done. Be ready. Tomorrow night.”

“A strange way to the cells, is it not?” Sir Norman remarked, with a lightness he was far from feeling.

“Her majesty's orders were for a dungeon,” the first guard grunted, a man with a face like a block of granite. “The antechamber to the north turret serves as such when the lower cells are full. It has a stout door and no windows.”

“How considerate,” Sir Norman replied, his mind racing. An antechamber; not a true cell deep within the earth. His hopes, which had been utterly extinguished, flickered with a faint, uncertain light. The queen's intervention had been so bizarre, so contrary to her icy indifference, that it could not be without motive. Was this it? A chance, however slim? He dared not let the thought take root, for disappointment would be a crueler blow than the headsman's axe.

They reached the top of the stairs and proceeded down another hall, this one hung with faded tapestries depicting grim hunting scenes, the woven eyes of stags and boars seeming to follow them from the walls. At the very end, a heavy, iron-studded oak door was set deep into the stone. The second guard, a younger and leaner man, produced a great iron key from his belt and labored to turn it in the rusty lock. With a groan of protest, the lock yielded, and the door swung inward, revealing a small, circular room, utterly bare save for a single wooden stool and the thick dust of disuse that lay upon everything.

“In you go,” said the first guard, with a shove that was not altogether unkindly. “Make your peace. You have not long.”

Sir Norman stepped across the threshold, and the door was immediately slammed shut behind him. The heavy bar on the outside dropped into its socket with a deafening thud, a sound of absolute finality. He was alone, entombed in the silence of the north turret. For a long moment, he simply stood there, listening to the guards' footsteps recede down the corridor until they were swallowed by the immense quiet of the castle. He was a prisoner again, his brief reprieve over, his fate merely postponed by half an hour. He walked the small circumference of his prison, running his hands over the cold, unyielding stone. As the guard had said, there were no windows, only the sealed and barred door. Escape was impossible.

He sat upon the stool and bowed his head, a wave of true despair washing over him for the first time. It was not the dying he minded so much as the manner of it; to be caught like a rat in a trap, to be dispatched as a footnote in the grotesque comedy of this midnight court, it was an ignominious end for a Kingsley. He thought of the queen, her face a mask of frozen beauty. She had saved him from the block for a moment, only to toss him into this stone box to await the same fate alongside another. Why the delay? Was it mere caprice? A petty show of power over her hideous consort? Or was there some deeper game at play, a game so subtle and cruel that he was but a blind counter being moved across the board?

It was then that he noticed it. A slight discrepancy in the stonework near the floor, a darker line where the mortar should have been uniform. His heart gave a great leap. He knelt, his fingers tracing the faint outline. It was a panel, cunningly disguised, so perfectly fitted that only the most desperate or discerning eye would have marked it. With trembling hands, he pressed upon one edge. Lying on a dusty shelf inside was a single object: a long, slender rapier, its hilt wrapped in worn black leather, its blade gleaming even in the faint light. Beside it lay a heavy, brocaded purse and a folded piece of parchment. Sir Norman stared, dumbfounded. This was no whim; this was a plan, intricate and carefully prepared.

He snatched up the parchment and unfolded it. On it was a crude but effective map of a section of the castle, drawn in a fine, feminine hand. A series of corridors and stairways were marked, leading from a point labeled "Here" to a small cross marked "Postern Gate - West Tower." Beneath the map were a few short, hastily scrawled words. "My husband's new playthings have grown tiresome. He seeks to build an army. These are his confederates. See that King Charles learns of their treason. The gate will be unbarred for one hour. Do not fail me." Below this was a list of a half-dozen names, names of powerful dukes and earls, men known to be discontented but whose loyalty to the crown had not, until now, been openly questioned.

Sir Norman read the list, and a cold shock ran through him. This was not just a list of traitors; it was a death warrant for some of the most powerful men in the kingdom. The queen was not just helping him escape; she was using him as a weapon, as a messenger to carry a declaration of civil war to the King. The poisoning of the Duchess of Sutherland, Lord Ashley's murder, his own trial--it was all part of some vast, Byzantine power struggle within this court of monsters. He was a pawn, yes, but a pawn who had just been given the power to topple queens and kings. He wasted no more time in thought. He buckled the sword belt around his waist, the familiar weight of it a profound comfort. He slung the purse, heavy with coin, over his shoulder. He tucked the precious, damning list inside his doublet, took one last look at the empty antechamber, and slipped through the secret panel, pulling the stone door shut behind him. He found himself in a narrow, pitch-black passage, thick with the smell of dust and disuse. According to the map, this passage should run behind the walls of the great hall and lead to a servants' staircase. He drew the rapier, its needle-sharp point a small comfort in the suffocating darkness, and began to move forward, one hand on the wall, into the unknown heart of the castle, a free man once more, and a far more dangerous one than he had been when he entered. His trial was over, but his true purpose, it seemed, was just beginning. The queen had given him his life, but she had bought with it a service, and Sir Norman Kingsley was a man who always paid his debts.

The passage was narrower than a coffin, and the air within it was stale and dead, tasting of stone dust and the faint, unsettling sweetness of decay, as if it had not been disturbed in a century. He moved with the utmost care, his left hand trailing along the rough-hewn wall, the point of his rapier extended before him into the utter blackness. The map, now committed to memory, had been a simple thing of lines and turns, but in this suffocating void, every footfall was an act of faith. He could hear the faint, muffled sounds of the castle through the stone; a distant burst of laughter from the hall, the clatter of a dropped plate, the far-off shouting of a command. These noises were his only compass, reminding him that the world of the living, however perilous, was only a few inches of stone away.

He gave it a gentle pull, and a section of the wall, disguised as paneling on the other side, swung open with a barely audible creak. He peered through the crack. He was, as the map promised, at the top of a tight, winding servants' staircase, spiraling down into the lower regions of the castle. The stairwell was dimly lit by a single, foul-smelling tallow candle set in a niche in the wall, its flame dancing in the drafts. Below, he could hear the hushed, hurried footsteps of servants. This was a far more dangerous part of his journey; the secret passage had afforded him solitude, but here he was liable to meet someone at every turn.

He held his breath and listened. The footsteps faded. Taking his chance, he slipped from the passage, closed the panel behind him, and began his descent. He moved like a phantom, his soft leather boots making no sound on the worn stone steps. The staircase seemed to go on forever, twisting down into the bowels of the fortress. Twice he was forced to press himself into a shadowy alcove as servants scurried past, their faces pale and anxious in the gloom, their arms laden with trays of food or bundles of linen. They paid him no mind, their own fears and duties all-consuming in this place of sudden death and capricious favor.

According to his mental map, he was to descend two full levels and then exit into a corridor that led toward the kitchens. From there, a passage used for hauling supplies would take him to the base of the West Tower. It was a circuitous route, designed to avoid the main thoroughfares and guard posts. He counted the landings as he went, his heart a steady drum against his ribs. The air grew warmer, thick with the smells of roasting meat, spilled wine, and unwashed bodies. He had reached the kitchen level.

He found the door, a plain, battered plank of wood, and cautiously opened it a crack. He looked out upon a long, stone corridor, bustling with a chaotic energy that was a world away from the silent disdain of the court above. Here, scullery maids with reddened hands hurried by, and burly men rolled barrels of ale, their curses echoing off the low ceiling. It was a place of frantic, sweaty work, the engine room of the magnificent feast he had so recently attended. To walk through here as he was, a gentleman in fine, albeit travel-stained, attire, with a rapier at his hip, would be to invite immediate and unwanted attention. He needed a disguise, or at least a distraction.

As he watched, a harried-looking steward bustled past, shouting at a pair of pot-boys. An idea, born of pure audacity, came to him. He waited until the corridor was momentarily clear, then stepped out, pulling the door to the stairwell shut behind him. He then picked up an empty wine flagon from a side table and set off down the corridor, walking with the hurried, slightly stooped gait of a man on an urgent errand.

It was a passable impersonation. Several people glanced at him, but their eyes slid away, seeing only another cog in the great machine. He kept his head down, his gaze fixed on the flagstones ahead, and made for the far end of the hall, where a wide, arched passage promised a route towards the West Tower. He was almost there when a voice, sharp and imperious, cut through the din.

“You, there! Stop!”

Sir Norman froze, his blood turning to ice. He did not turn. To look would be to confirm he was the one being addressed. He took another step, as if he had not heard.

“I said stop! Boy! Do you defy me?”

The voice was closer now. He had no choice. Slowly, he turned. Standing not ten feet away were two of the queen's guards, the very same men who had escorted him to the turret. But they were not looking at him. Their attention was fixed on a small, trembling figure they were dragging between them: a woman, her face ashen with terror, her fine silk gown torn at the shoulder. It was Lady Castlemaine.

Her trial, it seemed, had been even swifter than his own. She was being taken to the dungeons, or perhaps directly to the Black Chamber itself. Her eyes, wide and frantic, darted about the corridor, seeking a hope that did not exist. For a moment, her gaze met Sir Norman's. There was no recognition in them, only the raw, animal panic of the condemned.

The head guard, the man with the granite face, pointed a gauntleted finger, not at Sir Norman, but at a terrified kitchen boy who had stopped to gawk. “Get back to your work, you worthless whelp, before I give you a taste of the flat of my sword!”

The boy scurried away, and the guards resumed their grim task, dragging the half-fainting lady towards the same stairwell Sir Norman had just descended. His path was clear. He could turn and walk away, leaving her to her fate. It was the sensible thing to do. His mission, the list in his doublet, was of paramount importance to the kingdom. To intervene now would be madness, a suicidal act of chivalry that would accomplish nothing but his own recapture and death. The queen had given him an order: “Do not fail me.”

He watched them go, the woman's sobs growing fainter. And yet, he could not move. The image of her face, the sheer, helpless terror in her eyes, burned in his mind. He had shot a highwayman to save a stranger; could he now stand by and watch a woman be dragged to her execution, however guilty she might be? The name of Kingsley, which he had been so loath to see dishonored by a spy's death, would be utterly blackened by such an act of cowardice. His debt was to the queen, yes; but he had an older, deeper debt to his own conscience.

With a curse under his breath, he made his decision. It was a fool's choice, and he knew it, but it was the only one he could make and still live with himself for the few moments of life that might remain to him. He dropped the flagon, which shattered on the stone floor, the sound loud as a pistol shot in the suddenly quiet corridor.

“What in the devil's name was that?” the granite-faced guard snarled, spinning around.

Sir Norman was already moving. He strode towards them, pulling the grimy apron from his waist. “A regrettable accident,” he said, his voice once again that of a nobleman, cool and clear. “But I find I have business with you gentlemen.”

The guards stared, their jaws dropping in disbelief as they recognized the prisoner they had locked in the north turret not fifteen minutes prior.

“You!” the second guard gasped. “How--”

He never finished the question. Sir Norman's rapier was out, a flicker of light in the gloom, and its point was at the man's throat before he could even draw his own weapon. “Release the lady,” Sir Norman said, his voice dangerously soft. “And I may be inclined to let you live long enough to explain to the dwarf how you managed to misplace two prisoners in a single evening.”

The granite-faced guard, recovering from his shock, reacted not with fear, but with a bellow of rage. He shoved Lady Castlemaine aside, where she fell in a heap against the wall, and drew his own heavy broadsword. “You'll die here, you cursed spy!” he roared, and lunged.

It was the attack of a brute, powerful but clumsy. Sir Norman, trained in the finer, faster arts of fencing, sidestepped the ponderous blow with ease, the broadsword scraping sparks from the stone wall where his head had been a second before. While the guard was still recovering his balance, Sir Norman's rapier darted out like a striking serpent, not to kill, but to disable. The needle-point pierced the guard's sword arm, just below the shoulder. The man howled in pain and fury, his arm going numb, and the broadsword clattered to the floor.

The second guard, seeing his companion disarmed, tried to push past Sir Norman to raise the alarm, his own sword half-drawn from its scabbard. But Sir Norman was too quick. He spun, his rapier now held in a reverse grip, and brought the heavy pommel crashing down on the man's temple. The guard's eyes rolled up in his head, and he collapsed like a sack of grain, unconscious before he hit the ground.

The first guard, clutching his bleeding arm, stared at Sir Norman with a mixture of hatred and disbelief. “The castle will be roused! You'll never escape!”

“That is a problem for another minute,” Sir Norman replied, picking up the fallen broadsword. He held the point of it to the man's chest. “Now, you have a choice. You can lie here and bleed, and wait for your masters to find you, or you can take your friend and disappear. I would advise the latter. The queen may have spared me, but I doubt she will be so merciful with incompetent jailers.”

The guard's eyes widened at the mention of the queen. A flicker of understanding, and of terror, crossed his face. He looked from Sir Norman, to his unconscious comrade, to his bleeding arm, and made his choice. Without another word, he gritted his teeth, heaved his partner's limp body over his good shoulder, and staggered away down the corridor, disappearing into the shadows.

Sir Norman stood for a moment, his chest heaving, the adrenaline of the short, sharp fight singing in his veins. The corridor was empty again, save for him and the woman huddled on the floor. He went to her and offered a hand. “My lady Castlemaine, I presume? It seems our executions have both been postponed.”

She looked up at him, her beautiful face streaked with tears and dirt. She took his hand, her own trembling like a trapped bird. “Who are you?” she whispered, her voice hoarse. “Are you an angel, or a demon sent to torment me further?”

“Neither, madam,” said Sir Norman, pulling her to her feet. “Merely a fellow traveler in this infernal place. My name is Sir Norman Kingsley, and I intend to leave this castle tonight. If you wish to live, I suggest you come with me.”

She stared at him, a dawning horror in her eyes. “Kingsley? The man who was to be tried before me? But you were condemned! I heard the sentence!”

“Reports of my demise, as you can see, were somewhat premature,” he said with a wry smile, retrieving his rapier and wiping the guard's blood from its blade on the hem of his coat. “Now, we have very little time. The alarm will be raised soon. The West Tower, and a gate to freedom, await us. Will you come?”

For a moment she hesitated, looking back down the corridor from which the guards had fled, as if considering returning to her fate. Then she looked at the cold, determined set of Sir Norman's jaw, at the deadly steel in his hand, and at the impossible, fleeting chance he represented. Her expression hardened, a flicker of her own noble resolve returning to her eyes.

“Yes,” she said, her voice stronger now. “Yes, I will come. Anywhere is better than this place.”

“Very well,” Sir Norman said, turning toward the arched passage to the west. “Stay close, and make no sound. Our journey has just become twice as perilous.” And together, the condemned spy and the convicted poisoner plunged deeper into the labyrinth of the castle, their only hope a scrawled map, a single sword, and the desperate courage of those with nothing left to lose.

The guards advanced, their swords glinting. Sir Norman shoved Lady Castlemaine behind him and drew his own rapier, its slender blade looking hopelessly fragile against the guards’ heavy broadswords. His heart sank. He was a skilled swordsman, but he was one against two, and weary from his ordeal. It was a hopeless fight. But Lady Castlemaine was no longer the swooning, terrified woman from the dungeons. A desperate fire had been lit in her eyes. While the guards’ attention was fixed on Sir Norman, her gaze darted around the armory, searching for a weapon, any weapon. Her eyes fell upon a heavy crossbow, lying on a trestle table with a quiver of bolts beside it. She had never fired such a thing in her life, but she had seen it done in countless royal hunts. With trembling hands, she snatched it up, fumbled to load a bolt, and raised it. “Stand back!” she cried, her voice high and strained, but carrying a note of command that made even the duke pause for a second. The guards hesitated, glancing from the point of Sir Norman’s rapier to the menacing iron tip of the crossbow bolt. In that moment of hesitation, Sir Norman lunged. His blade was a blur of silver, deflecting one guard’s clumsy swing and sliding under his guard with a vicious thrust. The man gasped, his sword clattering to the floor as he crumpled. The second guard, seeing his comrade fall, roared in fury and charged. Sir Norman met him, their blades ringing together in a deadly dance.

The duke, seeing the fight turning against him, drew his own sword and advanced on Lady Castlemaine. “You foolish wench!” he snarled. “You’ll pay for that!” She held the crossbow steady, its weight making her arms tremble, the point aimed squarely at his chest. He faltered, his bravado wavering in the face of this unexpected resistance. He was no hero, and had no wish to be skewered by a desperate woman. It was then that Sir Norman, locked in his struggle with the remaining guard, saw his chance. With a powerful twist, he disarmed his opponent, sending the man’s broadsword flying across the room. It clattered against the wall and fell to the floor near the winch. He held the point of it to the man's chest. “Now, you have a choice. You can lie here and bleed, and wait for your masters to find you, or you can take your friend and disappear. I would advise the latter. The queen may have spared me, but I doubt she will be so merciful with incompetent jailers.” The guard's eyes widened at the mention of the queen. A flicker of understanding, and of terror, crossed his face. He looked from Sir Norman, to his unconscious comrade, to his bleeding arm, and made his choice. Without another word, he gritted his teeth, heaved his partner's limp body over his good shoulder, and staggered away down the corridor, disappearing into the shadows. Sir Norman stood for a moment, his chest heaving, the adrenaline of the short, sharp fight singing in his veins. The corridor was empty again, save for him and the woman huddled on the floor. He went to her and offered a hand. “My lady Castlemaine, I presume? It seems our executions have both been postponed.” She looked up at him, her beautiful face streaked with tears and dirt. She took his hand, her own trembling like a trapped bird. “Who are you?” she whispered, her voice hoarse. “Are you an angel, or a demon sent to torment me further?” “Neither, madam,” said Sir Norman, pulling her to her feet. “Merely a fellow traveler in this infernal place. My name is Sir Norman Kingsley, and I intend to leave this castle tonight. If you wish to live, I suggest you come with me.” She stared at him, a dawning horror in her eyes. “Kingsley? The man who was to be tried before me? But you were condemned! I heard the sentence!” “Reports of my demise, as you can see, were somewhat premature,” he said with a wry smile, retrieving his rapier and wiping the guard's blood from its blade on the hem of his coat. “Now, we have very little time. The alarm will be raised soon. The West Tower, and a gate to freedom, await us. Will you come?” For a moment she hesitated, looking back down the corridor from which the guards had fled, as if considering returning to her fate. Then she looked at the cold, determined set of Sir Norman's jaw, at the deadly steel in his hand, and at the impossible, fleeting chance he represented. Her expression hardened, a flicker of her own noble resolve returning to her eyes. “Yes,” she said, her voice stronger now. “Yes, I will come. Anywhere is better than this place.” “Very well,” Sir Norman said, turning toward the arched passage to the west. “Stay close, and make no sound. Our journey has just become twice as perilous.” And together, the condemned spy and the convicted poisoner plunged deeper into the labyrinth of the castle, their only hope a scrawled map, a single sword, and the desperate courage of those with nothing left to lose.

The corridors of the lower castle were a thing of stone and shadow, a place where the air hung thick and cold with the exhalations of damp rock and the faint, miasmatic odor of despair. Torches, set in iron sconces at long intervals, did little to pierce the gloom, but rather seemed to gather it about them, creating pockets of writhing darkness in which a legion of specters might have been hiding. Their footfalls, though they trod with the utmost care, seemed to echo with unnatural loudness in the profound silence, a silence broken only by the distant, rhythmic drip of water into some unseen cistern, a sound like the slow and patient ticking of a clock counting down their final moments. Lady Castlemaine clung to the arm Sir Norman had not offered her, her fingers a knot of ice and terror against the rough wool of his coat; and with every skittering sound from the black recesses of the walls, she started like a frightened deer, her breath catching in a little sob.

“Courage, madam,” Sir Norman whispered, his voice a low vibration near her ear, for he dared not speak any louder. “A fit of the vapors now will do for us both more surely than the headsman’s axe. We must be as ghosts, and ghosts, you will recall, are silent.”

“It is easy for you to speak of courage,” she returned, her whisper a tremulous thing, fraught with tears. “You are a soldier, a man of action; I am but a woman, and one who has seen nothing but drawing rooms and garden parties until this night of horrors. They say I poisoned the duchess, but I swear to you, Sir Norman, I did no such thing! She was my friend!”

“I believe you,” he said, and to his own surprise, he found that he did. There was a genuine terror in her, but not the terror of a hardened murderess caught in her crime; it was the bewildered panic of a dove fallen into a den of vipers. “This court has little use for truth. But save your story for a freer air, my lady. For now, we must think only of putting these walls behind us.”

He consulted the crumpled map, holding it in a sliver of faint torchlight that fell through a high, barred window, a window that showed only a patch of starless, inky sky. The drawing was crude, a spiderweb of lines and cryptic marks, and the passage they sought, leading to the base of the West Tower, seemed to be blocked by what the cartographer had ominously labeled the “Duke’s Kennels.” Sir Norman’s lip curled in distaste; he could well imagine what manner of beasts that sycophantic lord would choose to keep.

As they crept forward, a low growling sound reached their ears, confirming his suspicions, and it was accompanied by another sound, far more chilling--the sound of drunken laughter and a coarse, cruel voice that Sir Norman recognized at once. It was the duke himself, evidently enjoying some late night sport. They flattened themselves into a shallow alcove, the damp stone chilling them to the bone, as the duke and two of his cronies staggered into view, their path lit by a lurching lantern.

“--a fine chase, I tell you!” the duke was braying, his face flushed with wine. “The little rabbit ran for a goodly while, but my hounds are swift, are they not? A pity her majesty’s new lapdog, this Sir Norman, was not here to see it. He seems to have a taste for impertinence, and my Bracken has a way of silencing impertinent tongues!”

The three men roared with laughter as a great, black hound with eyes like hot coals strained at the leash in the duke’s hand, its jowls slathered in foam. Lady Castlemaine let out a gasp so faint it was little more than a thought, but the hound’s head whipped around, its growl deepening to a chest-rattling rumble.

“What is it, my beauty?” the duke slurred, tugging on the leash. “Smell another rabbit in the dark?”

Sir Norman’s hand flew to the hilt of his rapier, his knuckles white. To be discovered now, by this buffoon, would be an ignominy worse than death. The hound took a step toward their hiding place, its nose twitching, and the duke, his interest piqued, followed its lead. The lantern’s light began to creep into their alcove, painting a stripe of yellow across Lady Castlemaine’s terrified face. In that moment of direst peril, Sir Norman did the only thing he could; he reached into his coat, his fingers closing around the small, heavy purse he had taken from the highwayman he had shot hours before--an act that seemed a lifetime ago. With a flick of his wrist, he tossed the purse down the corridor in the opposite direction. It landed with a heavy, metallic clatter on the flagstones, a sound that no one, drunk or sober, could possibly ignore.

“What’s that?” one of the duke’s companions exclaimed, peering into the darkness.

“Gold, by the sound of it!” the other shouted.

The duke, for whom greed was a passion second only to cruelty, forgot the hound and the rabbit in an instant. “Fools! It is mine!” he bellowed, dropping the leash and scrambling after the purse with all the dignity of a pig rooting for truffles. His companions, with a similar lack of ceremony, shoved past him to join the hunt, and in a moment the three of them were a tangle of limbs and curses, fighting over the scattered coins. The great black hound, momentarily free, gave a confused bark and then, with the simple loyalty of its kind, trotted after its master to see what the commotion was about.

“Now,” Sir Norman breathed, and seizing Lady Castlemaine’s hand, he pulled her from the alcove and they sped past the brawling noblemen, their escape entirely unnoticed. They did not stop, nor did they dare to look back, until they had put two turnings of the corridor and a heavy wooden door between themselves and the Duke’s Kennels. Lady Castlemaine leaned against the wall, her chest heaving, her hand pressed to her heart.

“That was… that was…” she stammered, unable to find the words.

“A fortunate diversion,” Sir Norman finished for her, a grim smile touching his lips. “It seems the man I had the pleasure of shooting this eve has done me a second service from beyond the grave. Come, we are close now. The map shows a spiral stair just ahead.”

They found it behind a hanging tapestry so thick with dust and neglect that it seemed to have been woven from shadows and cobwebs. The stairwell was narrow, dark, and filled with a palpable cold, the steps slick with a fine, greasy damp. It wound upward into blackness, a helical descent into hell if one were going the other way. Sir Norman took the lead, his rapier held forth to probe the darkness, his feet testing each step before he committed his weight, while Lady Castlemaine followed so closely behind that he could feel her breath upon his neck. The climb was long and dizzying, and the silence was so complete that the frantic beating of their own hearts sounded like war drums in their ears.

After what seemed an eternity of climbing, the oppressive dark began to give way to a faint, silvery luminescence from above. A breath of cool, fresh night air, a scent as intoxicating as any perfume, drifted down to them, promising an end to their subterranean journey. They emerged at last into a small, circular chamber at the very top of the tower. It was empty, save for a thick carpet of dust and three tall, arched windows. And through the central window, which stood open to the night, was a view that made them both stop in their tracks.

They were high above the main body of the castle, and below them, spread out like a living tapestry, was a scene of the most surreal and fantastic revelry. The grand courtyard had been transformed into an open-air ballroom, lit by a thousand torches and braziers that cast a wild, flickering glow upon the proceedings. The strange, silent court was no longer silent; a dissonant orchestra of lutes, viols, and strange, reedy pipes played a tune that was at once merry and mad, a frantic dance on the edge of a volcano. The nobles and their gauzy nymphs spun and capered in a bizarre parody of a formal ball, their movements jerky and puppet-like, their laughter sharp and brittle on the night air.

And in the center of it all, seated upon two gilded thrones placed on a dais, were the queen and her consort. From this height, Sir Norman could not make out their expressions, but he could see the rigid stillness of the queen, a figure of ice in a sea of fire, and the animated, grotesque gesturing of the dwarf beside her, who seemed to be directing the whole chaotic affair like a malevolent conductor.

“My God,” whispered Lady Castlemaine, her voice filled with awe and horror. “They are all quite mad.”

“Madness is the coin of this realm, my lady,” Sir Norman said, his gaze fixed on the scene below. His eyes searched the crowd, and his heart gave a sudden, painful jolt. For a moment, he thought he saw her. Not the cold queen on the throne, but the girl she had been, the Miranda he remembered from a time before crowns and monstrous husbands. A memory, sharp and vivid as a shard of glass, pierced the fog of his reckless despair: a moonlit garden, the scent of roses, and a gentle hand staying his own as he prepared to fight a duel he could not possibly win. “You are too young and too foolish to die for such a small matter of honor,” she had told him, her eyes, then, not cold but full of a fiery concern. She had saved him then, with a word and a smile. Tonight, she had saved him with a fit of pique and royal boredom. The irony was so bitter it almost made him laugh.

His reverie was shattered by a sudden, harsh blare of a trumpet from the courtyard below, a sound that cut through the manic music and silenced it instantly. All motion ceased. Every face in the courtyard turned as one toward the main keep. Sir Norman’s blood ran cold. The alarm. The two jailers must have been discovered, or their own absence from the dungeons noted.

He grabbed Lady Castlemaine’s arm. “The map,” he said, his voice urgent. “It shows a way from this very window. A buttress, leading to the outer wall.”

He leaned out of the window, the wind whipping his hair. The view was terrifying. The stone arch of the flying buttress was a narrow, precarious bridge arcing out over a dizzying drop. It connected the tower to the crenelated top of the castle’s outer curtain wall, which seemed a world away. To attempt such a crossing in the dark, with the wind plucking at them, was an act of lunacy.

“We cannot!” Lady Castlemaine cried, pulling back from the abyss. “I will fall! It is certain death!”

“To stay is certain death,” Sir Norman countered, his face a grim mask in the moonlight. “The dwarf will have his hounds on our scent in minutes. Look!”

He pointed down. Already, figures with torches were swarming from the doors of the keep, their purpose clear. The hunt had begun. The mad ball was over. From the dais, the dwarf had risen and was pointing a stubby finger directly up at their tower. A great roar went up from the crowd below, a sound of bloodlust and anticipation.

“There is no other choice,” Sir Norman said, his voice hard but not unkind. He looked at her, at the absolute terror in her eyes, and a sliver of pity pierced his own desperate resolve. “I will go first. Do not look down. Keep your eyes on me, and place your feet where I have placed mine. I will not let you fall.”

Without waiting for her reply, for he knew that any hesitation now would be fatal, he swung a leg over the sill. His boot found purchase on the beginning of the buttress. The stone was wider than it had looked from within, but it was slick with night dew and crumbled at the edges. The wind tore at him, a physical force trying to push him into the chasm. For a moment, he swayed, his heart in his throat, and the upturned faces in the courtyard below became a blur of pale, hungry moons. Then he found his balance, flattened himself against the cold stone of the tower, and turned back to the window, his hand outstretched.

“Now, my lady,” he said, his voice barely audible above the rising wind and the clamor from below. “It is now or never.”

Lady Castlemaine stared at his outstretched hand, then at the yawning blackness below, and a whimper of pure animal terror escaped her lips. The wind tore at the fine silk of her gown, whipping it about her legs as if it were some hungry, invisible beast, and the shouts from the courtyard grew louder, more distinct, a baying chorus lusting for their blood. A crossbow bolt suddenly thudded into the stone casement just beside her head, showering her with chips of granite and a fine, gritty dust. The shock of it was a slap, a violent recall to the reality of their position.

“My lady, now!” Sir Norman’s voice was a low command, cutting through the gale. “That was but a ranging shot; the next will find its mark!”

Her eyes, wide and dark with dread, flew from the bolt to his face, a mask of grim determination set against the roiling clouds and the sliver of a hostile moon. In him, she saw not a savior, but a fellow damned soul offering the barest chance of a slightly less immediate end. With a sob that was half a gasp, she hitched up her cumbersome skirts, placed a trembling hand in his, and swung one leg over the sill, her satin slipper scrabbling for purchase on the precipice. His grip was like iron, his fingers wrapping around hers with a strength that was both reassuring and terrifying.

“Do not look down,” he repeated, his voice close to her ear. “Watch my feet. Only my feet. There is a pattern to the stones. One wide, one narrow. Step where I step.”

He began to move, a slow, agonizing shuffle sideways along the arc of stone. Lady Castlemaine followed, her breath coming in ragged bursts, her heart a frantic drum against her ribs. The buttress, which from the window had seemed a solid bridge, was in truth a treacherous path of weathered, moss slicked blocks, some of which shifted minutely under their weight. The abyss to their left was a dizzying vortex of shadow, while to their right, the cold, sheer face of the tower offered no handholds, only the unyielding chill of ancient stone. The torches in the courtyard below were like angry, swarming fireflies, and the jeering voices floated up to them on the currents of the wind, distorted and ghoulish.

“Faster!” a voice screamed from below; it was the dwarf, his high, piercing shriek unmistakable even at this distance. “A hundred gold pieces to the man who brings him down! Two hundred for the woman!”

Another bolt sang past them, so close that Lady Castlemaine felt the wind of its passage on her cheek, and she cried out, stumbling. Her foot slipped on a patch of damp moss. For one heart stopping moment, she dangled over the void, her entire weight held by Sir Norman’s unyielding grip. Her scream was lost in the wind. The upturned faces in the courtyard became a sea of pale, expectant discs.

Sir Norman did not hesitate; he braced himself, planting his feet wide, and with a grunt of immense effort, he hauled her back from the brink. He pulled her flush against him, her body trembling uncontrollably, her face buried in the rough tweed of his shoulder. They stood there, plastered to the side of the tower, two figures clinging to life on a sliver of stone high above the world.

“I cannot,” she wept, her voice muffled against him. “I cannot go on. Let me go. It is better than this.”

“It is not,” he said, his voice a harsh rasp. “A fall is a quick end; what they have planned for you is not. We are halfway there. Look.” He forced her head up, pointing not down, but ahead, to where the buttress met the solid block of the outer wall’s battlements. “It is not a league, my lady, but a few more paces. Courage. A little more courage, and we will cheat the monster of his sport.”

His words, or perhaps the sheer finality in his tone, seemed to stiffen her resolve. She nodded, a tiny, jerky movement, and without another word, she pushed away from him and took a deliberate step forward. He followed a half pace behind, a human shield between her and the next crossbow bolt, his hand hovering near the small of her back, ready to steady her, but not touching. She moved now with a strange, automaton’s grace, her eyes fixed on the goal ahead, her terror seemingly burned away and replaced by a cold, brittle desperation.

They reached the crenelated wall in another dozen agonizing steps and scrambled over the parapet, collapsing onto the stone walkway behind it. For a moment they lay there, gasping for air, the blessed solidity of the wide flagstones beneath them a luxury beyond all reckoning. The outer wall was a broad highway of stone, a good twelve feet across, but it offered no immediate sanctuary. To one side was the sheer drop to the moat and the dark woods beyond; to the other, the castle’s inner ward, the chaotic courtyard, and the keep from which they had just escaped. Torches were already appearing on the wall, moving toward them from the gatehouse to the north.

“Up! We cannot rest,” Sir Norman commanded, pulling himself to his feet. He scanned their surroundings, his mind racing. “They will have the walls sealed in minutes.”

Lady Castlemaine pushed herself up, her fine gown now torn and stained, her hair a wild tangle. “The map,” she said, her voice hoarse. “The map you found. Did it show a way down from this wall?”

“It showed this,” Sir Norman replied, pointing along the walkway toward a squat, round tower that served as a corner bastion for the outer defenses. “The archer’s post. There is a stairwell inside.”

They ran, hugging the inner edge of the parapet, staying in the deepest shadows cast by the merlons. The wind was still a constant pressure, but here on the wider wall it was less a threat than a cold, scouring nuisance. They heard shouts behind them, the clang of armored feet on stone. The hunt was closing in. They reached the wooden door of the bastion tower and found it barred from the outside with a heavy iron beam.

“Cursed luck!” Sir Norman hissed, throwing his shoulder against the unyielding wood. It was as solid as the wall itself.

“Here!” Lady Castlemaine cried out. She had darted to the other side of the walkway, to the outer parapet that overlooked the moat. A heavy coil of rope lay beside a small, mounted crane, a device used for hauling supplies up from the ground. “Can we use this?”

Sir Norman joined her in two strides. The rope was thick and coarse, but it felt strong. He peered over the edge. The drop was still immense, a fall of at least eighty feet, but the wall below was sheer, with no windows or projections. At the bottom, the black, still water of the moat lay like a ribbon of polished jet.

“It will have to do,” he said, already working at the knots that secured the rope. His fingers, numb with cold, fumbled with the stiff, wet hemp. The sounds of their pursuers grew louder, the torchlight flickering just a short distance down the wall.

“They are coming!” she urged, her voice tight with panic.

“I know,” he grunted, finally pulling the last knot free. He looped one end of the rope around the base of a merlon, testing it with his full weight. It held. “You first, my lady.”

“Me?” she gasped, staring at the rope that now dangled into the terrifying darkness. “I cannot! I have never…”

“There is no time for lessons,” he cut her off, his voice sharp but not unkind. “Wrap it around your wrists, twice, like so. Use your feet against the wall. Do not think, just go. I will be right behind you. I will not let you fall.” He placed the rope in her hands, his own closing over hers for a moment. “They are nearly upon us. It is this, or the dwarf’s axe. Choose.”

The choice was no choice at all. With a look of utter resignation, she gripped the rope, swung her legs over the parapet, and began a clumsy, scraping descent, her slippers finding no purchase on the smooth stone. Sir Norman watched her go for a second, then turned back to the walkway. The first of the guards, a hulking brute in a leather jerkin, was rounding the last bend, his face, illuminated by the torch he carried, a mask of brutish surprise.

Sir Norman did not wait. He seized the small crane, a heavy iron contraption on a swivel, and with a roar born of desperation, he put all his strength into swinging it around. The heavy arm of the crane caught the guard square in the chest, sending him staggering backward with a cry of pain and surprise, right into the two men behind him. They tumbled in a heap of tangled limbs and curses. It would not hold them for long.

Sir Norman scrambled over the parapet and seized the rope. He did not descend with the caution of Lady Castlemaine, but fairly slid down, the rough hemp burning his palms as he went. He passed her in a rush of air, her white, terrified face a fleeting image in the gloom, and hit the water of the moat with a jarring splash that drove the air from his lungs. The cold was a physical blow, a shock so profound it nearly stopped his heart. For a second he sank into the inky, weed choked depths, then his feet found the muddy bottom and he pushed himself back to the surface, sputtering.

A moment later, Lady Castlemaine landed beside him with a shriek and a great splash. The water, though deep, was not over their heads, and they floundered in the chest deep cold, gasping.

“This way!” he whispered, grabbing her arm and pulling her toward the opposite bank. “To the woods!”

They struggled through the clinging mud and reeds of the bank and crawled into the treeline, collapsing behind the trunk of a massive oak. They were soaked, freezing, and covered in mud, but for the moment, they were out of sight. Across the moat, the castle wall was alive with torches. Figures gestured and shouted, pointing down at the water. The dwarf himself appeared on the parapet, a tiny, silhouetted figure of incandescent rage. Even from this distance, they could hear his furious, high pitched screaming.

“He knows we are here,” Lady Castlemaine whispered, her teeth chattering violently, a sound that was both from cold and from fear. “They will send the dogs.”

“Let them,” Sir Norman said, his voice a low growl. He was peering into the dark woods around them. “The forest is vast. We must keep moving. We must put as much distance between us and this place as we can before dawn.”

He gave her a moment more to catch her breath, then pulled her to her feet. The woods were a lightless maze, the ground a treacherous carpet of roots and fallen leaves. They stumbled on, deeper and deeper into the darkness, driven by the sounds of a distant hunting horn that now echoed from the castle walls, a chilling promise of a pursuit that had only just begun. The mad revels of Prince Caliban’s court were over; the grim reality of his hunt had taken their place.

For what seemed an eternity, they ran, their lungs burning, their limbs aching. Sir Norman led the way, breaking a path through the tangled undergrowth, his hand never far from the hilt of the small sword he had taken from the jailer; a flimsy thing, but better than his bare hands. Lady Castlemaine followed, her initial terror having subsided into a kind of numb endurance. The fine lady who had trembled at the thought of a trial was gone, replaced by a survivor who scrambled over fallen logs and pushed through thorny thickets without a sound of complaint, her only guide the dark shape of the man moving before her.

At last, when they were both on the verge of collapse, he called a halt. They had reached a small, shallow stream that cut through the forest floor, gurgling over smooth stones.

“Here,” he said, his voice a ragged whisper. “We will walk in the water. It will hide our scent from the dogs, for a time.”

The water was shockingly cold, and the stones were slippery underfoot, but they waded upstream, the current tugging at their legs. All around them the forest was alive with the unseen rustlings of nocturnal creatures, and every snap of a twig, every hoot of a distant owl, sent a fresh jolt of fear through Lady Castlemaine. But Sir Norman seemed unnerved, his head cocked, listening not to the sounds of the forest, but for the sounds of their pursuers.

“I hear nothing,” he said finally, helping her onto the bank in a small, sheltered clearing where the moonlight filtered down through a break in the canopy. “We have bought ourselves a few minutes.”

He sank down with his back against a tree, pulling his sword from its sheath and laying it across his lap. Lady Castlemaine huddled near him, wrapping her arms around herself for warmth, her wet clothes clinging to her like a second skin. The silence, after the frantic noise of their escape, was profound.

“You saved my life,” she said, her voice barely audible. “Back there, on the wall. And on the buttress. I would be dead, or worse.”

Sir Norman merely grunted in reply, his eyes scanning the dark perimeter of the clearing.

“Why?” she pressed, inching a little closer. “You owe me nothing. In fact, you have every reason to loathe me. I was at the trial. I heard what they said of you, and I did nothing.”

“You are right,” he said, his voice flat. “I have every reason to loathe the whole pack of you. The giggling dwarf, the sycophantic nobles, the ice queen on her throne. You are all part of this waking nightmare.” He turned his head then, and his eyes, dark and unreadable in the faint light, met hers. “But I saw your face when the duke spoke of your trial. You were to be next. For poisoning the Duchess of Sutherland, was it not? You were as much a prisoner as I.”

A shudder ran through her, a tremor that had nothing to do with the cold. “It was a lie,” she whispered. “The Duchess was my friend. The dwarf… Prince Caliban… he coveted her jewels. He invented the charge, just as he invented yours.”

“I do not doubt it,” Sir Norman said grimly. “He seems a collector of false charges and death warrants. So you see, my lady, I did not save you out of any great chivalry. I merely saw a fellow rat in the same trap, and two rats have a better chance of gnawing their way out than one.”

His words were harsh, but there was no real malice in them. They were simply the unvarnished truth of their situation. She fell silent, contemplating this. He was right, of course. She had lived in the gilded cage of Queen Miranda’s court, enjoying its strange luxuries, turning a blind eye to its casual cruelties, until the moment its capricious malice had turned upon her. In that, she was no different from any of the other fawning courtiers who now hunted them through the woods.

“What will we do?” she asked at last, the enormity of their predicament settling upon her. “Where can we possibly go?”

“The map,” he said, reaching inside his sodden jacket. He withdrew the precious, waterlogged piece of parchment, handling it with great care. He unrolled it in the patch of moonlight. Much of the ink had run, but the main lines were still visible. “It shows more than the castle. It shows these woods. And it shows a path, a hidden way, that leads north, out of this cursed domain. To the King’s highway, and from there… freedom.”

He pointed a finger at a faint line that wound its way through the symbols for trees and hills. “If we can find that path before they find us, we have a chance. But it will not be easy. Caliban will have every road and track guarded. He will not suffer this humiliation lightly.”

As if to punctuate his words, the distant sound of the hunting horn echoed through the trees again, closer this time, accompanied by the faint, menacing baying of hounds. They were on their feet in an instant.

“They have picked up our trail again,” he said, his brief respite over. “The stream did not fool them for long. Come.”

He plunged back into the dark woods, moving with a renewed urgency, and she followed without question. Their shared enemy, their shared desperation, had forged a fragile, unspoken pact between them. He was no longer Sir Norman Kingsley, spy and condemned man, and she was no longer Lady Castlemaine, court beauty and accused poisoner. They were simply the hunted, and their only thought, their only purpose, was to outrun the hounds of hell that bayed at their heels.

“The map,” he said, setting her down gently. The shouts of their pursuers were much closer now.

Sir Norman stared at the weeping statue of rock, his mind working furiously. The map of the dead had led them this far. It would not abandon them now. “Where sorrow weeps stone,” he muttered. She followed his gaze. The dark streaks of water running down the rock face were not random. They formed a pattern, a series of lines and angles that looked almost deliberate. It was a map, drawn in water upon the face of the stone itself. It showed a path, leading from the base of the weeping face, down along the river, and then turning sharply inland towards a final, crudely drawn symbol. A key.

“By God,” she breathed. “A key.”

The sound of a hunting horn, sharp and triumphant, echoed from the woods just behind them. They had been seen.

“There is no time,” Sir Norman said, grabbing her hand. He looked down from the bluff. It was a perilous drop to the riverbank below, a jumble of sharp rocks and scree. “There is only one way down.”

He did not wait for her consent. Holding her tight against him, he half-jumped, half-slid down the treacherous slope, rocks scattering around them, dirt flying. They tumbled to the bottom in a heap, bruised and battered but alive, at the very edge of the rushing water. Without a moment's hesitation, he pulled her to her feet and plunged into the icy river, wading across to the other side. The cold was a physical blow, stealing their breath, but it was also a baptism, washing away their scent and their trail.

They clambered onto the opposite bank, soaked and shivering, just as the first of their pursuers, the officious duke among them, appeared on the bluff above. The duke’s face, when he saw them, was a mask of pure, apoplectic rage.

“After them!” he shrieked, his voice carrying clearly across the water. “They cannot escape! A hundred gold pieces to the man who brings me Kingsley’s head!”

Sir Norman offered him a mocking bow, then turned and, half-dragging, half-carrying the exhausted Lady Castlemaine, followed the path shown by the widow’s tears. They ran, not towards a vague hope of freedom, but towards a key; a final, tangible promise, drawn in sorrow on a face of stone. Behind them, the sounds of fury and pursuit faded, lost in the roar of the river and the promise of the coming day.

The path was no path at all, but a treacherous scramble through bracken and over stones that seemed placed by a malicious hand to trip and injure. The memory of the water-map, that fleeting chart of tears, was all Sir Norman had to guide them, a ghostly image seared into his mind’s eye. It had shown a course that clung for a time to the river, then veered sharply into the black maw of the forest that loomed before them like a gathering of giants. He kept Lady Castlemaine’s hand locked in his, his grip a thing of iron, for he knew if he let go she would fall, and if she fell, they were lost. Her breath came in ragged, painful sobs, and her fine court slippers, so ill-suited for this wilderness, were torn to shreds, her feet bleeding. Yet she did not complain, a fact which Sir Norman noted with a kind of grim approval; there was more substance to this lady than silks and accusations, it seemed.

“Can you go on?” he asked, his voice a low rasp, as they paused for a moment in the shadow of a great oak whose roots writhed from the earth like a nest of serpents.

“I must,” she gasped, leaning against the rough bark, her chest heaving. “To stop is to die. But tell me, Sir Norman, for my mind is a whirl of terror and confusion, why did you do it? He looked at her then, her face pale as bone in the strengthening dawn, her magnificent hair a tangled wreck, and a dark smudge of dirt across one cheek that somehow made her look more human than all her courtly finery. “Her majesty was kind enough to have our executions scheduled for the same hour,” he said, with a flash of his old, defiant humor. “It seemed only polite that we should share our escape as well. A shudder ran through her frame that had nothing to do with the cold. “You do not know him,” she whispered, her eyes wide with a terror that was absolute. “Caliban. He is not a man, not really. He is a wickedness given form. And the queen… Miranda… she is his keeper, and his captive. They are two halves of a poisoned whole.” She pressed a hand to her side, a stitch of pain lancing through her. “And this key you spoke of; what is it? Where does this mad path lead?”

“I know no more than you,” he admitted, turning his gaze towards the looming woods. “The map of tears ended with its symbol. It is our only hope, and so we must believe in it as a pilgrim believes in a shrine. Now, we have lingered long enough. The duke’s men will have found a way across the river by now, and their horses will make short work of this distance if we give them time.”

He helped her to her feet, and she did not protest, but leaned on his strength as they plunged from the relative open of the riverbank into the oppressive, ancient gloom of the forest. Here, the light of the new day was lost, filtered through a dense canopy of leaves that had not, it seemed, been disturbed since the world was young. The air was thick and damp, smelling of leaf-mold and decay, and a profound silence reigned, broken only by their own labored breathing and the snap of twigs under their feet. The path was harder still, a faint deer-track that wound its way between colossal trees, and Sir Norman found his own strength beginning to flag. The nervous energy of the trial and the initial flight had burned away, leaving behind a bone-deep weariness, and the icy plunge in the river had chilled him to the marrow.

They stumbled on for what seemed an eternity, lost in that green and silent twilight. The sounds of pursuit had faded entirely, replaced by a solitude so complete it was its own kind of threat. Sir Norman found his mind drifting back, in spite of himself, to the fantastic scene of the midnight court. He saw again the queen’s face, that mask of bored, cold beauty, as she signed his death warrant without a flicker of emotion, the same face that had looked upon him with something like interest when he had first been dragged into her presence. What manner of woman was she, who could save a man one moment and condemn him the next with the careless grace of a lady dropping her fan? Was she merely capricious, a tyrant amusing herself with the lives of lesser beings, or was there, as Lady Castlemaine had hinted, some deeper, darker drama at play between her and the monstrous dwarf she called her husband? He reflected that his own troubles, which had begun with a simple royalist mission and a highwayman on the road, had landed him in a world that operated by no known rules of logic or law, a waking nightmare tricked out in velvet and lit by candelabras.

It was Lady Castlemaine who saw it first. She gave a little cry and pointed a trembling finger. “Look! Sir Norman, look there!”

He lifted his head, following her gaze. Through a break in the trees, on a low rise ahead of them, stood a structure. It was a single, squat tower of grey stone, half-crumbled with age and shrouded in a thick mantle of ivy. It was utterly forlorn and desolate, yet at the sight of it, a fierce surge of hope shot through Sir Norman’s veins. For, rising from its broken top, a jagged arch of stone remained, silhouetted against the filtered light, and its shape, combined with the straight line of the tower beneath it, was unmistakably that of an ancient, warding key.

“The key,” he breathed, the words a prayer. “We have found it.”

The sight lent them a final, desperate burst of speed. They half ran, half fell up the little slope, their feet sinking into the soft, mossy earth. The air around the tower was still and heavy, as if time itself had stopped here. A low, stone doorway, like the mouth of a tomb, gaped open before them. Without hesitation, Sir Norman drew the pistol he had managed to keep tucked in his belt, the powder doubtless uselessly damp, but the weight of it a small comfort nonetheless, and stepped across the threshold, pulling Lady Castlemaine in after him.

The interior was dark and smelled of stone dust and damp earth. A single, high slit of a window let in a pale, ghostly beam of light that illuminated a small, circular chamber, empty save for a scattering of fallen stones and the thick roots of the ivy that had forced their way through the mortar. For a moment, they simply stood there, gasping for air, overwhelmed by the sudden sense of sanctuary. They were out of sight; they were sheltered. The maddening, relentless motion had stopped. Lady Castlemaine slid down the curving wall to sit upon the floor, burying her face in her hands, her shoulders shaking with silent, exhausted tears.

Sir Norman did not disturb her. He let her weep, knowing the release was necessary. His own gaze swept the chamber, searching for… he knew not what. A trap door? A hidden cache of weapons? The next clue? The room was bare, a simple guard post from a forgotten age. His hope, which had soared so high, began to curdle into disappointment. Had they run all this way, to this forsaken place, only for it to be an empty pile of stones? Was this the final, cruel jest of the map of the dead?

His eyes, adjusting to the gloom, traced the lines of the single beam of light. It fell upon the far wall, and where it struck the stone, he saw that the surface was not entirely rough-hewn. It had been smoothed, and upon that surface were carved letters, faint with age and obscured by lichen. He stepped closer, his heart beginning to beat a little faster, and used the sleeve of his coat to rub away the grime and moss. The carved words were stark and deep.

“THE KEY OPENS THE LOCK,” he read aloud. “THE CROWN LIGHTS THE PATH.”

Lady Castlemaine looked up, her tears checked by this new mystery. “The Crown?” she repeated, her voice hoarse. “What crown? King Charles’s crown?”

“No,” Sir Norman said slowly, his mind racing. “Not a king’s crown. An inn.” He looked at her, his eyes glinting in the dim light. “The Golden Crown. It was the inn where I stopped, just after my encounter with Lord Ashley on the road. It is where the dwarf said he first saw me.”

“Go back there?” she cried, scrambling to her feet in alarm. “You cannot mean it! It is the very heart of their territory! The duke, the dwarf, all of them will be searching. To go back is to walk directly into the snare!”

“Perhaps,” Sir Norman said, his gaze fixed on the inscription as if he could will it to give up more of its secrets. “And perhaps it is the one place they will not think to look for us. They will assume we have fled for the coast, or for the north. They will hunt us in the wilds. They would never believe we would have the audacity to double back, to hide ourselves in the serpent’s very nest.” He turned from the wall and faced her. “The message is clear. This tower, this Key, was but the first step. It has opened the way. But the next step, the path itself, is to be found at the Golden Crown.”

“It is madness,” she whispered, but there was less conviction in her tone now, replaced by a dawning, horrified understanding. In the inverted logic of this cursed land, such a mad plan might be the only one that made any sense.

“It is all we have,” he replied, his voice firm. He felt a strange calm settle over him, the clarity that comes when all other options have been stripped away. Their fate was not to be found in desperate flight, but in a daring, almost suicidal return. “We will rest here until midday. We are safe for a few hours at least. You must sleep, my lady, you are at the end of your strength. Then, when we have recovered somewhat, we will make our way back. Not as fugitives, but as simple travelers. We will find what we are meant to find at the Golden Crown, and then, God willing, we will find our way out of this nightmare.”

He sank down against the wall opposite her, the useless pistol still in his hand. The single beam of light moved slowly across the floor as the sun climbed higher in the sky. Outside, the forest was silent. Inside the stone key, the two fugitives, a condemned spy and an accused poisoner, sat in a shared, exhausted quiet, their strange and terrible journey only just beginning. The silence in the tower was a fragile, precious thing, a brief pause in a symphony of madness, and as Sir Norman finally allowed his eyes to close, he did not dream of freedom, but of the cold, impassive face of a queen, and the diabolical, death’s-head grin of a dwarf. The path ahead was not a flight from them, he now understood, but a journey deeper into the heart of their mystery.

The dwarf, Caliban, was a creature of pure, unadulterated malice, a being so twisted in form and spirit that his evil was almost a natural law, like the poison of a serpent or the hunger of a wolf. He remembered her eyes, those dark, lustrous orbs that seemed to hold entire galaxies of cold fire within them, and the way they had fixed upon the dwarf with such chilling aversion. There was a war in that midnight court, a silent, bitter struggle fought with glances and gestures, and he and Lady Castlemaine had, by some mischance, stumbled into the very heart of the conflict. The inscription on the wall, “The Key opens the way,” now seemed less a promise of freedom and more a formal invitation into the depths of that war.

He glanced over at Lady Castlemaine. In sleep, the terror had smoothed from her face, leaving behind a delicate beauty that seemed all the more fragile in this grim, stone chamber. Her dark hair was a spill of silk against the rough floor, and one hand was curled near her cheek, as defenseless as a child’s. A wave of profound pity, mingled with a fierce, protective instinct, washed over him. She had been accused of poisoning a duchess, a crime he found impossible to reconcile with the terrified woman who had wept in his arms. Whatever her story, she was as much a victim of this place as he was. They were bound together now, two stray souls caught in the same bewildering net. His path was her path, and the thought, which should have been a burden, brought with it a strange sort of comfort. He was no longer entirely alone in his defiance.

The sunbeam had nearly traversed the chamber when she stirred, her lashes fluttering open. For a moment, confusion clouded her features, followed by the swift, dawning horror of memory.
“Is it time?” she whispered, her voice husky with sleep and fear.
“Soon,” Sir Norman replied, his own voice gentle. “But we have rested. Drink some water. We must keep our strength.”
She did as he bade, sipping from the skin he offered, and some measure of color returned to her cheeks. She pushed her hair back from her face and looked at him, her gaze clear and direct.
“You are certain of this course? To return to that dreadful inn?”
“I am certain only that it is the one path offered to us,” he said, getting to his feet and stretching his stiff limbs. “To flee into the woods is to become common fugitives, to be hunted down by dogs and men for sport. But to walk back into the lion’s den, to sit at his table and drink his ale, that is an act of such desperate audacity they may not conceive of it. We are not fleeing, my lady; we are continuing the game.”
“The game that ends with our heads on a block,” she murmured, but she, too, rose to her feet, a new resolve hardening the soft lines of her mouth.
“Perhaps. But the game is not yet finished. We have a move to make. Come.”

Their preparations were slight, for they had nothing to prepare. Sir Norman, with a grimace of regret, tore a strip from the fine lace at his cuff to clean the barrel of the pistol, though he had no powder or shot with which to load it. He then took his cloak and deliberately scuffed it in the dust of the floor, rubbing grime onto his breeches and the shoulders of his coat.
“What are you doing?” Lady Castlemaine asked, her brow furrowed.
“Destroying Sir Norman Kingsley, a spy of the king,” he answered, a grim smile touching his lips. “And creating a weary traveler, too poor to be of interest and too tired to be a threat. You, my lady, must do the same.”
She looked down at her own gown, a rich brocade now stained and torn from their flight. With a deep breath, she took the hem and deliberately ripped it further, smudging her pale cheeks with the dust from her hands until she looked less like a lady of the court and more like a poor player’s wife, harried and worn. The transformation was startling, and in its own way, heartbreaking.
“Will it be enough?” she asked, her voice small.
“It will have to be,” he said, offering her his arm with a theatrical bow that was a mockery of their former lives. “Shall we, my good woman?”
A flicker of a genuine smile, the first he had seen from her, answered him, and together they descended the winding stair, leaving the silent, sunlit chamber of the Key behind them.

The forest that greeted them was of a different character under the high sun of midday. The shadows were shorter, the oppressive gloom somewhat lifted, and yet the silence was just as profound, just as unnatural. No birds sang, no squirrels chattered in the branches; the only sound was the crunch of their own footfalls on the thick carpet of dead leaves. Sir Norman led them, not by any path, for there was none, but by keeping the sun to their right, guessing at the direction from which they had come. He walked with a steady, unhurried pace, his hand never far from the hilt of his sword, his eyes scanning the impenetrable walls of green on either side.
“If this is their domain,” Lady Castlemaine said, her voice a low murmur, “where are its people? Its villages? I have seen nothing but this endless wood since I was brought here.”
“I believe this forest is a sort of wall,” Sir Norman mused, “a borderland. The true heart of their kingdom, if it can be called such, must lie beyond. The inn sits upon its very edge, a gateway for those few who stumble in, and for the queen’s men to ride out. It is a trap, disguised as a sanctuary.”
“And we are walking back to the bait,” she sighed.
“Precisely.”

They walked for what felt like several hours, the silence pressing in on them, a heavy blanket that muffled the world. Twice, Sir Norman stopped, holding up a hand and listening intently. He had heard something, or felt it; a subtle shift in the air, the faintest impression of being watched. But the woods remained still and empty to the eye. Whether the danger was real or a phantom of his own strained nerves, he could not say, but it served to keep them sharp, to remind them that every step was a gamble.

At last, when the afternoon sun began to dip toward the west, casting long, distorted shadows that turned the trees into grasping specters, a new sound reached them: the distant, rhythmic thud of an axe striking wood.
“We are close,” Sir Norman whispered, his grip tightening on his companion’s arm. “Someone is cutting firewood. That means a hearth, and a hearth means a dwelling.”
They moved more cautiously now, flitting from the shadow of one great oak to the next. The chopping grew louder, and soon they could smell the faint, acrid scent of woodsmoke on the air. Pushing aside a low hanging branch of yew, Sir Norman peered out. Not fifty yards away stood the Golden Crown.

It was a large, rambling structure of dark timber and wattle-and-daub, with a sagging slate roof and windows like squinting, suspicious eyes. A curl of smoke rose from a great stone chimney, and a crudely painted sign, depicting a tarnished and lopsided crown, creaked mournfully in the slight breeze. At the side of the building, a burly, unshaven man in a leather jerkin was splitting logs with a methodical, almost sullen rhythm. The place had an air of brooding neglect, yet there were three horses tied to a hitching post near the front door, their flanks steaming in the cool air; they were not alone.
“Now comes the test,” Sir Norman breathed, so low that Lady Castlemaine could barely hear him. “Do not speak unless you are spoken to. Keep your eyes down. You are my weary wife, and we seek nothing more than a meal and a bed for the night. Can you do it?”
She gave a single, sharp nod, her face pale but set. He drew a deep breath, released her arm, and stepped out from the cover of the trees, walking with the slightly stooped, shuffling gait of a man bone-weary from a long journey. Lady Castlemaine followed a pace behind him, her head bowed, her hands clutching a small, empty satchel at her waist.

The woodcutter stopped his work and watched them approach, his axe held loosely in one hand, his expression a mixture of dull curiosity and suspicion. Sir Norman gave him a short, tired nod, which the man did not return, and pushed open the heavy oak door of the inn.

The interior was even less prepossessing than the outside. It was a single, large, low-ceilinged room, thick with smoke and the reek of stale ale, spilled wine, and unwashed bodies. A dozen or more men were scattered about at crude wooden tables, drinking and talking in low, guttural voices. They were a hard-looking lot, their faces scarred and weather-beaten, and most were armed with swords or daggers worn openly. He recognized the livery of the duke on two men who were noisily dicing in a corner, and his heart gave a hard thump against his ribs.

At their entrance, all conversation ceased. Every head in the room turned toward them, and a dozen pairs of hostile eyes raked them from head to foot. The silence was absolute, a palpable weight of menace. Sir Norman kept his gaze fixed on the floor, shuffling slowly toward the bar, a long plank of wood set upon two barrels, behind which stood a hulking brute of a man with a bald head and a great, bushy black beard. This, he presumed, was the innkeeper.
“A room,” Sir Norman said, his voice deliberately rough and tired. “And some food. We have been on the road since dawn.”
The innkeeper polished a pewter tankard with a filthy rag, his small, piggish eyes assessing them coldly. He did not answer immediately, letting the silence stretch until it was nigh unbearable.
“From where do you travel?” the man grunted, his voice a low rumble.
“From the south,” Sir Norman replied vaguely. “Making for the northern towns. Work is scarce.”
“There’s no work in the north, neither,” the innkeeper said, his gaze lingering on the hilt of Sir Norman’s sword. “And no room here. We’re full.”
A low chuckle ran through the room. One of the duke’s men spoke up, his voice slurred with drink. “No room for beggars and their doxies. Be on your way before you sour the ale.”
Rage, hot and swift, flared in Sir Norman’s chest, but he choked it down, forcing his shoulders to remain slumped in defeat. He had expected this, or something like it.
“We have coin,” he said, reaching into his pocket and pulling out two silver pieces, which he placed on the bar. It was a risk; too little and they would be dismissed as vagrants, too much and they would be marked for robbery.
The innkeeper’s eyes flickered to the coins, and a flicker of avarice warred with his suspicion. He grunted again, a noncommittal sound, but did not move.

Her performance was perfection. She looked so genuinely ill and pitiful that a few of the men actually looked away, a faint hint of shame on their brutish faces. *Where midnight’s queen in silver weeps,*

The room’s single window was small and covered by a shutter bolted from within. It took their combined effort to draw back the warped, groaning bolt, a sound that seemed to fill the entire inn. They froze, listening, but no alarm was raised. Pushing the shutter open, a draft of cool night air, smelling of damp earth and distant woodsmoke, filled the cell. Below them was a drop of some fifteen feet to the cobbled stable yard, a dark well of shadow from which the occasional stamp of a horse or jingle of harness arose.

“It is too far to jump,” she breathed, peering into the gloom.

“Not if we are clever,” Sir Norman replied. He turned to their miserable pallet and, with his dagger, began sawing at the coarse blanket that covered the straw. It was slow, arduous work, but the wool was old and frayed, and in a few minutes he had sliced it into three long strips. He knotted them together with a sailor’s surety, testing each knot until he was satisfied it would hold his weight. One end he tied securely to the iron hinge of the shutter, the other he let fall, a thin, dark line against the pale stone of the inn wall.

“I will go first,” he said. “If I am not challenged, and the way is clear, I will give a low whistle, like that of a nightjar. Then you must follow. Can you do it?”

“I must,” she said, her voice a thin thread of courage.

Sir Norman wasted no more words. He swung his legs over the sill, gripped the woolen rope, and began his descent. The rough stone scraped at his knuckles, and the makeshift rope stretched and groaned alarmingly, but it held. He landed with a soft thud on the damp cobbles of the yard, his knees bending to absorb the shock. He crouched in the deep shadow of an archway, his sword now drawn, his eyes and ears straining against the darkness. The yard was empty save for the shapes of slumbering animals in their stalls. A single lantern burned with a low, smoky flame at the far end, near the great gate that led to the road, but the stable boy was nowhere to be seen, likely asleep in the hayloft. From within the inn, he could hear a sudden burst of coarse laughter, but it was distant, from the common room, and unconnected to their flight. Satisfied, he put two fingers to his lips and let out a soft, trilling whistle that was indistinguishable from the call of a hunting bird.

A moment later, he saw her silhouette against the star-dusted sky, a dark shape hesitating in the window frame. Then, with a courage he could not but admire, she trusted herself to the rope and began to descend, her movements less certain than his, but just as silent. He moved to the base of the wall to steady her as she reached the end of the rope, her feet finding the ground with a near-soundless touch. For a moment she leaned against the cold stone, breathing heavily, her flight from the upper room having cost her more than she would admit.

“To the stables,” he whispered, and together they flitted from shadow to shadow, their boots making no noise on the slick stones. The horses, disturbed by their presence, shifted and snorted in their stalls. Sir Norman placed a calming hand on the nose of a sturdy-looking gray, murmuring to it as he worked swiftly at the latch of its stall. Lady Castlemaine, following his lead, found a dark mare that seemed quiet of temperament. There was no time to saddle them properly; he threw a horse-blanket over each of their backs, slipped a bridle over their heads, and led them carefully out of the stable. The great gate was barred, a massive oak beam settled in iron brackets. It would be impossible to move without waking the entire establishment.

“There,” Sir Norman breathed, pointing with his chin. To one side of the main gate was a smaller postern door, used for foot traffic. A simple iron bolt, rusted from disuse, was all that held it. With a low, protesting screech, he drew it back, and they led the horses through the narrow opening and out onto the deserted road. Only when they had put a hundred silent yards between themselves and the Golden Crown did they mount, swinging themselves onto the horses’ bare backs.

“Now,” Sir Norman said, his voice low and urgent as he leaned toward her, “ride as if the headsman himself were at your heels.”

And they did. The horses, fresh from their rest, sprang forward into a gallop, their hooves drumming a frantic, echoing rhythm on the hard-packed earth. The town was a warren of sleeping houses, their windows like vacant eyes, and they clattered through the narrow streets and out the other side, taking the road that led back toward the sprawling, dark mass of the palace. The moon, a cold silver sovereign in a kingdom of indigo cloud, seemed to watch their desperate flight, casting long, dancing shadows that pursued them like phantom horsemen. Sir Norman rode with the easy grace of a man born to the saddle, but he glanced often at his companion. Lady Castlemaine rode with a fierce, locked determination, her hair breaking free from its pins to stream behind her like a dark banner, her face pale and set in the moonlight. She was no stranger to the saddle, that much was clear, and he felt a grudging admiration for this woman of the court, who could face a midnight ride for her life with as much grit as any hardened campaigner.

They did not dare approach the palace by the main avenue, which would surely be patrolled. Instead, Sir Norman led them on a winding course through a thick copse of woods that bordered the estate, the branches of ancient oaks clawing at them as they passed. He was gambling on his memory of the palace grounds, pieced together from maps he had studied in London and his own brief, disastrous visit. He remembered a section of the outer wall, far from the main keep, that was said to be older and in some disrepair, bordering the forgotten gardens of the original structure.

They tethered their horses deep within the woods, where they would not be easily found, and proceeded on foot. The air grew still and heavy, laden with the scent of overgrown roses and damp moss. Before them loomed the wall, a barrier of dark stone some twenty feet high, looking utterly impassable.

“There is no way over,” Lady Castlemaine whispered, her brief surge of hope visibly faltering.

“Walls are not always as solid as they appear,” Sir Norman replied, his hands already searching the mortared joints, feeling for weakness. He moved along the base of the wall, his fingers probing, his eyes scanning the surface. And then he found it: a section where the moonlight revealed a subtle difference in the stonework, a patch of newer, cruder masonry where a breach had been long ago repaired. Below it, half-buried in ivy and weeds, was a low arch, bricked up but not with the same solidity as the rest. With the hilt of his sword, he tapped at the bricks. One of them gave a hollow echo.

He set to work with his dagger, digging at the crumbling mortar around the loose brick. It was painstaking labor, his knuckles scraped raw against the stone, but desperation was a relentless taskmaster. At last, the brick came free, and then another, and another, until he had created an opening just large of enough for a person to squeeze through. A draft of cool, floral-scented air wafted out from the other side.

“The forgotten garden,” he said, a note of triumph in his voice. “I will go first. Wait for my signal.”

He wriggled through the narrow aperture, scraping his coat and bruising his ribs in the process, and dropped into the soft loam of a flowerbed on the other side. He was in. The garden was a wild, enchanted place, a ruin of its former glory. Weeds choked the ancient pathways, and untamed vines snaked their way up the arms of marble statues, whose pale forms glimmered like ghosts in the moonlight. The air was thick with the perfume of night-blooming jasmine, and the only sound was the chirping of crickets and the distant sigh of the wind. He gave a soft whistle, and soon Lady Castlemaine had joined him, her dress torn and stained, but her eyes wide with a fearful wonder.

“Now,” he breathed, “we look for a queen who weeps silver.”

They moved through the tangled garden like specters, their search guided by the sound of water. They passed statues of fauns and goddesses, their faces rendered blank or sorrowful by time and shadow, but none seemed to fit the rhyme. Then, in a small, secluded clearing, almost entirely consumed by ivy, they heard it: the gentle, rhythmic splash of a fountain.

At the center of a circular basin, green with algae, stood the statue. It was not of a queen in royal attire, but of a woman in a simple gown, her head bowed, her hands clasped before her as if in prayer or unending grief. Her face, though carved from white marble, was so exquisitely rendered that her sorrow felt real, a palpable presence in the silent garden. And from the corners of her sculpted eyes, two tiny, constant streams of water trickled down her cheeks, catching the moonlight and glittering, for all the world, like tears of liquid silver. They splashed softly into the basin below, the only movement in that place of profound stillness.

“Midnight’s queen,” Lady Castlemaine whispered, her voice filled with awe.

Sir Norman did not reply. He was already at the base of the statue, his hands moving over the mossy stone. “‘The silent duke his promise keeps’,” he murmured to himself. The promise would not be in plain sight. It would be hidden. He felt around the plinth, searching for a loose stone, a hidden catch, a secret known only to the dead duke and his intended recipient. His fingers traced the inscription at the base, nearly illegible beneath the grime: *MIRANDA, ANTE REGINAM*. Miranda, before she was queen. A monument to a sorrow she had long since buried under ice.

His fingers found it near the bottom, on the side facing away from the path. A single stone that shifted a fraction of an inch under his probing. Using the tip of his dagger as a lever, he pried at the edges. With a low groan of stone against stone, it came loose, revealing a dark, hollowed-out cavity within the plinth.

Reaching inside, his fingers brushed against cold metal and dry, brittle parchment. He drew them out. In his hand lay a heavy, ornate iron key, its head fashioned in the shape of a coiled serpent, and a small scroll, no bigger than his thumb, sealed with a blob of black wax bearing the impression of a wolf’s head, the sign of the Earl of Gloucester.

He had it. The promise.

He held the items up for Lady Castlemaine to see, a fierce, triumphant grin breaking through his grim expression. For the first time since his capture, a genuine spark of hope ignited within him. This key, this scroll, they were not merely a chance at life; they were a weapon.

But as the first flush of victory surged through him, a new sound broke the garden’s tranquility. It was not the sound of guards or the returning innkeeper. It was the soft, deliberate crunch of a footstep on the gravel of a nearby path, a sound of unhurried, almost leisurely approach. And then a voice, as cold and clear as winter ice, cut through the night.

“I have always found this place to be exceptionally peaceful. It is a pity so few appreciate it.”

Sir Norman and Lady Castlemaine froze, their blood turning to frost in their veins. They were no longer alone. Out of the deep shadows cast by a line of cypress trees, a figure emerged into the moonlight. She was wrapped in a cloak of dark velvet, the hood thrown back to reveal a face of impossible, chilling beauty, crowned with a diadem of diamonds that glittered like captive stars. The long, dark lashes lifted, and the cold, placid eyes that had condemned him with such utter indifference now fixed upon him with a look of sharp, unnerving intelligence.

It was the queen.
