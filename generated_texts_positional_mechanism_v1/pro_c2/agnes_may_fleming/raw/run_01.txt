Lord Valerius felt the silence of the room settle upon him, as heavy and suffocating as a winter fog, and he might have been excused for believing that the hangman’s noose was a more straightforward, if less elegant, end than this gilded cage. He longed for the affair to be concluded, for the wine to be drunk and the accusations made plain, so that he might face his ruin with the dignity it did not deserve. The source of his discomfort, Baron Fendrel, had not long in making his move; for the last of the candied fruits had scarcely been cleared away when the Baron was on his feet, his face a mask of practiced solemnity. Everyone appeared more relaxed after the wine, save for the Baron and the Governess; the former was sweating with a sort of righteous glee, as though he were a saint about to witness a miracle, and Lady Seraphina was more still, more placid, and more like a beautifully carved jade serpent than ever before; which was a terribly bad omen.

Even the Baron could not invent a pretext for further delay; and indeed, that odious man seemed eager to begin, had he the proper cue, but stood shifting his weight until the Governess, with a sigh of profound boredom, acknowledged his presence with a slight dip of her chin, as if dismissing a servant. The Baron and his cronies assembled themselves around a small rosewood table, and gestured for the footmen to bring Lord Valerius forward, which they did; while the other guests, their fans fluttering like nervous moths, settled into their silks and velvets, plainly intending to miss nothing. The justice in this household was administered with a swiftness that would have shamed a formal tribunal, and a caprice that would have made a tyrant blush. Lord Valerius took his place, regarding the Baron with an expression of mild curiosity, and the affair began with Fendrel clearing his throat, a sound like gravel being churned in a pail.

“My dear Valerius, I do hope you enjoyed the port,” the Baron began, his voice dripping with false concern; “it is a rare vintage, and we should hate for it to be wasted on a troubled palate.”

“It was adequate,” said Valerius, examining his fingernails idly; “though I confess, your tedious company has soured it somewhat. A rush of self importance to the head will be your undoing one day.”

“Insolence, my lord!” snapped a portly man to the Baron’s right; “remember you are speaking to Baron Fendrel, Chief Counselor and Confidante to the Governess herself!”

“Is he indeed! Then my only comment is that the Governess shows a shocking lack of judgment in her confidantes, unless her goal was to appoint the most pompous man in the province, just as she herself is the most cunning of women!”

The Governess, who had been observing a crack in the ceiling with profound interest, did not so much as twitch a muscle at this backhanded praise, but the Baron’s face went the color of a ripe plum.

“Silence!” he sputtered, his composure entirely shattered, “and answer the charge put to you. Your seal, you say, was not upon this letter?”

“No. Does your grace find my denial unsatisfactory?”

His grace ignored the sarcasm with a visible effort and continued with what he imagined was judicial gravity. “You are the same Lord Valerius whose lands border the Whispering Wood, where the king’s messenger was robbed not two nights past?”

“I have the misfortune of owning that dreary patch of rock, yes, and my only regret is that the highwaymen did not see fit to relieve the messenger of your company as well, in the person of your bloated self, before you scuttled back to town.”

A low ripple of amusement went through the room, and the Baron’s jowls quivered. “These insults are pointless, and beside the matter. The messenger was waylaid, and the dispatch stolen. You were seen near the wood that evening, were you not?”

“He was,” supplied the portly man, eagerly. “I saw him myself returning well after midnight.”

“Very good, that confirms one point, and is quite enough to see him stripped of his title. You went there as a conspirator, to steal the royal dispatch and sell it to our enemies!”

“Perhaps; you may believe what you wish!” said Lord Valerius, who knew his situation was hopeless, and felt a sudden, liberating indifference to the outcome.

“You admit your treason, then?”

“Not at all. I have admitted nothing. As I said, you are free to invent whatever narrative you find most entertaining.”

“Lord Valerius, this is childish wordplay! You confess you were in the wood?”

“Well!”

“Well, being in the wood at the time of the crime constitutes conspiracy, does it not? Therefore, you are a traitor.”

“The logic is as flawed as its source. What then?”

“Need you ask What is the punishment for all traitors?”

“I have no idea what it is in a proper court, but I am quite sure what it is here!”

“And that is?”

“A forfeiture of lands, and a long, dull exile; confinement in your tedious company, in a word!”

“You have spoken it! Have you any reason why such a judgment should not be passed?”

“None; pass it when you are ready.”

“With the most exquisite satisfaction!” said the Baron, who had been furiously signing a scroll of parchment, and now shoved it toward the Governess. “I have never known a man it gave me more pleasure to ruin. Will your ladyship ratify this judgment, and pronounce his sentence.”

Her ladyship, with a faint, mocking smile, took the scroll. One of the footmen presented it to her with a silver letter opener, and she took it with a look of utter apathy and slit the seal. Not a flicker of emotion crossed her calm, porcelain face, as smooth and unreadable as a frozen pond at dusk; evidently the fortune or ruin of her guest was of less consequence than a speck of dust on her sleeve. To him, her power was everything, and yet a sharp, bitter anger pierced his heart as he saw that delicate hand, which had invited him here, so casually confirm his destruction. But he had little time to dwell on it; for as she tossed the document aside and resumed her study of the ceiling, the Baron puffed out his chest with a triumphant sneer and began:

“Lord Valerius, you have been tried and found guilty of conspiracy against the Crown; and the judgment of this council, over which I have the honor to preside, is that you be stripped of your title and lands, and exiled from this province forever!”

“And a great relief it will be!” murmured a lady near the back, as the Baron concluded his grand pronouncement by glaring at Valerius, his small eyes alight with spiteful victory.

The Governess, who had seemed entirely detached from the proceedings, now sat up, her movement so slight it was barely perceptible, and fixed the Baron with a look as sharp and cold as an icicle.

“And just who gave your council the authority to pass judgment in my house? How many more petitions are on your docket for this evening?”

“Only one, my lady,” replied the Baron, his triumph deflating rapidly; “Lady Arnaud’s, for the matter of her husband’s missing jewels.”

“And what is Lady Arnaud’s petition to be?”

“A simple inquiry, in all likelihood,” he stammered, glancing nervously at the other guests.

“And how long will this inquiry take?”

“An hour, perhaps. The details are somewhat intricate, and there are witnesses to call.”

“Then let all the inquiries be finished first, and all the judgments be passed together at a more convenient time. We have no desire to be bored by a single exile, only to have the same tedious process repeated an hour hence for a different matter. Call Lady Arnaud, and let this man be taken to the conservatory, and there remain until we have need of him. Guards, do you understand? Escort him.”

The Baron’s face grew pale as milk, and he opened his mouth to object, his chins wobbling with indignation. But Lady Seraphina met his gaze with one of absolute, unpitying frost, and flicked her white fingers, in her elegantly dismissive way, toward the guards. Those stony faced men knew far better than to question their mistress when she was in one of her moods, which, if her placid exterior and the glint in her dark eyes told the full story, was her permanent state of being.

As the soldiers laid hands upon his arms, their grips as impersonal as iron manacles, Lord Valerius cast one last, lingering look at the assembly; a sea of faces, some curious, some smug, and some utterly indifferent. The Baron, though momentarily checked, was already puffing himself up again, his expression a mixture of thwarted rage and the smug certainty that this was but a temporary delay. But it was the Governess upon whom his gaze finally rested, this strange, cold woman who held the threads of his life in her slender fingers. She did not meet his look; her attention was already turned towards the far end of the hall, as if he had ceased to exist the moment her command was given. What new game was this? Was it merely a crueler form of torture, a brief stay of execution designed only to make the final fall more jarring? Or was there some other purpose, some inscrutable motive hidden behind that placid mask? He had no answers, and the guards, with a firm, insistent pressure, turned him about and marched him from the room, his fine boots making no sound upon the plush carpets that drank up all noise.

The conservatory was a world away from the stifling atmosphere of the drawing room. It was a vast dome of glass and wrought iron, wherein a miniature jungle thrived in the humid air. Moonlight streamed through the panes, silvering the broad leaves of exotic palms and casting eerie, skeletal shadows upon the tiled floor. The scent of night blooming jasmine and damp earth was thick, almost intoxicating after the perfumed air of the party. His guards led him to a small iron bench set beside a bubbling fountain, and with a gruff, “You will wait here, my lord,” they retreated to the doorway, their forms two rigid statues silhouetted against the light of the corridor. Alone, Valerius drew a deep breath, the fragrant air doing little to calm the frantic beating of his heart. He was a prisoner in a glass cage, surrounded by a silent, growing audience of ferns and orchids. He ran a hand through his hair, a bitter smile twisting his lips. To be ruined by such a man as the Baron was an indignity almost worse than the ruin itself; to be saved, or at least postponed, by a woman who looked upon him as she might a mildly interesting insect, was a humiliation of a far more perplexing sort.

He had not been seated for more than a quarter of an hour, a span that stretched into an eternity of silent speculation, when a faint rustle of silk announced another’s presence. He looked up, expecting a guard, but it was the Governess herself, Lady Seraphina, who stood before him. She moved with a liquid grace that seemed at odds with her frozen demeanor, a statue carved from winter moonlight come to life. She held no fan, wore no jewels save for a single, dark pearl at her throat, and her gaze, when it met his, was as direct and unreadable as ever.

“You seem ill at ease, my lord,” she observed, her voice a low, melodic murmur that was somehow more chilling than a shout.

“Your ladyship has a talent for understatement,” Valerius replied, rising to his feet. “Most men condemned to exile and poverty find their ease somewhat disturbed. I am no exception.”

A faint smile, as fleeting as a winter sunbeam, touched her lips. “You mistake the Baron’s petty theater for a true judgment. His authority in this house extends only as far as I permit it.”

“And you permitted it to extend to my condemnation. To what purpose, then, has your ladyship orchestrated this private interview? Do you mean to offer me a glass of poisoned wine to hasten the sentence along? I confess, after this evening’s proceedings, I should almost welcome it.”

She ignored his sarcasm, her dark eyes scanning his face with an unnerving intensity, as if she were reading the very thoughts in his head. “The Baron is a greedy and ambitious fool, Lord Valerius. He wishes for your lands, which adjoin his own, and he fears your influence with certain members of the court. His little council is a sham, a tool to lend the weight of legitimacy to his avarice. The charge of conspiracy was a convenient fiction.”

A spark of anger flared in Valerius’s chest, hot and sharp. “A fiction for which I was to pay with my entire life and fortune! A fiction you seemed quite content to endorse, my lady, until it suited your whim to interrupt.”

“Do not mistake my actions for whims,” she said, her voice dropping a fraction, taking on an edge of tempered steel. “The Baron has grown too bold. He and his faction believe they can act with impunity, even under my own roof. Tonight, I chose to remind him of the true measure of his power, which is to say, none at all. You were merely the instrument of that lesson.”

Valerius stared at her, a dawning, incredulous understanding beginning to replace his anger. He had been a pawn, a piece moved in a game so subtle he had not even been aware it was being played. “An instrument,” he repeated slowly. “So my ruin was a trifle, a theatrical device for your ladyship’s amusement and the Baron’s instruction.”

“It was a necessity,” she corrected him coolly. “And it is not yet your ruin. I have delayed the sentence, not dismissed it. That is a power I still hold in reserve. Whether I choose to exercise it depends entirely upon you.”

She took a step closer, the scent of her perfume, something cold and floral like mountain snow, reaching him. “The Baron and his friends are not merely greedy, my lord. They are traitors. Not in the clumsy way they accused you of, but in a deeper, more insidious manner. They seek to undermine the Royal Governor and seize control of this province for their own enrichment. The conspiracy is real; they simply accused the wrong man.”

He felt as though the ground were shifting beneath his feet. “And you oppose them?”

“I serve the Crown,” she stated, as if it were the most obvious fact in the world. “And the Baron’s clumsy machinations threaten the stability I have worked very hard to maintain. I require an ally, one who has reason to despise the Baron as much as I do. One who is clever, and bold, and currently has nothing left to lose. You, Lord Valerius, seem uniquely qualified on all three counts.”

Here it was, then. The price of his salvation. Not a simple pardon, but an enlistment. He would trade one form of servitude for another, escaping the Baron’s crude snare only to be caught in Lady Seraphina’s silken web. “And what is it you would have this qualified instrument do for you, my lady?” he asked, his voice laced with a cynicism he did not try to hide.

“The Baron keeps a private ledger,” she said, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “It is not a book of finances, but of loyalties. It details the bribes, the promises, and the seditious pacts he has made. It is the key to undoing him and his entire network. The book is kept in his study, at his own estate.”

“You wish for me to burgle the man who just tried to ruin me?” Valerius asked, a grim smile touching his lips. “There is a certain poetry in that, I admit. But his estate is surely guarded.”

“It is. But tomorrow night, he hosts a masquerade ball. The security will be focused on the guests, not on his private wing. And a man in a mask, as you well know, can go many places unnoticed. Procure that ledger for me, Lord Valerius, and I will not only see your title and lands restored, but I will see them doubled at the Baron’s expense. Refuse, and I will find I have no further use for your company, and the conservatory doors will be opened to the Baron’s men.”

She did not need to elaborate. The choice was as clear as the glass dome above them: risk his life for a chance at restoration, or accept a slow, certain descent into oblivion. She watched him, her head tilted slightly, her expression betraying nothing. She had laid out the terms with the detached precision of a general planning a campaign, and she awaited his decision with the patience of one who already knew the outcome.

“It seems your ladyship leaves me with very little choice in the matter,” he said at last.

“I find that is the best way to ensure cooperation,” she replied, the ghost of a smile returning. “Now, we should return. The second act of our little drama is about to begin, and it would not do for the principal players to be absent.”

Without another word, she turned and glided back towards the corridor, leaving him to follow in her wake, his mind reeling. His fate was no longer sealed, but it was now bound to this formidable, dangerous woman. He had been pardoned from the headsman’s axe, it seemed, only to find himself holding the sword of Damocles in his own hand.

When they re-entered the drawing room, the scene had changed. The Baron was once more seated in his place of authority, though his face was still flushed and his posture rigid with indignation. Before the impromptu tribunal stood a woman of middle years, her face a mask of anxious distress. This, Valerius presumed, was the Lady Arnaud.

“The matter is quite simple,” the Baron was saying, his voice loud and ponderous, as if to reassert his shattered consequence. “Your ladyship’s jewels, a priceless family heirloom, have vanished from their case. The only other person with access was your private secretary, a Mr. Finch. The conclusion is inescapable.”

“But Cyril would never!” Lady Arnaud protested, wringing her hands. “He is the most devoted, the most honest of young men! There must be some mistake!”

“There is no mistake, madam!” the Baron boomed. “The evidence is circumstantial but overwhelming! Bring in the accused!”

Two guards entered, escorting a pale, slender young man with spectacles and a look of terrified bewilderment. He glanced wildly around the room, his eyes landing on Lady Arnaud with a look of desperate appeal. Valerius watched the scene, but now with the Governess’s revelations echoing in his mind. He looked at Lady Seraphina, who had resumed her seat as if she had never left it, her expression one of sublime boredom. He knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that this was no simple theft.

“Mr. Finch,” the Baron began, leaning forward with a predatory gleam in his eye, “you are accused of the theft of Lady Arnaud’s diamond necklace. What have you to say for yourself?”

“I am innocent, my lord!” the young man stammered, his voice thin with fear. “I have served Lady Arnaud faithfully for five years! I would sooner cut off my own hand than steal from her!”

“A noble sentiment, but one which your actions betray!” the Baron retorted. “We have searched your quarters. And while we did not find the necklace itself, we found something far more damning. A letter, sir! A letter detailing your extravagant debts to certain disreputable moneylenders! You were desperate for funds, were you not?”

He waved a piece of paper triumphantly. The young secretary paled further, looking as though he might faint. “Those are…those are private matters, my lord. They have no bearing on this!”

“No bearing?” roared the Baron, enjoying his moment of recovered power. “They are the motive! You needed money, you saw an opportunity, and you took it! It is the oldest story in the book. I put it to the council that the man is plainly guilty!”

A murmur of agreement went through the Baron’s cronies seated around him. Lady Arnaud let out a small sob. Valerius glanced at the Governess; her eyelids were drooping, her expression unchanged, but he saw the slightest, almost imperceptible tightening of her fingers upon the arm of her chair. She was watching. Waiting.

And in that moment, Valerius understood. The secretary, Finch, was not a thief. He was a liability. He must know something, something connected to the Baron’s plots. The “jewels” were a lie, the trial a pretext to have him imprisoned or disposed of, to silence him under the guise of common criminality. And Lady Arnaud, his trusting employer, was the unwitting pawn in this far deadlier game.

He felt a surge of cold fury. Before he had fully considered the consequences, before he had weighed the wisdom of defying the Baron a second time, he took a step forward.

“My lord Baron,” he said, his voice cutting through the room with calm authority. Every head turned towards him, a collective gasp rippling through the guests. The Baron’s face, which had been a mask of triumph, contorted with pure, unadulterated fury.

“You are out of order, sir!” he sputtered. “Your case is concluded! You have no standing here!”

“On the contrary,” Valerius said, his eyes locking with the Baron’s. “As a guest in this house, I have a standing to prevent a gross miscarriage of justice from being perpetrated within its walls. And as a man who was himself falsely accused not an hour ago, I find I have a particular interest in the matter.”

He turned his attention to the trembling Lady Arnaud. “Your ladyship,” he said, his voice softening. “You refer to your jewels. Are you quite certain it was a diamond necklace that was taken?”

Lady Arnaud looked at him in confusion. “Why, yes…the diamonds of my grandmother…”

“Are you quite certain,” Valerius pressed, his gaze flicking back to the Baron, “that the missing items were not, in fact, a set of papers? Letters, perhaps? Letters bound in a blue Moroccan leather portfolio, which you had entrusted to your secretary for safekeeping?”

A deathly silence fell over the room. Lady Arnaud’s eyes widened, a dawning horror on her face. The young secretary, Finch, stared at Valerius as if he were a ghost. But it was the Baron’s reaction that was most telling. The florid color drained from his face, leaving it a pasty, mottled grey. He looked, in that moment, like a man who had been shot.

“This is preposterous!” he blustered, but his voice lacked all its former conviction. “What nonsense is this? We are speaking of jewels!”

“Are we?” Valerius replied, his voice like ice. “Because it seems to me that your lordship has a vested interest in certain ‘jewels’ of a literary nature. Perhaps the kind of jewels that, were they to be made public, would prove quite ruinous to their author. Perhaps Mr. Finch here was not a thief, but a custodian, one who refused to surrender his charge when asked. Or rather, when threatened.”

He had no proof, only the cold, hard logic of the situation as illuminated by the Governess. But as he saw the raw panic in the Baron’s eyes, he knew he had struck the very heart of the matter.

The Baron half rose from his seat, one hand gripping the table for support. “This is an outrage! An insult! I will not be questioned by this…this condemned traitor!”

“He is not condemned,” a quiet voice stated from the throne like chair. All eyes swung to the Governess. Lady Seraphina had sat up, her eyes no longer hooded but wide and bright and fixed upon the Baron with an expression of profound disappointment, as one might regard a clumsy servant who had just dropped a priceless vase.

“His sentence,” she continued, “was contingent upon the conclusion of all other business. And this business has just become far more interesting than a simple theft. It appears, Baron, that you have brought a matter of state into my drawing room under false pretenses. That is a thing I do not care for.” She flicked her eyes towards the pale secretary. “Mr. Finch. Did the Baron, or any of his associates, approach you demanding certain papers belonging to Lady Arnaud?”

The young man, seeing an unexpected and powerful new ally, found his courage. “Yes, your ladyship,” he said, his voice shaking but clear. “A man came to me yesterday. He offered me money for them. When I refused, he threatened me. He said they would find a way to ruin me, to see me put away where I could not speak. I did not know what to do!”

The entire assembly was now staring at the Baron, who seemed to shrink in his chair, his chins trembling. His allies looked away, suddenly fascinated by the patterns on the carpet.

Lady Seraphina rose to her feet in one fluid motion. Her face was as calm as ever, but her eyes glittered with a cold, triumphant fire. “Baron,” she said, and her voice, though still quiet, filled the entire hall, “your little council is dissolved. You have abused my hospitality and attempted to use my home as the stage for your squalid conspiracies. You will leave this house at once. And I would advise you to set your affairs in order, for I suspect the Royal Governor will soon have some questions for you regarding the contents of Lady Arnaud’s portfolio.” She turned to the guards. “Escort the Baron out. And see that Mr. Finch is taken to a guest chamber and kept safe. He is now under my protection.”

The Baron, utterly broken, could not even muster a protest. He stumbled to his feet, his face a ruin of apoplectic shock and humiliation, and allowed the guards to lead him away like a common criminal. The remaining guests began to murmur amongst themselves, a frantic, excited buzz filling the void left by the Baron’s departure.

In the midst of the commotion, Lord Valerius felt a presence at his elbow. It was the Governess.

“A bold move, my lord,” she said, a note of what might have been genuine approval in her voice. “You learn quickly.”

“I had a most excellent teacher,” he replied, turning to face her. “And a powerful incentive.”

“Indeed,” she said, the faint smile reappearing. “You have made a most formidable enemy tonight. But you have also secured a most powerful friend. Do not forget our arrangement. The masquerade is tomorrow. I shall expect you to deliver on your end of the bargain.”

She gave him a slight nod, a gesture of dismissal that was also, somehow, a confirmation of their new and perilous alliance. Then she turned away to speak with a relieved and grateful Lady Arnaud, her composure as perfect and unruffled as if she had just been discussing the weather. Valerius watched her go, a man restored to his station, yet more deeply entangled than ever before. His exile had been rescinded, but he had just walked into a far more gilded and dangerous cage.

As the soldiers, with a discipline born more of fear than loyalty, moved to obey, Sir Norman found himself seized by two stout guards whose gaudy uniforms did little to disguise their jailer’s grip. The dwarf prince, for his part, seemed on the very verge of an apoplectic fit; his face, already a study in grotesque malignancy, turned a shade of mottled purple, and a froth of rage gathered at the corners of his mouth. He took a half step forward, his jeweled hand clenched upon the hilt of a tiny, ornamental dagger, as if he meant to challenge the queen’s authority by force.

“You dare!” he hissed, the sound like steam escaping a boiling kettle. “You dare countermand my sentence! He is a spy, condemned by this court, and he will die now!”

The queen did not even deign to look at him fully, but merely turned her head a fraction of an inch, her profile a perfect, chilling cameo against the torchlight. “I have spoken,” she said, her voice dropping to a temperature that might have frozen the very air between them. “Do not presume to question my will in my own palace. The man will wait. We find it tedious to have our entertainments parceled out in such miserable, piecemeal fashion. See to the second trial, or we shall retire and leave you to your bloody games alone.”

This last threat, it seemed, was the one that struck home. The prospect of losing his audience, and most particularly the exquisite torment of losing the queen’s cold, captive presence, was an ignominy his highness could not bear. He drew himself up, his little body trembling with suppressed fury, and shot a look of pure, unadulterated hatred at Sir Norman, a look that promised a death of such excruciating slowness that the headsman’s axe would seem a tender mercy. Then, with a curt, vicious nod to the duke, he reseated himself, his grin gone, replaced by a sullen pout that made him look like a monstrous, overgrown child.

Sir Norman, for his part, felt a strange sort of detached amusement at the entire proceeding. To be saved from the block, not by justice or mercy, but by the petulant whim of a bored tyrant, was a fate so absurd as to be almost comical. He offered the dwarf a slight, mocking bow as the guards pulled him away from the green table, an impertinence that earned him a sharp jab in the ribs from one of his captors. He was led from the great hall, through a smaller antechamber, and towards a narrow stone archway he had not previously noticed. The sounds of the court, the rustle of silk and the murmur of voices, faded behind him, replaced by the damp, echoing chill of stone corridors.

They descended a flight of winding stairs, the air growing colder and heavier with each step, smelling of wet earth and ancient decay. The torches of his escort cast long, dancing shadows that seemed to writhe and leap upon the walls like imprisoned spirits. At the bottom of the stairs, a long, low passage stretched before them, its sides lined with heavy oaken doors, each studded with iron and fitted with a small, barred grate. It was a place of silence and despair, a place where hope came to die. One of the guards produced a great, rusty key and, selecting a door near the end of the passage, thrust it into the lock. The screech of the bolt being drawn was a sound of dreadful finality.

The door groaned open, revealing a cell of absolute blackness. Sir Norman was unceremoniously shoved inside, stumbling forward into the oppressive dark. He heard the door slam shut behind him, the bolt shriek home, and the receding footsteps of his guards. And then, there was only silence; a silence so profound it seemed to have weight and substance, pressing in on him from all sides. He stood for a moment, letting his eyes adjust, and could just make out the faintest sliver of light from the grate in the door, not enough to see by, but enough to prove he had not been struck blind. The cell was small, its stone floor slick with a damp, slimy moisture, and the air was thick with the foul stench of a forgotten tomb.

He felt his way to a wall and leaned against it, his brief levity evaporating in the face of this grim reality. His trial had been a farce, his sentence a foregone conclusion, and his reprieve a mere stay of execution, granted for the most frivolous of reasons. He was to die here, in this black hole, and the beautiful, cold-hearted queen who held his life in her hands would watch him perish with no more emotion than if she were watching a play. It was a miserable, ignoble end, and yet, he could not find it in himself to feel fear, only a deep, burning anger at the sheer injustice of it all.

“It seems we are to be neighbors in misfortune,” said a voice from the darkness, a voice that was low and musical, with a note of weary cynicism that startled him. “I trust you will find the accommodations as wanting as I do.”

Sir Norman pushed himself from the wall, turning towards the sound. He had not been alone. In the far corner of the cell, a deeper patch of shadow began to resolve itself into a human form. “I was not aware the dungeon offered shared lodgings,” he replied, his own voice sounding rough in the stillness. “Had I known, I would have insisted on a more private suite.”

A soft, dry laugh met his ears. “They are not so generous. We are to share a fate, you and I, so I suppose they see no harm in us sharing a final resting place. I am the Lady Castlemaine. And you, if I am not mistaken, are the spy who so rudely interrupted her majesty’s dinner.”

As she spoke, she moved slightly, and the sliver of light from the grate caught the pale oval of her face and the glint of a diamond in her dark hair. She was a woman of perhaps thirty years, possessed of a sharp, intelligent beauty that had not been dimmed by her squalid surroundings. Her eyes, which seemed very large and dark in the gloom, regarded him with a kind of cool appraisal.

“Sir Norman Kingsley, at your service,” he said, managing a slight bow. “Though I fear my services are of little value at present. And I was not a spy, madam, but a traveler who took a wrong turn into a nest of vipers.”

“A common mistake in these parts,” she said, her tone unchanged. “We are all travelers here, Sir Norman, who have run afoul of the queen’s consort. That is his chief amusement; luring flies into his web. What was your supposed crime, besides the obvious one of carrying a sword in his presence?”

“I am accused of being an agent of King Charles, and of shooting a highwayman who, it appears, was a friend of the court,” he explained. “Though my true crime, I suspect, was in failing to laugh at his highness’s jests.”

“Ah, yes, the ugliest man in the world cannot abide being reminded of it,” she murmured. “My own transgression is somewhat more domestic. I am accused of poisoning the Duchess of Sutherland.”

“And did you?” Sir Norman asked, his curiosity piqued by her placid demeanor.

“Of course not,” she replied, with no particular indignation. “The duchess was my friend. Besides, the poison they found was a clumsy, brutish sort of thing, a man’s poison. If I were to kill someone, I assure you, it would be with a great deal more subtlety and art. No, this has the dwarf’s grubby fingerprints all over it. The duchess was one of the queen’s few confidantes, and her death weakens Miranda’s position. Caliban wishes his wife to be utterly alone, utterly dependent upon him. He removes her allies one by one, you see, like pulling the legs from a spider.”

“The queen?” Sir Norman said, surprised. “She seems to me to be the one in authority. She countermanded my execution with a single word.”

Lady Castlemaine sighed, a soft, weary sound in the dark. “You mistake the nature of her power, Sir Norman. She has the power of a tempest, sudden and fierce, but she is trapped in a bottle. She can rage, she can strike, but she cannot escape. This entire court, this mockery of a kingdom, is her prison, and Prince Caliban is her jailer. He was foisted upon her by a political alliance she could not refuse, and he has spent every day since trying to break her spirit. He surrounds her with his own creatures, like that odious duke, and orchestrates these midnight trials as a form of theater, a constant reminder that he controls life and death here.”

“Then why did she spare me, even for a moment?” he wondered aloud. “It seemed an act of pure caprice.”

“It was,” Lady Castlemaine agreed. “But it was a caprice with a purpose. She despises him, and she will take any opportunity, no matter how small, to thwart him and remind him that she is still a queen. Your defiance amused her, and his fury at being overruled pleased her even more. She did not save you, Sir Norman; she merely used you as a whip with which to lash her husband. You are a pawn in a game you did not even know you were playing. As am I.”

A grim silence fell between them. The truth of her words was self evident. He had stumbled into a domestic quarrel of the most savage and gothic kind, and his life was to be the price of a moment’s spite. He thought of the queen’s face, as cold and beautiful as a statue, and the flicker of pain he had felt when her hand signed his death warrant. He had thought it a betrayal, but now he saw it differently. It was the act of a fellow prisoner, one who had long since grown numb to the horrors of her gilded cage.

“So this trial of yours,” he said at last, “is as much a sham as mine was.”

“More so,” she replied. “Yours was a hasty improvisation. Mine has been planned for weeks. They will produce false witnesses and forged letters. The duke will bluster, the nobles will nod their heads, and his highness will grin and pronounce my sentence. And the queen will sit and watch, her face like ice, because she is powerless to stop it. To intervene for me would be seen as a personal matter, an act of favoritism, which would give Caliban a pretext to tighten his control still further. She cannot save her friend, so she sits in silent disdain of them all. It is her only remaining defense.”

Suddenly, the sound of a distant trumpet, thin and reedy, echoed down the corridor. It was followed by the tramp of marching feet and the clatter of arms.

“Ah,” said Lady Castlemaine, her voice utterly devoid of emotion. “It would seem my trial is about to begin. They do not like to keep the headsman waiting for his evening’s entertainment.”

The footsteps grew louder, stopping outside their cell. The bolt was drawn back with another agonizing screech, and the door was thrown open, flooding the small space with the harsh, smoky light of torches. A captain of the guard stood silhouetted in the doorway, his helmet plumed, his breastplate gleaming.

“Lady Castlemaine,” he said, his voice a harsh, official bark. “The court summons you.”

She rose to her feet with a grace that seemed entirely out of place in that squalid dungeon. She smoothed the silken folds of her gown, a futile gesture that was nonetheless performed with a kind of defiant elegance. As she walked towards the door, she paused and looked back at Sir Norman, who remained in the shadows.

“Do not abandon all hope, Sir Norman Kingsley,” she whispered, her voice so low only he could hear it. “The queen may be a prisoner, but even a caged lioness has claws. Watch the trial, if you can. Watch for the queen’s signal. Not all is as it seems in the court of Queen Miranda.”

Before he could ask what she meant, she was gone, escorted from the cell by the guards. The door was not locked this time, but left slightly ajar, and one of the two guards who had brought him down remained stationed outside. Through the gap, Sir Norman could see the flickering torchlight on the stone passage, and hear the faint, distorted sounds from the great hall above as the second act of the night’s grotesque drama commenced. He moved closer to the door, pressing his eye to the grate. His own fate was sealed, yet he found himself consumed by a desperate curiosity to witness the trial of this strange, courageous woman, and to understand the signal of the frozen queen.

Upstairs, the court had rearranged itself. The brief intermission had done little to improve the general mood, which was now one of grim, anticipatory excitement, like that of a crowd awaiting a public flogging. Lady Castlemaine was led to the same spot where Sir Norman had stood only an hour before. She did not look at the judges, but instead fixed her gaze upon the queen, her expression one of quiet, sorrowful loyalty. The queen, for her part, remained a study in perfect indifference, her lashes lowered, her hands resting motionless in her lap.

The duke, looking pleased to be back on script, cleared his throat and began. “Lady Frances Castlemaine, you stand accused of a most heinous crime. You are charged with the murder of her Grace, the Duchess of Sutherland, by means of poison, administered on the evening of the tenth of this month.”

“I am aware of the charge,” Lady Castlemaine replied, her voice clear and steady. “And I deny it.”

“Your denial is of little consequence,” sneered the duke. “We have evidence. Irrefutable evidence! First, we have the testimony of the duchess’s own physician, who will attest that she died not of a natural ailment, but of a fast acting poison.”

A small, nervous man was bustled forward, who stammered out a prepared statement about the duchess’s symptoms, all the while avoiding Lady Castlemaine’s steady gaze.

“Furthermore,” the duke continued, growing in confidence, “we have motive! It was well known that you were in debt to the duchess for a considerable sum of money. A gambling debt, I believe?”

“The duchess and I sometimes played cards for trifles,” Lady Castlemaine said calmly. “She was my dearest friend. The notion of a debt between us is an absurdity.”

“An absurdity you sought to resolve with a vial of nightshade!” the duke thundered. “And we have a witness who saw you purchase just such a substance! Bring forth the apothecary!”

Another man, this one shifty eyed and greasy, was brought before the table. He swore on a stack of dubious relics that Lady Castlemaine had come to his shop in disguise and purchased a potent poison, claiming it was for rats. As he spoke, Sir Norman, watching from his dungeon grate, saw the dwarf prince lean forward, a look of intense concentration on his face, his eyes darting from the witness, to Lady Castlemaine, and finally to the queen. He seemed to be waiting for something, anticipating a reaction that did not come. The queen remained utterly still, a marble effigy of a woman.

“Lies,” Lady Castlemaine said, her voice filled with contempt. “All of it, lies woven by a clumsy plotter. I have never seen that man before in my life.”

“Your indignation is well acted, my lady, but it will not serve you,” the duke said with a smug smile. “For we have one final piece of evidence. A piece of evidence that will seal your fate beyond any doubt.” He paused for dramatic effect, letting the silence hang heavy in the air. “We have found, hidden in your own chambers, a letter. A letter written in your own hand, to a confederate, in which you confess the entire sordid affair!”

He produced a folded piece of parchment with a triumphant flourish and passed it to the lord chancellor, who began to read it aloud. The letter was a crude forgery, its language stilted and its sentiments unbelievable, detailing a plot so obvious that only a fool could have written it. A low murmur went through the room as the chancellor read. This was it, the final nail in the coffin.

Sir Norman felt a pit in his stomach. It was hopeless. The plot was too complete, the trap too well laid. And yet, he remembered Lady Castlemaine’s words: *Watch for the queen’s signal.* He tore his eyes from the chancellor and stared at the queen, searching for any sign, any flicker of movement. Her face was a mask, her posture rigid. But then he saw it. It was not a grand gesture, not a fiery glance, but something infinitely more subtle. As the chancellor read the most damning line of the forged letter, the queen’s right hand, which had been resting limply in her lap, moved. Her fingers, long and white, curled slowly, just once, into a tight fist, before relaxing again. It was a gesture of suppressed rage, so small and so private that no one, save someone watching for it with desperate intensity, could possibly have noticed.

No one, perhaps, but Lady Castlemaine. At that precise moment, the accused woman, who had been listening to the reading with an air of weary resignation, suddenly straightened up. A new light came into her eyes, a spark of desperate, calculated daring.

“A forgery!” she cried out, her voice ringing with a sudden and surprising strength that made the chancellor falter. “A clumsy and obvious forgery! And I can prove it!”

The dwarf prince laughed, a harsh, grating sound. “Prove it? And how do you propose to do that, my lady? Will you call upon the angels to testify for you?”

“No, your highness,” she said, turning to face him directly, her eyes glittering. “I shall do something far simpler. I shall prove that on the day this letter was supposedly written, I was not even in the city. I was miles away, and I have a witness who can attest to it. A witness whose word even this court would not dare to question!”

The duke scoffed. “A witness? And who is this mysterious witness, who has remained silent until now?”

Lady Castlemaine took a deep breath, and for the first time, she smiled. It was a smile of pure, audacious genius, a gambler’s final, desperate throw. She turned from the judges and looked directly at the impassive, frozen figure on the throne.

“My witness,” she declared, her voice filling the silent hall, “is Her Most Gracious Majesty, the Queen!”

As the soldiers paused for dramatic effect, letting the silence hang heavy in the air, Lady Castlemaine’s declaration echoed in the sudden, profound stillness. For the space of a dozen heartbeats, no one moved, no one breathed; the entire assembly seemed to have been turned to stone by the sheer audacity of her words. The duke, his mouth agape, looked from the accused to the throne as if watching a game of battledore and shuttlecock, where the shuttlecock was a live grenade. His highness the dwarf, for the first time that night, was struck completely dumb, his usual grin of malice frozen into a grotesque mask of disbelief. All eyes, as if drawn by a single magnetic force, turned to the queen.

And the queen did nothing. She remained as she was, a figure of glacial beauty, her lashes still lowered, her hands still resting in the lap of her purple velvet gown. It was as though she had not heard, as though the desperate plea of one woman and the fate of another were beneath the threshold of her royal notice. The silence grew heavier, more oppressive, until it was a physical weight upon the room.

It was the dwarf who broke it. A low, guttural chuckle escaped his lips, which slowly, hideously, unfroze and stretched back into his signature grin. The chuckle grew into a full-throated, cackling laugh that was more terrible than any roar of anger. “Her majesty!” he shrieked, slapping his thigh with a sound like a wet cloth hitting a butcher’s block. “Oh, that is rich! That is a masterstroke of impudence, my lady! You would drag the sun from the sky to be your candle! You would implicate the queen herself in your filthy, treasonous affairs to save your own worthless neck! Guards, what are you waiting for? Do you need a written invitation to take this lying wretch to the block?”

“The woman lies!” bellowed the duke, finding his voice at last and rushing to support his diminutive master. “It is blasphemy! An insult to the crown! Her majesty has not left the palace grounds in a fortnight, as all here know!”

“I do not lie,” said Lady Castlemaine, and her voice, though not loud, cut through the clamor with the sharpness of cut glass. She did not look at the dwarf or the duke; her gaze was fixed, with an almost hypnotic intensity, upon the still figure of the queen. “I do not lie, and her majesty knows it.”

At this, at this direct and personal address, there was a change. It was a change so subtle it was almost imperceptible, yet to Sir Norman, who was watching with the desperate focus of a man whose life hung upon the outcome, it was as seismic as the cracking of a continent. The queen’s head, which had been bent slightly forward, lifted. Slowly, deliberately, she raised her long, dark lashes, and for the first time that night, her eyes were fully revealed. They were not merely dark; they were the color of a starless midnight, and in their depths, one could imagine a cold and terrible wisdom. They swept over the court, not deigning to look at Lady Castlemaine, nor at the sputtering duke, but settling, with a chilling lack of haste, upon her husband.

“His highness,” she said, and her voice was low and musical, yet carried the dispassionate cold of a winter wind, “seems very certain of our movements.”

The dwarf was so taken aback by being addressed that he could only stammer. “I--I only state the truth, my love! Your majesty has been occupied with matters of state! You have been here, within these walls!”

“Indeed,” the queen murmured, a single, resonant note. “And is it so inconceivable that we should desire a moment of privacy? A brief respite from the endless, tedious pageant of this court?” Her eyes remained locked on his, and a flicker of something--was it amusement? or contempt?--danced in their black depths. “Tell me, your highness, was the letter which you hold as your chiefest proof dated, by chance, for the Tuesday before last?”

The dwarf glanced at the lord chancellor, who, flustered, fumbled with the parchment. “Why--why, yes, your majesty! It is so dated!” he cried, as if this confirmed his case entirely.

“How fortunate,” the queen said, with the faintest whisper of a smile playing on her lips. It was a smile that held no warmth, no joy, but was merely a slight upward curve of the mouth, as terrifying in its way as the dwarf’s grin. “For it was on the Tuesday before last, at the hour before dawn, that I had a sudden whim. I wished to see the sunrise from the ruins of the old priory on Sentinel Hill. And as I do not enjoy riding alone in the dark, I commanded Lady Castlemaine to attend me.”

A collective gasp went through the room, a sound like the sea drawing back from the shore. The dwarf’s face, which had been a mask of triumphant scorn, seemed to collapse in on itself, the ruddy color draining away to leave a waxy, sallow pallor.

“I remember the morning well,” the queen continued, her voice now a lazy, reminiscent drawl, as if she were idly recounting a pleasant memory to herself. “It was quite chilly. Lady Castlemaine wore a dark green cloak, and she complained that the dew would ruin her slippers. She also remarked, as the sun’s first rays touched the clouds, that the sky was the color of a bruised plum. An odd fancy, I thought, but memorable.” She paused, and her gaze, with an almost physical weight, moved to Lady Castlemaine. “Is that not so, my lady?”

Lady Castlemaine, her face pale but her posture erect, bowed her head deeply. “It is exactly as your majesty remembers it,” she said, her voice thick with an emotion that might have been relief, or gratitude, or simply awe.

The case was shattered. The lynchpin of the prosecution had not merely been removed; it had been declared a fiction by the one authority no one in that room could gainsay. The forged letter, so damning moments before, was now just a piece of useless, lying parchment. The duke looked as if he might faint, his face a mottled patchwork of red and white; he took a half-step away from the dwarf, a small, instinctive act of disassociation.

His highness, however, was made of sterner, or at least more malignant, stuff. His face contorted in a spasm of pure, unadulterated fury. “A trick!” he hissed, his voice a venomous rasp. “A woman’s trick! This proves nothing of the poison! The charge of murder still stands! This creature is a poisoner, and she will die for it!”

“Will she?” the queen asked softly. The question hung in the air like a poised blade. She turned her cool gaze to the lord chancellor, who flinched as if he had been struck. “My lord chancellor, it is a principle of justice, is it not, that if the prime evidence in a case is proven to be a deliberate and malicious fabrication, the entire prosecution is thereby tainted?”

The lord chancellor, caught between the incandescent rage of the dwarf and the icy displeasure of the queen, began to sweat profusely. “Well--your majesty--in principle--that is to say, under normal circumstances--”

“These are normal circumstances,” the queen stated, cutting him off without a trace of impatience. It was not a debate; it was a decree. “The central pillar of your accusation has been exposed as a forgery. The rest is mere speculation. Who, I wonder, was so eager to see Lady Castlemaine condemned that they would stoop to creating such a clumsy lie?” Her eyes drifted back to her husband, and this time there was no mistaking the contempt in them.

The dwarf stood his ground, but it was the defiance of a cornered rat. He could not confess to the forgery, but he could not escape the clear implication. He swelled his chest, preparing to unleash another torrent of invective, but the queen was finished with him.

“The charge against Lady Castlemaine is dismissed for want of credible evidence,” she announced, her voice ringing with an absolute finality. She made a small, dismissive gesture with her hand. “My lady, you are free to go.” Then, as Lady Castlemaine opened her mouth to pour forth what would have surely been a flood of grateful thanks, the queen added, with chilling indifference, “Do not presume upon this. I found your chatter about the sunrise tedious then, and I find the thought of your gratitude tedious now. Leave.”

Lady Castlemaine, looking as though she had been both saved and slapped in the same breath, curtsied low and melted back into the whispering assembly, a ghost returning to the shadows.

The tension in the room, which had been drawn to a screaming pitch, seemed to slacken, and Sir Norman felt a wild, unreasoning surge of hope. If one trial could be so overturned, why not his? But his hope was to be short-lived.

The dwarf, thwarted and humiliated in the matter of Lady Castlemaine, turned the full, focused venom of his fury upon the prisoner who remained. His face, which had been pale, was now suffused with a dark, purplish rage, and his eyes gleamed with a malevolent fire. “This changes nothing for the spy!” he screamed, pointing a trembling, sausage-like finger at Sir Norman. “His guilt is confessed! His sentence is pronounced! That sentence stands! Guards! I told you to take him away! Obey me!”

The two guards, who had been standing as motionless as statues, shifted uncomfortably. Their eyes darted from the raging dwarf to the impassive queen. They were soldiers, trained to obey, but here were two commanders giving conflicting orders, and they knew, with the simple, animal cunning of men who wish to keep their heads attached to their necks, that a mistake in this moment would be their last.

Sir Norman’s heart, which had leaped with hope, now plummeted into the abyss. It was over. The queen had intervened for her lady-in-waiting, a debt repaid or a whim indulged, but he was a stranger, an enemy, a confessed spy. There was no reason for her to save him. He squared his shoulders, a grim resignation settling over him. At least he would meet his end on his feet.

“One moment,” said the queen.

The words, spoken as quietly as before, once again commanded an absolute silence. The guards froze. The dwarf turned his furious visage toward his wife, his mouth working silently.

The queen’s gaze fell upon Sir Norman, and he felt it like a physical touch, a shard of ice against his skin. It was the first time she had truly looked at him, and he found himself unable to look away, trapped in the cold, dark depths of her eyes.

“You are Sir Norman Kingsley,” she stated, not a question but an affirmation.

“I am, madam,” he replied, his voice steady.

“And you are the man who, not an hour ago, stood in this hall and called my husband the ugliest man in the world.”

A nervous titter went through the court, quickly suppressed. The dwarf looked as though he might actually explode. Sir Norman braced himself. This was it, the final insult before the axe. “I spoke what I saw, your majesty,” he said, a last spark of defiance flaring within him.

The queen’s lips twitched again in that cold, unnerving smile. “And you also called me,” she continued, her voice dropping to a near whisper, “the most beautiful of women.”

The silence in the room was now so profound that Sir Norman fancied he could hear the frantic beating of his own heart. He had said it as a piece of reckless flattery, a desperate gambit in a hopeless game. What could he say now? He could only give a slight, almost imperceptible nod.

The queen regarded him for a long moment, her expression unreadable. Then she said, in a voice clear and distinct to every straining ear in the hall, “One cannot, in good conscience, condemn a man for the crime of having eyes in his head, and a tongue honest enough to describe what they see. The first observation was a truth universally acknowledged; the second, a matter of opinion in which we happen to concur.”

A wave of shock rippled through the onlookers. The duke’s jaw dropped so far his face resembled a tragic mask. The dwarf made a choking, gurgling sound in his throat, a sound of pure, impotent rage.

“Your highness,” the queen said, turning back to her husband, her voice losing its gossamer edge and taking on the hard, sharp glint of steel. “You have had your amusement with this prisoner. You have played at being a judge, and you have, by your own bungling, been made a fool of twice in one night. This entertainment is concluded. I am bored with it.”

She rose to her feet, a fluid, effortless motion that was in itself an act of command. She was taller than he had imagined, a column of white skin and purple velvet, radiating an aura of absolute authority. “The sentence of death passed by this ridiculous mockery of a court is commuted,” she declared. The collective sigh of relief from the onlookers was clearly audible. Sir Norman felt his knees weaken, a dizzying lightness in his head.

“However,” she went on, and the word fell like a hammer blow, silencing the whispers, “the man is, by his own admission, a spy. He came here to pry into our affairs. Such an offense cannot go unpunished. Nor can such a useful talent for seeing and hearing be allowed to go to waste.”

She looked down at Sir Norman, and her eyes held a strange, predatory gleam. “He will not be executed. He will not be imprisoned.” She paused, savoring the moment, the absolute, rapt attention of every person in the hall. “From this moment, Sir Norman Kingsley, your life is forfeit to me. You are no longer a spy for King Charles; you are a sentinel for Queen Miranda. You will serve as my personal guard, a post which has been conveniently vacant. You will stand by my side, you will walk at my heel, and your eyes will be my eyes. You are bound to my service, and should you ever fail me, or displease me, or attempt to flee, the original sentence will be carried out. Instantly. And without the tedious formality of a trial.”

She did not wait for a response. She turned away from him as if he were a piece of furniture she had just arranged to her liking. “The court is dismissed,” she announced to the room at large. “We are weary.”

With that, she swept from the dais, her velvet train whispering over the stone floor. The guards, who had been frozen in indecision, now moved with purpose, but not toward Sir Norman. Two of them fell in behind the queen, forming an honor guard. The dwarf, his face black with a thunderous, murderous hatred, stood alone and forgotten, watching her go. The duke and the other nobles, eager to escape the charged atmosphere, began to disperse, chattering in low, excited tones.

One of the guards approached Sir Norman, but not with manacles. He merely gestured with his pike towards the space beside the now-empty throne. “Your post, my lord,” he said, with a tone that was a mixture of deference and cynical amusement.

Sir Norman Kingsley, the man who an hour ago had been reflecting on the headsman's axe, moved as if in a dream. He was not a prisoner, but he was not free. He was not condemned, but his life hung by a thread far more fragile than the one that held the sword of Damocles; it hung upon the whim of a woman who was as beautiful as a goddess, as cold as a glacier, and as predictable as a lightning strike. He took his place, his hand resting on the hilt of his sword, and watched as the queen disappeared through a far doorway, a regal vision in purple and disdain. He had escaped the simple, honest chop of an axe, only to find himself a player in a game whose rules he did not know, on a board where the squares shifted beneath his feet, with a queen whose motives were as deep and as dark as her mysterious eyes. And he had the sinking feeling that his scrape, to which the plague had seemed a trifle, had only just begun.

They were his keepers, and yet his subordinates; his jailers, and yet the first subjects of his new and most unwelcome command. The great hall, lately so full of life and light and the glitter of jewels, was now a cavern of echoing silence, lit only by the sputtering torches that cast long, dancing shadows upon the stone. It seemed to Sir Norman that hours, not minutes, had passed since he had stood in this same spot, a condemned man awaiting the fall of an axe; now he was a sentinel, a living statue, whose only purpose was to guard the memory of a departed queen.

He stood as still as the stone griffins that flanked the dais, his mind a whirlwind of confusion and a strange, unwelcome relief that was colder than any fear. The headsman's axe had been stayed, it was true; but in its place hung a sword of silk, a thread of the queen's whim, infinitely more terrifying in its uncertainty. He had bartered a swift, clean death for a life of perpetual jeopardy, where a single misstep, a misplaced word, or a look that displeased his new mistress could send him to the very fate he had just escaped. He reflected, with a grim and bitter humor, that he had merely exchanged one executioner for another; and that the latter, being a beautiful woman, was likely to be the more capricious and cruel of the two.

His somber reflections were interrupted by a sound, a sort of guttural chuckle, that seemed to bubble up from the very stones beneath him. He looked down and saw that he was not, after all, alone. His highness, Prince Caliban, whose face had remained a mask of thunderous fury, did not follow the others, but instead turned his malevolent gaze upon the new guardsman, his lips writhing back from his yellowed teeth in a silent snarl. The duke and the lord chancellor had also lingered, standing behind the dwarf like two malevolent shadows, their expressions a mixture of outrage and thwarted ambition.

“Well, well, my pretty popinjay,” the dwarf began, his voice a rasping poison. “So you have found favor in her majesty's eyes. A spy one moment, a royal pet the next. It is a swift promotion, even for this court.”

“It is a position I did not seek, your highness,” Sir Norman replied, his voice level and cold, his eyes fixed on a point just over the dwarf's misshapen head.

“Oh, I have no doubt of that!” Caliban sneered, taking a step closer, his small eyes glittering with a spite so profound it was almost sublime. “You would have preferred the block, would you not? A quick end to your troubles. But her majesty is given to strange fancies. She collects things; pretty stones, rare birds, and now, it seems, handsome traitors. But I would advise you not to grow too comfortable in your new perch, Sir Norman Kingsley.”

He hobbled around Sir Norman, inspecting him as a butcher might inspect a prize hog, his knuckles dragging ever so slightly upon the floor. “She tires of her toys as quickly as she acquires them. One day you are her favorite plaything, the next you are cast aside, forgotten, or broken. And when she does tire of you,” he paused, and his grin widened into a ghastly crescent, “I shall be waiting. The sentence of this court still stands, you know. It is merely… postponed. And I am a very, very patient man.”

“Your highness is too kind to concern yourself with my fate,” Sir Norman said, his contempt a thin veneer over the chilling truth of the dwarf's words.

“Silence, you insolent dog!” roared the duke, stepping forward. “You forget to whom you speak!”

“On the contrary,” Sir Norman answered, his gaze finally dropping to meet the dwarf's. “I am acutely aware to whom I speak. It is a lesson this court has taken great pains to teach me.”

Before the duke could retort, the dwarf held up a hand, his grin never faltering. “Let him have his sharp tongue, my lord. It is all he has left. His sword belongs to the queen, his life belongs to the queen, and soon enough, his head will belong to me. Enjoy your brief reprieve, spy. Stand straight, look handsome, and try not to displease our glorious Miranda. For I shall be watching your every move, and I shall treasure every stumble.”

With a final, lingering look of pure hatred, the dwarf turned and shuffled away, his cronies trailing in his wake like vultures following a leper. The great doors at the end of the hall boomed shut behind them, and the silence that returned was heavier, more menacing than before. Sir Norman was left alone with his two guards, who had not moved a muscle during the entire exchange, their faces as impassive as their steel helmets. He felt a bead of cold sweat trace a path down his spine; the dwarf’s threat was no idle boast, but a solemn promise, and Sir Norman knew with a dreadful certainty that Prince Caliban would relish its fulfillment more than any king would relish his crown.

He did not know how long he stood there, a prisoner on his own strange pedestal, but it must have been a considerable time. The torches began to burn low, their flames turning from a bright yellow to a smoky, sullen red. The great hall grew colder, and the moonlight that slanted through the high, arched windows seemed to draw all warmth from the air. He was beginning to think he had been forgotten entirely, left to stand guard over an empty room until dawn, when a new figure emerged from the same doorway through which the queen had vanished.

It was one of the gauzy nymphs, as he had mentally dubbed them; a young woman with a pale, anxious face and eyes the color of a summer sky. She approached him not with the fawning reverence of the guards, but with a skittish sort of haste, as if the very shadows might leap out and devour her.

“Sir Norman Kingsley?” she whispered, her voice trembling.

“I am he.”

“Her majesty bids you attend her. In her private apartments. At once.” She cast a fearful glance back over her shoulder, as if the dwarf himself might be lurking there. “Follow me. And for the love of heaven, walk softly.”

Sir Norman gestured to his two guards, who seemed uncertain what to do. “Her majesty summoned me,” he said, with an authority he did not feel. “You will remain here.”

The guards exchanged a look, but a subtle nod from one to the other confirmed their obedience. Sir Norman fell into step behind the lady-in-waiting, his boots making no sound on the cold flagstones. He had thought the corridors of the court were a labyrinth before, but now, guided by the timid nymph, he was led into a maze of passages so narrow and winding that he knew he could never find his way back on his own. They passed no other soul; the castle seemed deserted, a sleeping giant whose dreams were filled with treachery and murder. The tapestries on the walls here were different, depicting not battles and coronations, but strange, mythological scenes of transformation and capture; nymphs turning into trees, men into beasts, and goddesses ensnaring mortals in webs of gold.

Finally, his guide stopped before a heavy door of polished oak, bound with straps of black iron. She did not knock, but pushed it open a mere crack, and gestured for him to enter. “Her majesty awaits,” she breathed, and then, with the quickness of a startled fawn, she was gone, her light footsteps vanishing down the corridor.

Taking a steadying breath, Sir Norman pushed the door open and stepped inside. If the courtroom had been a stage for public power, this chamber was a shrine to private caprice. The room was circular, with a high, domed ceiling painted with the constellations of the night sky. There was no fire in the hearth, and the only light came from a hundred slender candles, whose flames barely flickered in the still, cold air, making the chamber feel like the inside of a sepulcher. Priceless silks from the Orient were draped over furniture of stark, black ebony, and the air was thick with the scent of night-blooming jasmine, a fragrance both sweet and funereal.

The queen was there. She had shed her purple velvet and her crown, and was now clad in a simple gown of white silk that clung to her form and made her look like a wraith in the candlelight. She was seated not upon a throne, but in a simple chair by a vast, uncurtained window, through which the cold moonlight streamed, bleaching the color from her gown and turning her skin to polished alabaster. She was not looking at him, but out at the dark, sleeping landscape, her profile as perfect and as remote as a figure on an ancient coin.

For a long time, she said nothing, and Sir Norman remained by the door, silent and watchful, every inch the sentinel she had decreed him to be. He had faced cannon fire with less trepidation than he felt now, standing in this silent room with this silent woman, whose whim was the sole arbiter of his existence.

At last, she spoke, her voice low and clear, without a trace of its former imperious fire. “You are wondering why I spared you.”

It was not a question, but a statement of fact. Sir Norman, choosing his words with the care of a man walking through a minefield, answered simply. “The thought had crossed my mind, your majesty.”

She turned her head slowly, and her dark eyes, now free of the disdainful veil of her lashes, fixed upon him. In the candlelight, they seemed vast, bottomless pools of ink. “Prince Caliban believes I was taken with your handsome face. The duke believes I wished to slight him. They are both fools.” She paused, and a flicker of something that was not quite a smile touched her lips. “Though it is true that I delight in slighting the duke, and your face is not entirely disagreeable to look upon.”

She rose from her chair and glided towards him, the silk of her gown whispering against the stone floor. She stopped a mere arm's length away, forcing him to meet her gaze. “I spared you, Sir Norman Kingsley, for the same reason a chess master spares a captured pawn. Because I may yet have a use for it. You showed no fear in my court. You defied the dwarf, you mocked the duke, and you faced a death sentence with contempt. Such qualities are rare. And they are useful.”

“Useful for what, your majesty?” he asked, his voice a low murmur.

“This court,” she said, turning away and beginning to pace the room, her hands clasped behind her back, “is a viper's nest. My nobles plot against each other. My husband plots against me. They all smile to my face and sharpen their daggers behind my back. They think me a fool, an iceberg, a creature of cold whims. Let them. An underestimated queen is a queen who survives.”

She stopped before a large, ornate chest of dark wood and ran her long, slender fingers over its carved surface. “You said you were sent to see and to hear. Very well. You shall see and hear for me. You will be my eyes in the shadows, my ears at the keyholes. Your defiance, which so offended my court, will be your shield. No one will suspect the queen's personal guard, the defiant spy, of being the queen's personal agent.”

She looked at him over her shoulder, her expression unreadable. “Lady Castlemaine, whose trial was so conveniently postponed, is the duke's lover. He sought to use her trial to embarrass me, to reveal secrets of the court that he believes I do not know. The poison she was accused of using was intended for Prince Caliban. An unfortunate miss. The Duchess of Sutherland was simply in the wrong place at the wrong time.”

Sir Norman felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room. He had stumbled not into a simple scrape, but into a warren of interlocking conspiracies, where everyone was a target, and everyone was a threat.

“What would you have me do, your majesty?” he asked, his role becoming clearer, and more deadly, by the second.

“For now, you will do nothing,” she said, turning back to the window. “You will stand, and you will watch. You will learn the faces, the whispers, the alliances. You will become part of the scenery, as invisible as the air. But when I give you an order, you will obey it without question and without hesitation. Your first loyalty is not to King Charles, not anymore. It is not even to yourself. It is to me. Do you understand?”

“I understand that my life depends upon it,” he replied, the words tasting like ash in his mouth.

“Precisely.” A genuine, chilling smile finally graced her lips. “It is so much more effective than a mere oath of fealty, is it not? Fear is the only loyalty that cannot be bought or betrayed.” She gestured to a small, unassuming door he had not noticed before. “Your post will be outside this chamber. There is a cot in the antechamber. You will sleep when I permit it. You will eat what is brought to you. You will speak to no one unless I command it. You are a sentinel. And a sentinel's watch never ends.”

She dismissed him with a wave of her hand, her attention already returning to the moonlit landscape outside, as if he had ceased to exist. Sir Norman bowed, a stiff, formal gesture, and backed out of the room, pulling the heavy door shut behind him. He found himself in a small, bare stone room, furnished with nothing but a narrow cot and a single stool. One of the impassive guards stood by the outer door, holding a tray with a piece of bread and a cup of water.

The guard set the tray on the floor and spoke in a low monotone. “Your quarters, my lord.”

Sir Norman looked from the cot to the hard bread, and then to the door behind which the queen held court with the moon. He had escaped the simple, honest chop of an axe, only to find himself a player in a game whose rules he did not know, on a board where the squares shifted beneath his feet, with a queen whose motives were as deep and as dark as her mysterious eyes. He was no longer a man, but a tool; no longer a knight, but a pawn, moved by an unseen hand in a game of life and death. And he had the sinking feeling that his scrape, to which the plague had seemed a trifle, had only just begun. The freedom of the gallows seemed a paradise compared to the gilded cage in which he was now imprisoned.

As the soldiers laid hands upon him, their grips firm but not unnecessarily brutal, he did as he was bid, for what choice had he? He offered no resistance, but cast one final, searching look at the queen. She did not meet his gaze; her attention was fixed upon the dwarf, her expression a mask of such imperial contempt that it seemed a wonder his highness did not shrivel under its cold fire. For a man who had faced death but moments before, it was a strange thing to be grateful for a dungeon, and yet a cell seemed a sanctuary compared to the suffocating splendor of that room, and the terrible, silent warfare waged between the royal spouses. He was led through a side door, down a winding flight of stone steps that were slick with a damp, ancient chill, the air growing heavy and tasting of mildew and despair. The guards halted before a heavy iron-bound door, one of them drawing a great, rusty key from his belt and wrestling with the lock, which groaned in protest. “In you go, my lord,” the man said, his tone not unkindly, “'tis not the royal suite, but it will serve.” The door swung inward, revealing a space of utter blackness, from which a foul air issued, as from a long-sealed tomb. Sir Norman was propelled inside, and the door was slammed shut behind him, the key turning in the lock with a sound of dreadful finality. For a moment he stood in absolute darkness, his heart hammering against his ribs, not with fear of the gloom, but with the sudden, crushing weight of his new reality. He had been a knight, a free man, an agent of his king; now he was a prisoner, a pawn, the personal property of a woman who was as beautiful as the dawn and as merciless as the grave. He was a sentinel, she had said, but a sentinel watching over what? A dungeon door. He felt his way along a damp stone wall until his hand met the rough wood of a low cot, and he sank down upon it, the straw-filled mattress rustling beneath him. The trial of Lady Castlemaine. He had all but forgotten. The queen’s decree had been to hold all executions at once, a matter of grim convenience, it seemed, like postponing a household chore. He wondered who this lady was, and what she had done to earn the enmity of this terrible little court. Poisoning the Duchess of Sutherland, the duke had said. It sounded a grand and awful crime, fit for such a grand and awful place. He strained his ears, but the stone walls were thick, and only the faintest murmur of sound reached him from the world above. Time passed in that lightless void, an hour, perhaps two; he had no way to reckon it. He was lost in a sea of his own turbulent thoughts, reliving the swift, farcical trial, the cold signing of his death warrant, and the queen's sudden, inexplicable intervention. Why had she saved him? It was not for mercy's sake, of that he was certain. A woman who could condemn a man to death without lifting her eyelashes was not given to sentimental pity. No, it was a move in a game, a game played against her grotesque husband and his coterie of nobles. She had seen a use for him, a piece to be placed upon the board, and had plucked him from the headsman's block as a child might pluck a curious pebble from the shore. The thought was no comfort; it merely exchanged one form of doom for another, the quick mercy of the axe for the slow, uncertain torment of servitude to a capricious tyrant. A sudden noise startled him from his reverie; the sound of the key, once more grating in the lock. The door creaked open, and a sliver of torchlight cut through the blackness, blinding him for a moment. It was one of the guards. “Her majesty's orders,” the man grunted, thrusting a torch into a sconce by the door, which cast flickering, monstrous shadows across the small cell. “You are to be returned to the antechamber. The other trial is done.” Sir Norman rose, his limbs stiff, and followed the guard back up the winding stairs, his eyes adjusting to the light. He was led not back into the great hall, but to the small, bare room where he had first been told of his new station. The impassive guard stood there still, as if he had never moved. His fellow soldier gave a curt nod and departed, leaving Sir Norman once more in the quiet antechamber, the heavy door to the queen’s chambers before him, the cot and stool his only companions. He was a sentinel again, his watch resumed. Scarcely had a minute passed when the great door to the hall opened, and the court began to file out. First came the lesser nobles, chattering in low voices, their faces a mixture of excitement and fear, like ghouls departing a satisfying feast. Then came the duke, his face still flushed with indignation, walking shoulder to shoulder with his highness, Prince Caliban. The dwarf caught sight of Sir Norman standing by the queen's door, and his perpetual grin widened into a snarl of pure malice. A wise precaution. A still tongue makes for a loyal servant, does it not, my lord duke?” The duke grunted his assent, looking at Sir Norman with undisguised contempt. “Her majesty has peculiar tastes in servants, as in all things. We shall see how defiant he is then. He is a spy and a murderer, and he belongs in a dungeon, not guarding the queen’s door.” Sir Norman’s hands clenched into fists at his sides, his knuckles white. To be taunted by this monstrous creature, this caricature of a man, was a trial far greater than the one he had faced before the green table. Every instinct screamed at him to answer, to strike the jeering smile from that hideous face, but the queen’s words echoed in his mind. *Your life depends upon it.* He held his ground, his expression unchanging, his silence absolute. The dwarf’s grin faltered, a flicker of frustration crossing his features. He had expected a reaction, an outburst, something he could use. Sir Norman’s unnatural stillness seemed to unnerve him more than any retort could have. “Bah!” he spat, turning away in disgust. “Let her have her toy. Toys can be broken.” He and the duke swept past, their footsteps receding down the corridor. Sir Norman let out a slow, quiet breath, the tension draining from him. He had passed his first test. Then he saw her. Lady Castlemaine was being led out by two guards, her hands bound before her. She was a tall, slender woman, no longer in the first blush of youth, but with a pale, aristocratic beauty that was now marred by terror. Her elaborate coiffure was disheveled, and her gown of silver brocade was torn at the shoulder. Her eyes, wide and dark with fear, darted around the corridor as if seeking a final, hopeless avenue of escape. They fell upon Sir Norman, and for a moment, a flicker of desperate appeal lit them. He could do nothing. He was as much a prisoner as she. He merely stood, a statue in the queen's livery, and watched as she was led toward the same stone steps down which he himself had been taken. She gave a small, choked sob as she passed him, the sound of a heart breaking, and then she was gone, her footsteps and those of her guards descending into the waiting silence. The sentence had been passed. The axe would not be cheated of its due, it seemed; its feast was merely postponed. The corridor was empty now, save for him and the impassive guard at the far end. A moment later, the door to the queen's chamber opened. One of the gauzy nymphs, the same one who had presented the death warrant, beckoned to him. He entered, his heart once more a cold knot in his chest. The queen stood alone in the center of the vast room, which was now lit only by the silver moonlight pouring through the great window. She had dismissed her ladies. She turned as he entered, her face unreadable in the dim light. “You did well,” she said, her voice low and cool. “My husband enjoys baiting my servants. Your silence will have infuriated him more than any insolence.” “I merely obeyed your majesty’s command,” he said, his voice level. “Indeed.” She glided closer, the moonlight catching the jewels at her throat, making them glitter like chips of ice. “And what did you think, Sir Norman, as you watched them lead Lady Castlemaine to the dungeons?” The question caught him off guard. He had expected an order, not a query about his thoughts. “I thought that she was a woman condemned,” he answered carefully, “and that I was powerless to help her.” “You are not powerless,” the queen corrected him, her voice dropping to a whisper. “You are merely restrained. There is a difference. Power held in reserve is the greatest power of all. Lady Castlemaine was a fool. She chose the wrong side. She put her faith in my husband and the duke, thinking their power was absolute. She conspired with them, and when the conspiracy was discovered, they sacrificed her to save themselves. That is the way of this court. Alliances are fleeting, and loyalty is a commodity to be discarded when it is no longer convenient.” She paused, her dark eyes searching his face as if to gauge the effect of her words. “She was accused of poisoning the Duchess of Sutherland. Was she guilty?” Sir Norman asked, emboldened by her strange candor. The queen laughed, a soft, mirthless sound that held no warmth. “Guilty? What is guilt in this place? The Duchess of Sutherland is not dead; she is not even ill. She merely suffers from an unfortunate allergy to strawberries, a fact the duke and my husband conveniently forgot when they needed a charge to bring against their former confidante. Lady Castlemaine’s crime was not poisoning, but indiscretion. She knew too much about their dealings with our enemies across the sea. They feared she would betray them, so they betrayed her first.” Sir Norman felt a fresh wave of revulsion. This was not a court of justice; it was a nest of vipers, each one ready to strike the other. “And your majesty allowed this… this travesty?” “Allowed it?” Her eyes flashed with that familiar fire. “I orchestrated it. It was I who 'discovered' the plot. It was I who made sure the evidence, flimsy as it was, was brought to light. The duke and his highness thought to rid themselves of a dangerous associate. They did not realize they were walking into a trap of my own design.” She was a spider, and this entire court was her web. He had seen her as a beautiful, cold tyrant; now he saw something more, something infinitely more dangerous. Not you, not I, and certainly not Julia Castlemaine. She made her choices. And her death serves a purpose. It weakens the duke. It frightens my husband. A position of great trust. A position that requires a confidante who is loyal, discreet, and utterly beholden to me. She is the youngest of my ladies, the one they all dismiss as a flighty, silly child. They are wrong. She is clever, and she is ambitious. And, most importantly, she is terrified of the dwarf. Sir Norman bowed and retreated to the antechamber, his mind reeling. He had been spared the axe only to be made a keeper, a spy, a tool in the queen’s intricate and deadly machinations. He looked at the hard cot, at the cold stone walls. The simple freedom of the gallows, where a man might die with his honor intact, seemed a distant and impossible paradise. His scrape was no longer a scrape; it was a labyrinth, and with every step he took, he was being drawn deeper into its dark and treacherous heart. The night was far from over.

The night, as he had suspected, was indeed very far from over.

He had not been long in this state of uneasy contemplation when the sound of approaching footsteps echoed down the corridor, and the guard he had dispatched reappeared, bearing not only a loaf of coarse but welcome bread and a skin of wine, but also a slab of cheese that might have served a lesser man for a week's rations. The man deposited the provisions upon a stone bench with a clatter and a thud, and then resumed his post with the impassive stolidity of his profession, his face a mask that revealed nothing. Sir Norman, whose appetite had returned with a vengeance, fell upon the humble fare as though it were the finest feast in Christendom, his thoughts for the moment occupied with the simple, satisfying business of staying alive. The wine was sour, the bread was hard, and the cheese was strong enough to walk on its own, but to a man who had faced his own execution not an hour before, it was a banquet of the gods. He was in the very act of breaking a piece from the loaf when the heavy bar on the ladies' door was drawn back with a grating shriek of old iron, and the door itself swung inward just enough to permit a single figure to slip through into the torchlight.

It was, of course, the Lady Eleanor.

She closed the door softly behind her, leaving it unbarred, and stood for a moment with her back to it, a vision of pale silk and shimmering hair against the dark wood. She watched him chew his bread for a moment in silence, her head tilted to one side like a curious bird. The vapid innocence he had noted earlier was entirely gone, replaced by a shrewd and calculating light in her blue eyes; the silly, girlish smile was nowhere to be seen.

“I trust the accommodations are to your liking, Sir Knight?” she asked, her voice low and musical, but with a new edge of irony that had not been present before. “The queen’s hospitality, you will find, is full of such charming inconsistencies; a death sentence one moment, bread and cheese the next.”

Sir Norman swallowed his mouthful and took a slow draught of the sour wine, his eyes never leaving hers. “A man who complains about his rations, my lady,” he said, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, “is a man who has forgotten how to be grateful for the gift of another breath. Your queen, it seems, enjoys reminding men of such simple truths.”

A flicker of an appreciative smile touched her lips, a genuine one this time, and it transformed her face, making her look both older and infinitely more interesting. “My queen, as you call her, enjoys a great many things, Sir Norman. Most of them dangerous. You have made yourself the most fascinating of her new playthings; I trust you have the constitution for it.”

“And you, my lady? Are you one of her playthings as well?” he countered, his voice quiet. “Or do you merely enjoy watching the game?”

She laughed, a soft, genuine laugh this time, without a trace of the earlier manufactured giggle. “Oh, I am a player, Sir Norman. Of a sort. We are all players here, in Miranda’s court. The trick is to know which game you are in, and to ensure you are not merely a pawn in someone else’s. You, for instance, are now a castle, set to protect a queen.” She took a step closer, her silk gown whispering over the stone flags. “But who is the queen on this board? Is it Miranda, who placed you here? Or is it me,” she tapped her own chest lightly with a slender finger, “whom you are meant to be guarding?”

This was it, the heart of the matter. The question he had been asking himself. “Her majesty’s commands were explicit,” he answered, his tone carefully neutral. “I am to ensure the safety of all the ladies within that room. Your own included.”

“Ah, but there is safety, and then there is safety,” she mused, now circling the small stone bench where he sat, her movements as fluid and graceful as a cat’s. “Is it my person you are to guard, or my loyalties? Am I to be kept safe from harm, or simply kept? Miranda does not like loose ends, Sir Norman. She does not like secrets she does not own. And I,” she paused, her back to him now, looking down the long, dark corridor, “am full of secrets.”

He said nothing, merely watching her, feeling the shift in the very air of the corridor. This was no silly child; this was a woman as sharp and as dangerous, in her own way, as the queen herself. She was a different kind of weapon; not a broadsword, but a stiletto, hidden and deadly.

“She thinks I am a spy for her enemies,” Eleanor continued, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “For the old guard, the ones who whisper that a dwarf, even a royal one, has no true place on a throne. She thinks I carry messages, that I plot against her in the rustle of my silks and the flutter of my fan.” She turned to face him again, and her blue eyes were wide, but not with innocence; they were wide with a kind of desperate sincerity. “And she is not entirely wrong.”

Sir Norman felt a cold knot form in his stomach, a chill that had nothing to do with the drafty castle. He had been set to guard a traitor. Or a woman pretending to be a traitor. Or a woman pretending to be a woman pretending to be a traitor. The labyrinth was growing deeper, its walls closing in.

“Why are you telling me this?” he asked, his voice a low rasp. “Do you take me for a fool, my lady? Or are you so confident in your own charms that you think I would betray the queen who holds my life in her hand, for a pretty face and a whispered confidence?”

“Neither,” she said, her expression serious. “I take you for a man who has looked death in the face and not flinched. I take you for a man who spoke truth to the duke and mocked the dwarf, knowing it would cost him his head. Such a man is either a fool, as you say, or he is a man who values his own honor above his own life. I am gambling that you are the latter. The queen may own your life, Sir Norman, but you are the only one who can command your honor.”

She glided closer still, until she was standing directly before him, so close he could smell the faint scent of lavender and something else, something wilder, like rain on stone. “I am no traitor to the queen,” she said, her voice barely audible. “I am a traitor to the Prince Consort, Caliban. They are not the same thing, though he would have all the world believe they are. He is the dragon, Sir Norman. Not the one with scales and fire, but the one with a crown and a green table. He poisons the court from within, and he will poison the queen’s reign until it withers and dies, and he alone is left upon the throne. I work to save her from him; and for that, she suspects me of working to destroy her.”

Before he could process the full weight of this revelation, this impossibly dangerous confidence, a new sound intruded upon their secluded drama. It was not the steady tread of a guard, but a shuffling, uneven gait, accompanied by a low, guttural chuckling that seemed to rise from the very stones of the floor. It was a sound he recognized with a feeling of profound dread.

Lady Eleanor’s face went white. The shrewd, confident player was gone in an instant, replaced by a girl seized with genuine terror. “He is coming,” she whispered, her eyes wide with fright. “You must hide me. He cannot find me here with you!”

But there was nowhere to hide. The corridor was a straight, narrow channel of stone. The torchlight was merciless. As the shuffling footsteps grew closer, Sir Norman rose slowly to his feet, placing himself between the approaching sound and the Lady Eleanor, his hand instinctively resting on the hilt of his sword.

And then he appeared, emerging from the shadows at the far end of the hall. His Royal Highness Prince Caliban.

His highness’s face, never a thing of beauty, was now a mask of such concentrated malevolence as would have curdled milk at fifty paces; his lips were drawn back from his teeth in a snarl that was the very antithesis of his former mirth, and his little eyes glowed like coals in the shadowed distortion of his features. The queen had thwarted him, and his rage, having been denied its primary target, was now seeking a new one. He stopped some twenty feet away, his huge head swiveling as he took in the scene: the two guards, standing like statues; the lady, cowering behind the tall knight; and the knight himself, standing defiant, a half-eaten loaf of bread on the bench behind him.

“Well, well, well,” the dwarf rasped, his voice like stones grinding together. “What have we here? The condemned man, enjoying a last supper. And with such charming company! I was not aware, Sir Norman, that my wife’s generosity extended to providing female companionship for her spies before their execution.”

“His highness is mistaken,” Sir Norman said, his voice level and cold, projecting a calm he was very far from feeling. “The Lady Eleanor was feeling faint. I was merely ensuring she did not fall.”

“Faint!” The dwarf let out a short, barking laugh. “She looks more than faint; she looks terrified. As well she should. People who consort with spies and traitors have a tendency to end up in the same predicament.” He took another shuffling step forward, his eyes fixed not on Sir Norman, but on the lady trembling behind him. “Come here, my dear Eleanor. Step away from the dead man. We have things to discuss, you and I. Matters of loyalty. Secrets. The sort of things a husband has a right to know.”

“The lady will remain where she is,” Sir Norman stated, not as a request, but as a fact. He did not move, did not draw his sword, but his entire posture was a barrier, a challenge.

The dwarf’s grin widened, a truly terrible sight. “Oh, will she? And who are you to say? You are a prisoner. Your sentence has been passed. The queen, in a moment of feminine whimsy, has merely delayed the swing of the axe. A delay I can rectify at any moment. Guards!” he snarled, turning his glowing eyes to the two soldiers. “My wife’s orders were to take him to a dungeon. This is not a dungeon. This is a corridor. Take him. Now.”

The two guards exchanged a look of profound unease. They were caught between the hammer and the anvil; the queen’s order, and the Prince Consort’s. One held the power of life and death, the other held the power of immediate, terrifying violence.

“Our orders, your highness,” the first guard said, his voice strained, “were from her majesty. She placed the prisoner here as a sentinel.”

“A sentinel!” Caliban shrieked, his voice rising in fury. “A sentinel for what? For her little pet plotter? I am the Prince Consort! I am the master of this castle! My word is law! Seize him, I say, or you will take his place in the Black Chamber!”

The guards hesitated, their hands twitching on the hilts of their swords. Sir Norman knew this was the moment of crisis. If the guards obeyed the dwarf, all was lost. He would be dragged away, and Eleanor would be left to the prince’s tender mercies, which he imagined were nonexistent. He had to hold the line, not with force, which was impossible, but with the only authority he had been given.

“The queen’s authority,” Sir Norman said, his voice cutting through the dwarf’s tirade like cold steel. “You question her majesty’s judgment, your highness? You seek to countermand her direct order, given before the entire court? An order concerning a prisoner whose case she has taken a personal interest in?” He let the words hang in the air, each one a carefully chosen barb. He was using the queen’s power as a shield, hoping it was strong enough to withstand the dwarf’s rage.

Caliban’s face went from red to a mottled purple. He was an animal of pure impulse, and he was being checked by rules he despised. He wanted to give the order to kill, to maim, to destroy, but the specter of his wife, the cold, imperious queen, stayed his hand. He knew, as everyone in the castle knew, that to openly defy her in a matter she had claimed as her own was a form of suicide.

“She is not here now, is she, spy?” the dwarf hissed, taking another step forward, his knuckles white where he gripped the ornate handle of a dagger at his belt. “The iceberg queen is in her chambers, dreaming of new cruelties. But I am here. And I see a traitor consorting with a spy. A crime that requires immediate justice.”

“And I see a loyal subject,” Sir Norman countered, “obeying his queen’s command. And another loyal subject, a lady of the court, who is under my protection by that same command. If you wish to harm her, your highness, you will have to pass through me. And if you do that, you will have to explain to her majesty how her chosen sentinel came to be indisposed, and what you were doing alone in a corridor with one of her ladies-in-waiting in the dead of night.”

It was a bluff, a desperate gamble, piling inference upon innuendo, but it was all he had. He was framing the dwarf’s actions in the language of a court of law, the very court the dwarf had just presided over with such glee.

For a long moment, the corridor was utterly silent, save for the sputtering of the torches and the dwarf’s heavy, labored breathing. Caliban stared at Sir Norman, his little eyes burning with a hatred so pure it was almost a physical force. He looked from the knight’s implacable face to the pale, frightened girl behind him, and then to the two guards who now stood more rigidly than ever, their gazes fixed on the wall ahead, determined to see and hear nothing. He was beaten, for the moment. The invisible chains of his wife’s authority held him fast.

With a final, guttural snarl of frustration, the dwarf spat upon the stone floor. “Enjoy your brief reprieve, spy,” he rasped. “Enjoy the protection of your ice queen. The night is long, and there are many corridors in this castle. And the axe will still be sharp in the morning.” He turned his venomous gaze on Eleanor. “As for you, my dear little dove. We will speak again. Soon.”

And with that, he turned and shuffled back into the darkness from whence he came, his angry chuckles echoing back down the corridor until they were swallowed by the silence.

The moment he was gone, Lady Eleanor’s legs seemed to give way, and she sagged against Sir Norman’s back, her breath coming in ragged sobs. He did not move, but stood as he was, a living wall, until her trembling subsided. The immediate danger had passed, but the air was thick with its after-effects. The scrape he had gotten himself into was no longer a scrape; it was a war, and he had just been placed squarely on the front line, between two of its most powerful, and most dangerous, combatants. The queen’s sentinel had held his post. But as he stood there, feeling the faint tremor of the terrified woman behind him, he knew that the cost of holding it was likely to be higher than he could possibly have imagined.

He felt her weight against him, a fragile burden, and heard the small, broken sounds of her weeping, which seemed a thing too delicate for the gross stone and echoing shadows of the passage. The two guards, who had been as still as carved effigies, now shifted their weight, their armor creaking softly, the sound unnaturally loud in the wake of the dwarf’s departure. One of them cleared his throat.

“Her ladyship’s chambers are at the end of this wing, Sir Norman,” the man said, his voice a low rumble, respectful and yet carefully neutral. “Second door on the left. We are to escort you.”

“I am grateful,” Sir Norman replied, his own voice steady, though his heart was still beating a heavy tattoo against his ribs, a frantic drumbeat of a battle narrowly won. He did not turn to Lady Eleanor, but spoke so that she might hear him. “The danger is passed, my lady. He will not trouble you further this night. You have the queen’s own word, and it seems that even her consort is not bold enough to defy it so openly.”

Her sobbing lessened, though her trembling did not. She drew back from him, and he could feel the cold air rush into the space where her warmth had been. “I am… I am sorry, sir,” she whispered, her voice husky with tears. “I am not usually so… so foolish.”

“There is no foolishness in fearing a serpent, my lady,” Sir Norman said, turning at last to look at her. In the wavering torchlight, her face was a pale oval, her eyes huge and dark with remembered terror. “And that one is the most venomous I have ever had the displeasure to meet. Let us see you to your room, where you may be at ease.”

She nodded, pulling her cloak more tightly about her as if for protection, and they proceeded down the corridor, Sir Norman walking beside her, the two guards falling into step behind them like silent, iron-shod ghosts. The castle was a labyrinth of such passages, all of cold stone and hung with ancient, faded tapestries depicting hunts and battles from a bygone age, the woven figures seeming to watch them with sightless eyes. It was a place of secrets, heavy with the weight of years and intrigues, and Sir Norman felt its chill seep into his very bones; it was a chill far colder than the mere absence of the sun.

They reached the door, a plain, unadorned plank of dark wood, and one of the guards moved past them to test the handle. It was unlocked. He pushed it open, revealing a chamber as modest as the door that guarded it. It was small, containing little more than a narrow bed, a washstand with a basin and ewer, and a single wooden chest for her belongings. A lone candle guttered on a small table, casting long, dancing shadows that made the simple room seem full of lurking phantoms. It was the chamber of a poor relation, not a lady of a royal court.

Lady Eleanor hesitated on the threshold. “Will you not… come in, sir? For a moment?”

“The queen’s command was that I stand sentinel,” he said quietly. “My post is here, outside your door.”

“But he… he said he would return,” she breathed, her gaze darting back toward the darkness of the corridor. “He will not dare, not while you are here. But when you are gone…”

“I am not yet gone, my lady,” he assured her. “And I shall remain here until I am commanded otherwise. Now go in and rest. Bar the door from the inside; it will not stop a determined man, but it will give you some peace of mind.”

She looked at him then, a long, searching look, and for the first time he saw something more than fear in her eyes; there was a flicker of gratitude, and a dawning wonder. “Why did you do it?” she asked, her voice barely audible. “To defy him, it is madness. No one defies the prince.”

“Perhaps I am mad,” Sir Norman answered with a faint, grim smile. “Or perhaps I have found that when one’s death is already decreed, there is very little left to be afraid of. A man with nothing to lose can afford a certain recklessness.”

“The queen,” she said, as if to herself. “She saved you. I saw it. I do not understand it. She never does such a thing. She is not… kind.”

“No,” Sir Norman agreed, his mind flashing back to that icily placid face, the beautiful, cold autocrat who had signed his death warrant with one hand and stayed it with the other. “She is not kind. I do not flatter myself that her action was born of any mercy. She plays some game of her own, and I am merely a piece she has chosen to move upon the board. But for now, that move keeps you safe. Take what comfort you can from that, and get some sleep. The night is not yet over.”

She gave a small, jerky nod, and slipped inside her room. He heard the heavy bar fall into place, a solid, reassuring sound. He turned and took up his station beside the door, his back against the cold wall, his arms crossed over his chest. The two guards took up similar positions on the opposite side of the passage, their faces impassive and unreadable in the flickering light. For a long time, the only sound was the distant, mournful sighing of the wind as it worried the castle’s ancient battlements.

An hour crawled by, then another. The torches in their sconces burned low, sputtering and casting a greasy, yellow smoke that stung the eyes. From the direction of the great hall, they heard a brief swell of noise, a raised voice, a woman’s sharp cry, quickly silenced, and then a murmur of grim satisfaction. The trial of Lady Castlemaine had evidently reached its conclusion, and by the sound of it, the verdict had been as swift and merciless as his own. The queen’s court was an efficient machine, and its chief product was death. Sir Norman felt a pang of something akin to pity for the unknown lady, another soul caught in the gears of this terrible place.

One of the guards, the one who had spoken before, a man with a square jaw and a scar that cut through one eyebrow, shifted his feet. “She is a strange one, the queen,” he said, his voice a low whisper, not looking at Sir Norman but staring down the darkened hall.

“That is the one thing in this castle that seems plain to all,” Sir Norman replied, not moving.

“Aye,” the guard grunted. “They say she was born during a storm, on a ship lost in the northern ice fields. They say her mother died giving birth, and the sailors believed the babe was a curse, a spirit of the frost. When they were rescued, the child would not cry, only stare at them all with eyes as old as the sea.” He shook his head. “Folk here say she brought the ice with her. Her heart never thawed.”

“A convenient story to explain a cold nature,” Sir Norman mused.

“Perhaps. Or perhaps it is the truth. With her majesty, who can say? But I have served in this court for ten years, and I can tell you this, sir. She is as changeable as the weather, but she has one constant humor; she despises the dwarf. She hates him with a passion that is the only truly hot thing about her. She married him for his name and his claim, to cement her own. Now she is shackled to him, and he to her. And they play their games of power, day in and day out.”

“And I have been made a pawn in this game,” Sir Norman finished for him.

“The most interesting pawn in some time,” the guard corrected. “He has wanted to be rid of you since you arrived. You are a man of the king, a true knight, everything he is not. Your very presence is an insult to him. And her majesty… she saw that. To thwart him is her greatest pleasure. She would save a condemned murderer from the block if she knew the dwarf wished him dead.” He paused, then added, “But do not mistake it for safety, sir. Her favor is as sharp and as treacherous as a shard of glass. It can protect you one moment, and cut you to the bone the next. Today you are her shield against her husband. Tomorrow, you may be the stone she throws at him. Either way, you will be broken.”

Before Sir Norman could reply to this grim but likely accurate assessment, new footsteps echoed from the far end of the corridor. It was not the shuffling gait of the dwarf, but a light, almost silent tread. A figure emerged from the shadows, one of the queen’s ladies, the same “gauzy nymph” who had presented her with the death warrant. She moved with a liquid grace, her pale gown seeming to float around her, and came to a stop before Sir Norman.

“Her majesty, the queen, requires your presence,” she announced, her voice a melodious, empty chime. She did not look at him, but at a point on the wall just past his shoulder. “You are to come with me. Now.”

The words struck him with the force of a physical blow. So this was it. The game was over. The whim had passed. He was being summoned not for an audience, but for the final act; the journey to the Black Chamber, and the chop with an axe. The guard’s warning echoed in his mind. The stone, having served its purpose, was about to be thrown away. Inside the small room, he heard a faint gasp, and knew that Lady Eleanor had heard and come to the same conclusion.

“And these men?” he asked, indicating the two guards. “Are they to come as well?”

“They are to remain,” the lady-in-waiting said, a hint of impatience in her tone. “The queen wishes to speak with you. Alone.”

Alone. That was a small, curious wrinkle. Executions were rarely private affairs in this court; they seemed to be a form of entertainment. He looked at the guard with the scar, who gave him an infinitesimal shake of the head, a gesture so slight it was barely perceptible. It was not the summons for the axe. It was something else. Something, perhaps, even stranger.

“Very well,” Sir Norman said. He turned to the barred door. “My lady Eleanor,” he called, his voice calm. “I am summoned by the queen. These men will remain to guard you. You have nothing to fear.”

A choked sob was his only answer. With a deep breath, he turned from the door and followed the silent, gliding lady-in-waiting back into the belly of the beast. She led him not toward the great hall, nor in the direction he guessed the dungeons and the Black Chamber to lie, but up a winding stair, through corridors more richly appointed than those below. The tapestries here were vibrant, the torches burned in polished silver sconces, and the air was scented with dried herbs and beeswax. They were entering the queen’s private domain.

Finally, they came to a pair of tall, carved oak doors, inlaid with mother-of-pearl. The lady-in-waiting knocked once, a soft, precise tap.

“Enter,” a voice commanded from within. It was the queen’s voice, clear and cold as a winter bell.

The lady pushed open one of the heavy doors and gestured for him to go inside. He stepped across the threshold, and the door closed silently behind him, leaving him alone with Queen Miranda.

The room was vast and circular, the private study at the top of a tower, with tall, arched windows looking out into the star-dusted blackness of the night. The chamber was not filled with the fripperies he might have expected of a queen; instead, it was dominated by books. Shelves climbed the curved walls from floor to ceiling, crammed with volumes of all shapes and sizes. A great telescope of brass and polished wood stood near one window, aimed at the heavens. In the center of the room, on a circular rug woven with the constellations of the night sky, stood a small table. And on the table was a chessboard, the pieces of carved ivory and jet set in the middle of a game.

The queen herself was standing by one of the windows, her back to him, a tall, slender silhouette against the myriad stars. She wore a gown of dark silk that seemed to drink the light, and her hair was unbound, falling like a cascade of black water down her back. She did not turn as he entered.

“You are a subject of King Charles,” she stated, her voice devoid of inflection. It was not a question.

“I have that honor, your majesty,” he replied, his own voice sounding loud in the quiet room.

“Honor,” she repeated the word, and it sounded like a foreign curiosity on her tongue. “He is a weak king. A sentimentalist, ruled by his favorites and his memories. His court is a nest of flatterers and bankrupt fops. Is it not?”

“I am a soldier, your majesty, not a courtier,” Sir Norman said carefully. “My business is with the king’s enemies, not his friends.”

“And you believe we are his enemies.” Again, it was a statement of fact.

“Your majesties have set yourselves up in opposition to his rule. You have claimed lands that are his by right. That is the common understanding.”

“The common understanding is a fool’s comfort,” she said, turning at last. Her face, in the soft light of the room’s many candles, was even more impossibly beautiful than it had been in the great hall, and even colder. Her dark eyes were not fathomless, as a poet might have said; they were like the polished surface of obsidian, reflecting everything, revealing nothing. “This world is not run by rights, Sir Norman. It is run by power. He who has the power, makes the rights. Your King Charles has the sentiment of tradition on his side. We have this.” She made a small, sweeping gesture that encompassed the grim, warlike castle around them. “Which do you think will prevail in the end?”

“That is for God to decide, and for men to discover in battle,” he answered.

A faint, fleeting smile touched her lips, a tiny crack in the glacial facade. “A soldier’s answer. You are refreshingly direct. It is a novelty. My court is composed entirely of liars, cowards, and my husband.” She let the final word hang in the air, freighted with a universe of contempt. “Tell me, Sir Norman Kingsley, why did you defy him in the hall?”

“He made a mockery of your justice, your majesty.”

“My justice?” she laughed, a short, sharp sound without a trace of mirth. “There is no justice in this hall. There is only the exercise of will. His, and mine. You mistook a battle of wills for a court of law. It was an amusing error.” She moved toward the chessboard and idly picked up a black knight. “When I ordered you to be taken to the dungeons, I gave you a reprieve. My husband was furious. He would have had your head on the floor within the minute. Why do you think I did it?”

“As your majesty said, it was amusing,” he replied, hazarding a guess based on her own words. “And to anger your husband.”

“That is part of it,” she conceded, her eyes fixed on the carved piece in her hand. “His predictable little rages are one of my few reliable entertainments. But it is not the whole of it. I saw you stand before them, condemned and with no hope, and you did not beg. You did not grovel or plead. You met their farce with contempt. You looked at me, as I signed that paper, not with fear, but with… what was it? Disappointment?”

The observation was so unnervingly accurate that it took him a moment to answer. “It was a sharp pain,” he admitted, his voice low, “to see the hand that had once offered salvation so casually decree damnation.”

She looked up at him, her gaze sharp and penetrating. “Salvation? When did I ever offer you salvation?”

“You do not remember? Some months ago, on the road to Fenwick, a carriage was set upon by brigands. A lady traveling within shot one of them with a pistol from the window, which gave her guards the moment they needed to rally and drive the rest off. I was one of those guards. The lady was you, your majesty.”

The queen stared at him, and for a long moment, the absolute stillness in the room was broken only by the faint hiss of the candlewicks. She set the black knight down on the board with a soft click. A flicker of something unreadable passed through her eyes, a shadow of a memory. “A dark green carriage,” she said slowly. “A rainy afternoon. There were three of them.”

“There were, your majesty. The man you shot was their leader. You saved my life that day, and the lives of my comrades.”

“I saved my own life,” she corrected him, her voice returning to its customary coldness. “And my jewels. That you benefited from my actions was a matter of circumstance, not intent. And yet… it is interesting.” She walked a slow circle around the table, her gaze never leaving him. “The man whose life I inadvertently saved turns up in my court as a spy, condemned to die by my own hand. The threads of fate are tangled in a most peculiar pattern, are they not?”

“So it would seem, your majesty.”

“And now,” she continued, coming to a stop before him, “I find myself with a dilemma. You are a spy. The penalty for spying is death. That is the rule. And I am a great believer in rules; they are the framework of power. To ignore them is to weaken the entire structure. However, my husband is determined that you should die. His eagerness on the matter has become… tiresome. It has made your execution his victory, not a simple function of the state. And I do not permit him victories.”

She was so close now that he could see the tiny flecks of gold in her dark irises, like distant, frozen stars.

“So I have a new use for you, Sir Norman Kingsley,” she said, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that was more chilling than any shout. “You are a dead man. Your sentence stands. It is merely… postponed. Indefinitely. You will continue to serve as my sentinel, but your duties will be broader. You are an honest man, or you play the part of one very well, which in this court amounts to the same thing. You will be my eyes and my ears. You will stand guard, you will walk the halls, you will listen. You will report to me, and only to me, what you see and what you hear. You will be my one true piece on a board of treacherous pawns.”

“And in return?” he asked.

“In return, you will continue to draw breath,” she replied with brutal simplicity. “Each day you wake, you will owe it to me. Your life is no longer your own; it is a gift, which I may revoke at any moment, for any reason. If you displease me, if you fail me, if you simply cease to be… interesting… the axe will fall. Do you understand your position?”

“I understand that I am your prisoner, your servant, and your creature,” he said, the words tasting like ash in his mouth.

“You understand perfectly,” she said, and the faint, cold smile returned to her lips. “Your first report, then. My husband. What was his purpose in accosting you and the girl in the corridor?”

“He seemed to believe I was a traitor consorting with a spy,” Sir Norman said, choosing his words with care. “As for the lady, he… expressed an interest in speaking with her again. Alone.”

“He did, did he?” The queen’s eyes narrowed. “The little spider. He collects frightened little flies for his web. The girl, Eleanor, is a distant cousin of the late Duchess of Sutherland, the one Lady Castlemaine was just condemned for poisoning. The dwarf likely believes the girl knows something that may be of use to him. Or he simply wishes to torment her. With Caliban, his motives are always base. You did well to protect her. Henceforth, she is under your specific protection. No harm is to befall her. That is your charge.”

She turned away from him and went back to the chessboard. “The game is at a delicate stage,” she murmured, more to herself than to him. “One wrong move, and the king is lost.” She looked back over her shoulder. “You may go, Sir Norman. Resume your post. We will speak again when I summon you. And remember,” she added, her voice dropping to an icy whisper, “amuse me. Your life depends upon it.”

As the soldiers laid hands upon him, not with the rough haste of executioners but with a certain grudging deference, Sir Norman Kingsley bowed his head, a stiff and formal gesture that felt wholly inadequate to the strange, terrible compact just sealed between them. Backing away from her presence as one might from a caged tigress whose mood was ever uncertain, he turned and quit the chamber, the heavy oak door closing behind him with a sound of finality, like a coffin lid being nailed shut upon a man still breathing. He was, for all intents and purposes, a ghost; a spectre haunting the very halls wherein his life had been forfeit, his existence a fragile bauble in the hands of a woman who seemed to find the breaking of things her chiefest amusement. The guards escorted him not to the dungeons, as the queen’s first command had implied, but back to the great hall, which was now emptying in a confused flutter of silk and velvet, the courtiers dispersing like so many startled birds.

His two escorts took up their positions on either side of him, their faces impassive beneath their steel helmets, and there he stood, a prisoner at liberty, a dead man on duty. The sensation was profoundly bizarre; he felt as though he were watching himself from a great distance, a player in some mad pantomime whose rules were inscrutable. The sentence of death still hung over him, not poised by a hair, as he had imagined before, but held fast by a chain of ice in the queen’s own hand; a chain she might lengthen or shorten, or let drop altogether, at her merest whim. He was her creature, her sentinel, her spy. And his first charge, a duty given with the casual air one might employ to order a change in the draperies, was to find and protect the girl, Eleanor.

He scanned the thinning crowd, his eyes searching for the slight figure in the pale blue gown. The air was thick with whispers, the aftermath of the night's grim entertainments. He caught a fragment of conversation from two passing lords, their faces alight with ghoulish excitement.
“And Lady Castlemaine?” asked the younger one, a fop with an extravagantly curled wig. “Did she confess to the poisoning?”
“Not in so many words,” his companion replied, a portly man whose laughter rumbled in his chest, “but her terror was a confession in itself. Her majesty was much diverted by her pleas; she laughed, I tell you, a sound like the tinkling of icicles, as the lady was dragged away. They say the headsman is sharpening his axe for two strokes instead of one.”
The two men passed on, their callous chatter fading into the general murmur of the hall. Sir Norman felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach. This was the world he now inhabited, a place where a woman’s terror was a source of diversion, and a death sentence a subject for light gossip. His gaze swept the room again, a new urgency propelling him. Where would such a girl, a fawn in a forest of wolves, seek refuge? Not in the main thoroughfares, certainly. He looked towards the shadowed archways and deep-set windows that lined the hall, places where one might shrink from view.

And there, huddled in the recess of a tall, gothic window, half hidden by a heavy tapestry depicting some forgotten battle, he saw her. She was weeping, her shoulders shaking with silent, desolate sobs, her face buried in her hands. He dismissed his guards with a quiet word, a test of his new, peculiar authority, and was obscurely gratified when they hesitated only a moment before stepping back and resuming a post near the main entrance. They had their orders, it seemed, and those orders were to obey him, at least for now.

He approached her slowly, his footsteps making no sound on the thick carpets.
“My lady,” he said, his voice softer than he had intended.
She started violently, her head snapping up, her eyes wide with terror. When she saw who it was, the fear did not recede, but was mingled with a profound and pitiable confusion.
“Sir Norman!” she whispered, her hand flying to her throat. “But they… they condemned you! I heard them!”
“And so they did,” he answered, offering her a smile that he knew was a poor, weary thing. “But as you may have noticed, this court is not always swift to act upon its own decrees. Her majesty has seen fit to postpone the… proceedings.”
“Postpone?” Her brow furrowed, as if the concept were too complex for her terrified mind to grasp. “But the dwarf… he looked so certain! He hates you!”
“His highness is often certain, and rarely pleased when his certainty is proven premature. As for his hatred, it is a burden I must learn to carry.” He looked at her, at the tear-stains on her pale cheeks and the genuine distress in her eyes, and a feeling other than his own grim predicament stirred within him. She was not merely a charge, a task set by the queen; she was a terrified young woman, utterly alone. “The queen has placed you under my protection, Lady Eleanor. No harm will come to you while I am able to prevent it.”
A fresh wave of tears welled in her eyes, but these seemed born of relief rather than despair. “Truly? Oh, Sir Norman, I have been so afraid! Ever since my cousin… the duchess… they say she was poisoned, and that wicked man, the dwarf, he keeps looking at me, as if he thinks I know something of it! But I know nothing, nothing at all!”
“I believe you,” he said simply. “And you need not fear him. His power is great, but it is not absolute.”
“Is that so?” a voice sneered from the shadows behind them, a voice that was a grotesque parody of human speech, like the grinding of stones. “And who, pray tell, is the authority on the limits of my power? The condemned man who yet draws breath only by the fickle charity of my wife?”

Sir Norman turned, placing himself between Eleanor and the newcomer. Prince Caliban emerged from behind the tapestry, his oversized head cocked to one side, his lips pulled back from his teeth in what he imagined was a knowing smile, but which looked more like the rictus of a skull. His diminutive form was clad in black silk, making him appear like a venomous beetle scuttling out of the darkness.
“Your highness,” Sir Norman said, his tone perfectly even. “We were just speaking of you. I was assuring the lady that your bark is considerably worse than your bite.”
The dwarf’s eyes, small and black and intelligent, narrowed to glittering points of malice. “Were you, indeed? A bold statement, from a man whose neck is already measured for the block. And you, my dear,” he said, his gaze sliding past Sir Norman to fix upon the trembling Eleanor, “you have been avoiding me. I expressed a wish for a private conversation. It is unwise to ignore my wishes.”
“The lady is fatigued,” Sir Norman interposed, not moving an inch. “She is overwrought by the night’s events. I am escorting her to her chambers.”
“You are doing nothing of the sort,” Caliban hissed, his malevolence now undisguised. “You are a guard, a walking statue. You will stand where you are told and be silent. The girl comes with me. We have matters to discuss concerning her late cousin, and secrets best shared in private.”
He took a step forward, his hand outstretched as if to grab Eleanor’s arm. Sir Norman’s own hand moved faster, not to his sword, which would have been folly, but to grasp the dwarf’s wrist in a grip of iron. It was a shocking breach of etiquette, an assault on royalty, and for a moment the air crackled with the enormity of his action. The dwarf froze, his face a mask of astonished fury, his small body rigid.
“Unhand me, you insolent cur!” he spat, his voice trembling with rage.
“I will,” Sir Norman replied, his voice a low and dangerous murmur, “as soon as your highness understands that the queen has given me a direct command. And that command was to ensure the lady’s safety. I cannot do that if I allow her to be taken into a private room by a man she fears, for the discussion of secrets she does not possess.”
“The queen!” Caliban’s laugh was a short, sharp bark. “You hide behind her skirts already! A pretty protector you are, clinging to the whim that keeps you alive. A whim, I remind you, that can change with the rising of the sun. She will grow bored of you, spy. And when she does, I will be waiting. I will have the girl, and I will have your head, and I will take great pleasure in both.”
He wrenched his wrist free, not through strength but because Sir Norman chose to release it. He stood rubbing the joint, his eyes burning with a hatred that was almost pure enough to be admirable.
“You have won this little skirmish, Sir Norman Kingsley,” the dwarf said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper that carried more menace than any shout. “But the war is long, and you are but one man, and I have a thousand ways to cause you pain. Enjoy your temporary reprieve. It will make the end all the sweeter.”
He cast one last, lingering look at Eleanor, a look that was a vile promise of future torment, then turned on his heel and melted back into the shadows from whence he had come, leaving behind a palpable chill.

Eleanor was leaning against the cold stone of the window frame, her face ashen. “He will kill you,” she breathed. “As soon as he is able, he will see you dead.”
“He will certainly try,” Sir Norman agreed, a grim weariness settling over him. “Which is all the more reason for us to be cautious. Come, my lady. I will see you to your apartments. You must not be alone.”
He offered her his arm, and she took it, her small hand trembling on his sleeve. He led her through the now mostly deserted hall, past lingering knots of courtiers who stared at the unlikely pairing of the condemned traitor and the frightened young noblewoman. Their whispers followed him like the rustling of dry leaves, but he paid them no mind. His world had contracted to a single point: this duty, this moment. He was a piece on the queen’s board, as she had said, but a piece that could still move, could still act.

He delivered Eleanor into the care of an older, severe-looking lady-in-waiting, a woman whose face suggested she feared neither dwarfs nor princes, and saw the door to her apartments securely shut. Only then did he allow himself a moment to breathe. His new life, his un-life, had well and truly begun. He had fulfilled one command and, in doing so, made a powerful and implacable enemy. He had no doubt that word of his defiance would reach the queen’s ears before the hour was out. Whether it would amuse her, or sign his death warrant, was a question to which he had no answer.

He returned to the great hall, resuming his post near the main doors. The last of the revellers had departed; the servants moved about extinguishing the torches, plunging the vast space into a growing gloom that felt more honest than the frantic gaiety of the preceding hours. He was alone with the looming tapestries and the ghosts of the day’s justice. He stood at attention, his eyes scanning the darkness, no longer a simple soldier, but a hunter in a forest of shadows. He began to notice things he had not seen before: a guard in a far corner who was not a guard at all, but one of the duke’s men, his livery poorly disguised; a hushed exchange between two ladies of the court, a small, folded paper passing between them before they parted with feigned indifference.

He saw the duke himself emerge from a side passage, accompanied by the same portly lord who had spoken of Lady Castlemaine. The duke’s face was still flushed with the humiliation of the trial, and he spoke in a low, angry torrent, his hands gesturing emphatically.
“…an upstart, an insolent rebel! The queen indulges him for her own sport, but it is an affront to us all, to the dignity of this court!” the duke fumed, his voice echoing slightly in the empty hall.
“Indeed, your grace,” the portly lord soothed, his expression oleaginous. “An intolerable situation. If her majesty will not act, then perhaps others must, for the stability of the realm.”
The duke stopped, glancing around nervously, as if he suddenly felt eyes upon him. His gaze fell upon Sir Norman, standing silent and motionless in the deepening shadows. A flicker of alarm crossed the duke’s face, followed by a sneer of contempt.
“Look at him,” he muttered to his companion, loud enough for Sir Norman to hear. “The queen’s new watchdog. Let him stand there. A dog on a leash is still a dog.”
They swept past him and out of the hall, leaving Sir Norman to his solitary watch. He logged the conversation in his mind, filing it away. The duke’s resentment, the hint of conspiracy, the passing of the secret note. These were the things he was meant to see, the currency with which he would purchase another day of life. He was a spy. The dwarf’s accusation had become his reality.

The moon rose high outside the tall windows, its cold, white light spilling across the marble floor in sterile patches. The castle grew quiet, a slumbering beast full of venomous dreams. Sir Norman did not move. He felt no fatigue, only a strange, heightened awareness, the keen-edged sensibility of a man who knows each breath might be his last. He thought of the queen, in her silent chamber, moving pieces on her board. He thought of the dwarf, nursing his rage in the dark. He thought of Eleanor, sleeping fitfully behind a locked door. He was connected to them all, a linchpin in a machine he did not understand.
The queen had told him to amuse her. Tonight, he had defied a prince and protected an innocent, making an enemy of one and a dependant of the other. He wondered, with a sense of detached, almost academic curiosity, if that was amusing enough. The performance had begun; he could only pray he did not forget his lines.

The hours crept by on leaden feet, each one a small eternity in the echoing silence of the great hall. Sir Norman remained at his post, a statue carved from shadow, his senses stretched to a fine, vibrating wire. The moonlight shifted, drawing long, skeletal fingers across the polished floor, and the tapestries seemed to writhe with a life of their own, their woven figures enacting silent, grim histories. He had been given a part to play, but no script; a stage, but no certainty of who made up the audience. Was it for the queen alone he performed, or for the unseen eyes of the court, the vipers she had warned him of? He suspected it was for both, and for himself most of all; a desperate ad libitum to keep the headsman’s axe at bay.

It was in the deepest pit of the night, when the very air seemed thick and heavy with sleep, that a flicker of movement caught his eye. Not the bold stride of a guard or the furtive scuttling of a courtier, but something else; a lithe, silent shape that detached itself from the deeper gloom of a far archway. It was one of the gauzy nymphs from the trial, one of the queen’s handmaidens, her bare feet making no sound upon the stone. She drifted towards him, a phantom in the gloom, her face a pale oval in the darkness.

“Sir Norman Kingsley,” she whispered, her voice like the rustle of dry leaves. “Her majesty requires your presence.”

There was no question of refusal, no thought of delay. This was the summons he had been awaiting, the next scene in the bizarre and deadly play. He gave a single, sharp nod, his duty to the girl Eleanor a sudden, pressing concern. “And the young lady placed in my charge?” he asked, his own voice sounding harsh and low in the quiet.

“She is safe in the queen’s own wing,” the nymph replied, a flicker of what might have been surprise in her wide, dark eyes. “She will not be disturbed. You are to follow me. Now.”

He followed, his footsteps unnaturally loud behind her utter silence. They did not take the grand staircases or the main corridors, but plunged instead into a labyrinth of narrow, unlit passages, a secret circulatory system hidden within the castle’s bones. Here the air was stale and close, smelling of damp stone and forgotten years. The handmaiden navigated the twists and turns with an unerring certainty, as if she could see in the absolute dark. It was a journey designed, he realized, to disorient; to remind him that he was in a place of secrets, a place where the queen held all the maps.

At last, she stopped before a small, unremarkable door, a simple plank of dark wood set flush into the stone. She tapped upon it a sequence of soft, rhythmic knocks, and it swung inward without a sound. “Her majesty will see you,” she breathed, and melted back into the shadows from whence she came, leaving him alone on the threshold.

He stepped inside. The room was not the vast, imperial chamber he might have expected, but a smaller, more intimate space, though no less luxurious for its size. It was a study, or a library, the walls lined from floor to ceiling with books bound in leather and gold. A fire crackled in a great stone hearth, casting a warm, dancing light over thick velvet curtains and a desk of polished ebony. And there, seated behind the desk, was the queen.

She had shed the purple velvet and the artifice of the court. She wore a simple gown of black silk that made her skin seem impossibly white, like alabaster in the firelight. Her dark hair was unbound, cascading over her shoulders in a great wave. Without the crown and the courtly stiffness, she looked younger, and yet, paradoxically, more formidable. The coldness was still there, in the set of her mouth and the glacial clarity of her eyes, but now it seemed less a performance and more the core of her being. She was not playing the part of an iceberg; she was the iceberg.

“Come closer, Sir Norman,” she said, her voice a low murmur that nonetheless filled the room. “And shut the door. We would not wish to be disturbed.”

He did as he was told, the heavy door closing with a soft, final click. He walked across the plush Persian rug and stood before her desk, feeling strangely like a schoolboy called before the headmistress. She did not bid him sit. She merely watched him for a long moment, her gaze analytical, weighing and measuring him.

“You are an observant man, I think,” she began, her tone conversational, yet edged with steel. “You were told to watch. Tell me what you have seen.”

Here it was. The test. He met her gaze without flinching. “I have seen a duke whose pride is more sensitive than his honor,” he said, his voice steady. “I have seen him conspire with a portly lord, his words full of resentment for your majesty’s ‘indulgence’ and concern for the ‘stability of the realm’.”

A flicker of something—not surprise, but confirmation—passed through her eyes. “Indeed. Did you happen to hear the name of this portly lord?”

“I did not. But I would recognize his unctuous face were I to see it again.”

“You will,” she said, a simple statement of fact. “Continue.”

“I saw two ladies of the court exchange a folded paper in secret. I saw a guard in a corner who was no guard, but one of the duke’s men in poor disguise. I saw a court that smiles to your face, and sharpens its knives behind your back.”

“Aptly summarized,” she said, a ghost of a smile touching her lips, a chilling expression that held no warmth whatsoever. “You called my husband the ugliest man in the world.”

The abrupt change of topic was a new test, a feint. He did not rise to it. “I spoke the truth as I saw it, your majesty. A habit that has brought me little but trouble.”

“Truth is a luxury in a place like this, Sir Norman. A commodity to be bought and sold. You gave it away for free. A reckless expense.” She leaned forward slightly, the firelight catching in the dark depths of her eyes. “You also called me the most beautiful of women. Was that truth, or merely the desperate flattery of a condemned man?”

He considered the question. The air in the room was thick with unspoken things, with power and danger. “It was a statement of fact,” he said simply. “As plain as the other.”

She held his gaze for another long moment, and then she laughed. It was not the boisterous roar of the dwarf or the tittering of the court, but a sound as cold and clear as shattering ice. It stopped as suddenly as it had begun.

“You are amusing,” she declared, as if delivering a verdict. “For now. But amusement is a fleeting thing. I require utility. That girl, Eleanor. The dwarf wanted her for his sport. Why did you intervene?”

“He was tormenting a helpless creature, your majesty. I have a distaste for bullies, whatever their rank.”

“A distaste for bullies,” she repeated, her voice laced with a fine, sharp irony. “You stand in the court of Prince Caliban, whose every waking moment is a study in cruelty, and you have a ‘distaste for bullies’. You are either a fool, Sir Norman, or you possess a courage so profound it borders on madness.” She rose from her chair and walked to the fireplace, her back to him. “The dwarf is not merely my husband. He is a necessity. His family, his armies, his wealth… they are the pillars upon which my throne rests. A throne my own people would gladly see me fall from. They see a woman, and they see weakness. They see his ugliness and his power, and they see strength.”

She turned back to him, her face a mask of cold fury. “He is a monster, but he is my monster. And when you defy him, you challenge the very foundation of my power. You make me look weak. And I will not be made to look weak.”

She advanced on him, stopping so close he could feel the chill that seemed to radiate from her. “Do you understand? I saved you tonight not out of mercy, but because your defiance was, for a moment, useful. It put the duke in his place. It reminded my husband that even he is subject to my whims. But your life is balanced on a razor’s edge. One misstep, one moment where your usefulness wanes or your presence becomes an inconvenience, and that warrant I signed will be executed.”

“I understand perfectly, your majesty,” he said, his own voice low and intense.

“Good.” She seemed to relax, the tension flowing out of her as she returned to her desk. “Now, to the matter at hand. The duke’s conspiracy is more than just wounded pride. He speaks of ‘stability’ because he means to bring chaos. He believes I am not fit to rule. He and his allies would see another on the throne.”

“King Charles?” Sir Norman ventured, recalling the name from his farcical trial.

Her expression hardened. “Charles is a distant threat, a boogeyman they use to frighten children and rally support. The true threat is closer. It is within these walls. The note you saw passed between the ladies, did you see to whom it went?”

“No, your majesty. They separated quickly.”

“Find out,” she commanded. “Find out who the portly lord is. Find out which guards are loyal to the duke. Find out who in my court dreams of a new ruler. You are my eyes and ears now, Sir Norman. You are no longer a prisoner; you are a ghost. You will move through this court, and you will see everything. You will report it all to me, and to me alone. Your life depends on the value of your information.” She picked up a small, exquisitely crafted dagger from her desk, its blade like a sliver of moonlight, and used its point to indicate a tapestry on the wall. It depicted a grim hunting scene. “Behind this tapestry is the passage you entered by. You will use it, and only it, to come to me. You will come at this same hour, every night, whether you have something to report or not. Is that clear?”

“It is,” he said.

“One more thing,” she added, her eyes narrowing. “The girl, Eleanor. You have made her your responsibility. Therefore, she is now your weakness. The dwarf knows this. He will try to use her against you, to provoke you, to make you act the fool and force my hand. Protect her. If any harm comes to her, it will be a failure on your part. I do not tolerate failure.” She tossed the dagger, and it landed point-first in the ebony desk, quivering, a mere inch from a stack of papers. “You are dismissed.”

Without another word, Sir Norman turned, walked to the tapestry, and pushed it aside. The dark, narrow opening gaped at him like a silent mouth. He stepped into the oppressive blackness of the passage, the tapestry falling shut behind him, cutting off the warm firelight and leaving him once again in the cold and the dark. The interview had been everything he expected, and worse; he was a pawn, a tool, a temporary amusement whose life had the value of a single, interesting secret.

His first thought, as he navigated the suffocating corridor, was of Eleanor. The queen’s words had been a threat and a warning. She was his weakness. He had to see her, to assure himself of her safety. The handmaiden had said she was in the queen’s wing, but that was a vague and sprawling place. He emerged from the secret passage not far from where he had entered it, back in the cavernous main hall. The moon had moved on, and the hall was now plunged into a deeper gloom. He made his way, silent as a wraith, towards the section of the castle the nymph had indicated.

The corridors of the royal wing were more richly appointed, with carpets to muffle his footsteps and sconces that held softly glowing embers. Guards stood at intervals, but they were the queen’s personal guard, their livery different from the duke’s men or the general castle watch. They watched him pass with cold, incurious eyes; his presence, it seemed, was already expected. A word from

the queen was as good as a royal decree, and he passed unmolested down corridors that would have been his death-walk but an hour before.

He found the chamber without great difficulty; it was set apart from the main thoroughfare of the wing, a quiet anteroom leading to a more sumptuous suite, and two of the queen’s own guard stood sentry before it, their halberds crossed. They did not move to stop him, but merely uncrossed their weapons and stood aside, their faces as impassive as the stone gargoyles studding the castle walls. Sir Norman gave them a brief nod, which was not returned, and pushed open the heavy oaken door.

The room within was a world away from the cold grandeur and midnight justice of the court. A fire crackled merrily in a wide hearth, casting a warm, dancing glow over soft rugs and comfortable-looking furniture. And there, seated on a low stool by the fire, was Eleanor, her head bent over a piece of needlework, though her hands were still. Opposite her sat an older woman, with a kind but watchful face and hair the color of spun silver, who looked up sharply as he entered.

“Sir Norman!” Eleanor cried, dropping her needlework and springing to her feet. The relief in her voice was a sharp pang in his own chest, and she ran to him, her hands finding his arm as if to assure herself he was truly there, and not some fleeting phantom.

“Eleanor,” he said, his voice softer than he had intended. “You are safe, then.”

“They have been most kind,” she said, glancing back at the older woman. “This is Dame Agatha, her majesty’s first lady of the wardrobe. She has seen to everything.”

“His highness has come to see you are settled, my dear,” said Dame Agatha, her voice a calm and pleasant melody, yet with an undertone of authority that did not escape him. “You must forgive her agitation, Sir Norman; the night has been a trial for the poor child.”

A trial, indeed, he thought, with a grim inward smile. “Her agitation is most understandable,” he replied, his eyes meeting the older woman’s. “And I am grateful for the queen’s generosity, and for your care, Dame Agatha.” He was speaking to the lady, but the message was for the queen; a polite acknowledgment of the gilded cage.

Dame Agatha gave a slight, formal curtsy. “Her majesty is ever merciful to the innocent. I will leave you for a moment; I must see that the linens are properly aired.” It was a transparent excuse, and they all knew it; she was affording them a moment of privacy, but her very departure was a reminder that she, and by extension the queen, controlled even the air they breathed.

When the door had closed behind her, Eleanor’s composure crumbled. “Oh, Norman, I was so afraid! When they took you away, I thought… I thought they meant to kill you. The things they said, the way that dreadful little man looked at you!”

“Hush now,” he said, leading her back towards the fire. “It is over. You see me here, do you not? Alive and well.”

“But how? What happened after I was taken away? The queen… she looked so cold, so unforgiving. I feared she would sign your death warrant without a second thought.”

“And so she did,” Sir Norman said, with a carelessness he did not feel. “But her majesty is a creature of moods, it would seem. The execution was delayed, and in the interim, she discovered a new use for me.” He could not tell her the truth, not the whole of it; to do so would be to place her in even greater peril, to make her a conscious conspirator rather than an innocent bystander. “I am to be a sort of… special attendant in the court. A curiosity, you might say. My life is my own, for so long as I continue to amuse her.”

Eleanor looked at him, her brow furrowed with concern. “Amuse her? Norman, this is no game. This court… it is a terrible place. The ladies who brought me here, they whispered of things… of poisons, and disappearances, and rivals who are found floating in the moat. They say the queen and the dwarf are locked in a constant struggle for power, and that we are all but pawns in their cruel sport.”

“Then I shall be the most careful pawn on the board,” he said, forcing a smile. “And you, my dear Eleanor, must be careful as well. You are under the queen’s protection, which is the safest place to be in this nest of vipers; but it is a conditional safety. Be quiet, be observant, and trust no one but myself, and perhaps the good Dame Agatha, who seems to be the queen’s own shadow.”

He looked around the comfortable room, a stark contrast to the dungeon cell he had expected. “You have been given this chamber?”

“Yes. Dame Agatha says I am to serve as one of her majesty’s companions, to read to her, or sing, if she wishes it. She says the queen values novelty.”

Novelty. So that is what they both were; new toys for a bored and dangerous monarch. The queen’s warning echoed in his mind: *she is now your weakness*. He looked at Eleanor’s innocent, frightened face, and a cold resolve settled in his heart. He would not fail. He would become the ghost the queen desired, he would haunt these halls, and he would unravel the secrets of this wicked court, not just for his own survival, but for hers.

“Then be novel,” he said, taking her hands in his. “Be the picture of grace and gratitude. Your life, and mine, may depend upon the performance. Now, I must leave you. I have duties to attend to.”

“So soon?” she asked, her grip tightening.

“It is best. We must not appear too familiar, too attached. Here, every eye is a spy, and every word is weighed. I will see you when I am able, but you must be brave. Can you do that for me?”

She looked into his eyes for a long moment, and he saw the fear recede, replaced by a flicker of the same defiant spirit that he himself possessed. “I can,” she said, her voice firm. “I will.”

He gave her hands a final squeeze and turned to go, passing Dame Agatha in the hallway as she returned with a pile of folded silks. The older woman’s eyes met his, and in their placid depths he saw a universe of unspoken understanding. She knew his role, she knew the danger, and she would watch over the girl. For now, it was enough.

He stepped out of the royal wing and back into the more public sections of the castle, a man with a new and terrible purpose. He was no longer Sir Norman Kingsley, cavalier and prisoner; he was a shade, an instrument of the queen’s will, and his work was just beginning. The great hall was no longer empty; the courtiers, having finished their midnight repast, were beginning to congregate again, like moths drawn to a guttering and treacherous flame. They stood in small, whispering clutches, their faces masks of civility that barely concealed the fear and ambition beneath. Their laughter was too loud, their glances too sharp.

Sir Norman moved among them, a solitary figure in his travel-stained clothes, an object of intense curiosity. He had been the star of the night’s most dramatic entertainment, a man condemned to death, and yet here he was, walking free. Necks craned, whispers followed in his wake, and eyes, both friendly and hostile, tracked his every move. He ignored them all, his face a polite, unreadable mask. He was a ghost, and ghosts do not concern themselves with the whispers of the living.

He found a shadowed alcove near one of the great fireplaces, a vantage point from which he could observe the room without being its center. He saw the duke, his temporary judge, holding court in a corner, his face still flushed with indignation from Sir Norman’s earlier impertinence. A group of sycophantic nobles surrounded him, hanging on his every word, their laughter a fraction too eager. Sir Norman’s eyes narrowed. These were the men the queen wanted him to watch, the duke’s faction, the core of the rot in this corrupt court.

He let his gaze drift, cataloging faces, noting allegiances. He saw men whose fine clothes did not quite hide the desperation in their eyes, and women whose jewels seemed to burn with the fire of their ruthless ambition. He saw Lady Castlemaine, the woman whose trial for poisoning was to have followed his own, chatting with a group of ladies, her face a picture of unconcern, as if the prospect of an appointment with the headsman’s axe was no more troubling than a misplaced glove. Her trial, he recalled, had been postponed. By the queen’s whim, everything had been thrown into a new and uncertain alignment.

A pair of young noblemen, their tongues loosened by wine, detached themselves from the duke’s group and strolled near Sir Norman’s alcove, their voices low but not low enough.

“A devilish strange turn of events,” said the first, a tall fellow with a weak chin and an extravagant wig. “I had five hundred gold crowns riding on Kingsley losing his head before sunrise. The dwarf will be apoplectic.”

“The dwarf is always apoplectic,” replied his companion, a shorter, stouter man. “But he will be more so now. The queen has checkmated him again. She plucks this cavalier from the jaws of death simply to spite her husband. It is a bold move.”

“Or a foolish one. They say the man is a spy for King Charles.”

“He is whatever the queen says he is. For tonight, he is her pet. Tomorrow, he may be dog’s meat. But did you see the duke’s face? I thought he would burst a blood vessel when Kingsley called him a coward to his face! And the best part is, it was the truth!”

The two men shared a hushed, conspiratorial laugh. “Speaking of truths,” the first man murmured, leaning closer, “have you heard about Captain Mallory? Of the palace guard?”

“What of him?”

“He commands the western gatehouse. A dull post, but a vital one. It seems our noble duke has found Mallory’s loyalty to be… negotiable. A rather large sum of money has found its way into the captain’s coffers, and in return, he has found it in his heart to be profoundly deaf and blind on certain nights of the week.”

“Indeed! The duke moves to secure the keys to the castle, does he? He grows bolder.”

“He has to. The queen holds him on too tight a leash. He chafes, and a chafing prince is a dangerous prince. Mark my words, something is coming to a head soon. This cannot last.”

They moved on, their voices fading into the general din of the hall. Sir Norman remained motionless in the shadows, his heart beating a steady, deliberate rhythm. Captain Mallory. The western gatehouse. It was the first thread. A small thing, a piece of gossip, but it was exactly what the queen had sent him to find. He had a name, a location, and a motive. The duke was not merely posturing; he was making practical arrangements, securing the means for some future, treacherous action.

A soft, scraping sound behind him made him turn. Out of the very shadows he had thought his own, a figure emerged, and his hand instinctively went to the hilt of his sword.

“So jumpy, my little friend,” hissed a voice that was already seared into his memory. “One would think you had a guilty conscience.”

It was the dwarf. Prince Caliban stood there, not three feet away, his enormous head cocked to one side, a malevolent grin splitting his grotesque features. He had approached with an unnatural silence, his velvet-clad feet making no sound on the stone floor.

“Your highness,” Sir Norman said, his voice level. He forced himself to relax, letting his hand fall from his sword. “You have a habit of startling people. You should wear a bell.”

The dwarf’s grin widened, showing a formidable array of yellowed teeth. “And you have a habit of surviving, which I find intensely irritating. Her majesty has found a new plaything, I see. How does it feel, Sir Norman, to be the queen’s lapdog?”

“I could not say, your highness,” Sir Norman replied, his eyes cold. “But I imagine it is preferable to being her majesty’s trained ape.”

The grin vanished, replaced by a flash of pure, murderous rage. The dwarf’s face darkened to a mottled purple, and his hands clenched into tiny, knotted fists. “You will learn to regret that tongue of yours, Englishman. You have been saved by a woman’s whim, a fleeting caprice. But whims pass. And when her majesty grows tired of you, as she does with all her toys, I will be waiting. And I am not so capricious.”

“I shall look forward to it,” Sir Norman said with a placid smile. “It gives a man something to aim for.”

“Perhaps we need not wait so long,” the dwarf sneered, his eyes flicking pointedly across the hall, towards the corridors leading to the royal wing. “You have made friends quickly, I hear. A pretty little thing, the handmaiden. So innocent. So… vulnerable. Accidents happen in a place like this. A slip on the stairs, a sudden sickness. It would be a pity for such a fresh young flower to wither so soon.”

The queen’s prediction, made manifest. The threat was as clear and as deadly as a poised dagger. A hot, white anger surged through Sir Norman, and it took every ounce of his self-control not to seize the loathsome creature by the throat. But he saw the eager, watchful look in the dwarf’s eyes. This was the trap. He was being goaded, baited into an action that would prove the dwarf’s point, that would force the queen’s hand. He was the savage Englishman, the uncontrolled brute, and the dwarf was giving him the perfect opportunity to prove it.

He took a slow, deliberate breath, and when he spoke, his voice was unnaturally calm. “Her majesty has taken the girl into her own service. She is under the queen’s personal protection. I cannot imagine that even your highness would be so foolish as to risk her majesty’s displeasure over such a trivial matter.”

He had turned the threat back on its sender, invoking the one power in the court that the dwarf could not openly defy. He watched the frustration and fury war in Prince Caliban’s distorted features. The dwarf had expected an outburst, a passionate defense that he could twist into an act of insubordination. Instead, he had been met with cold logic and a reminder of his own subordinate position.

For a long moment, the dwarf simply stared at him, his chest heaving. Then, he let out a short, barking laugh that held no mirth. “Clever,” he hissed. “Very clever. You hide behind the queen’s skirts, you and your little milkmaid. But they are not as broad as you think. Enjoy your reprieve, Sir Spy. Haunt these halls. See what good it does you. You are one man, and I have a hundred eyes, a hundred ears. I will know your every move. And when you make a mistake, and you will make a mistake, I will be there to tear you down.”

With a final look of concentrated venom, the dwarf turned on his heel and stalked away, his misshapen form swallowed up by the crowd that parted before him as if from a leper. Sir Norman watched him go, his own body rigid with suppressed violence. He had won the exchange, but it had cost him. The mask of the detached, insouciant ghost was a heavy one to wear.

The night wore on, a fever dream of intrigue and veiled threats. He saw the duke slip a heavy purse to a man in the livery of the guard, a transaction so blatant it was an act of contemptuous pride. He heard two ladies-in-waiting dissecting the queen’s mood with the precision of surgeons, speculating on who was in favor and who was marked for a fall from grace. The entire court was a shimmering, beautiful, deadly web, and he was now caught in its center, bound to the queen who sat at its heart like a magnificent, patient spider.

At last, as the first grey fingers of dawn began to creep through the high arched windows, the weary court began to dissolve. Nobles and ladies retired to their chambers, their faces showing the strain of the long night’s performance. The great hall emptied, leaving behind only servants to sweep away the debris and guards to begin their morning patrols. Sir Norman, too, was shown to a chamber, a small but comfortable room not far from the one Eleanor occupied. It was another sign of his new, ambiguous status. He was a prisoner with privileges, a guest with a leash.

He did not sleep. He sat by the window, watching the sun rise over the strange, twisted landscape that surrounded the castle, his mind racing. He had survived the night. He had his first piece of intelligence. He had protected Eleanor, for now. But the dwarf’s words echoed in his ears. *I will be waiting*. This was only the beginning.

The day passed in a blur of tense observation. He was left to his own devices, a freedom that felt more like a test than a privilege. He explored the castle, mapping its corridors and courtyards in his mind, noting the guard rotations, the servant’s passages, the lines of sight. He was always watched, he knew; the eyes of the queen’s servants, and the dwarf’s, were ever upon him. He took his meals in the great hall, a solitary figure at a small table, eating the fine food that tasted like ash in his mouth. He saw Eleanor once, from a distance, walking in a walled garden with Dame Agatha and two other ladies. She looked pale but composed, and she did not look in his direction. She was learning the rules of the game.

When night fell again, it was with a sense of grim relief. The darkness was his element now. The pretense of the day could be shed, and the real work could begin. At the appointed hour, he made his way to the tapestry in the queen’s study. He did not need to be shown the way this time. He slipped through the hidden entrance, the familiar darkness and stale air closing in around him like a shroud.

He emerged into the fire-lit study to find the queen exactly as he had left her, seated at her ebony desk, a quill in her hand, the very picture of regal composure. She did not look up as he entered.

“You are punctual,” she said, her voice a low murmur. “That is a promising start. Report.”

There was no preamble, no greeting. He was her instrument, and she was ready to use him.

“Your majesty,” he began, his voice quiet in the still room. “Prince Caliban and the duke are not merely discontented. They are making preparations. The duke is buying the loyalty of key officers in the palace guard. I have the name of one: a Captain Mallory, who commands the western gatehouse. He has been paid to be selectively negligent.”

He expected some reaction, a flicker of surprise, a sharp question. But the queen merely continued to scratch at the vellum with her quill, her expression unchanging. For a moment, he wondered if she had even heard him.

Then, she laid the quill down with delicate precision. “Mallory,” she said, the name a soft, cold stone dropping into the silence. “Yes. I have had my eye on the good captain. His wife has a fondness for expensive jewels that his salary cannot support. Your information confirms my suspicions. That is something. What else?”

“I had an encounter with his highness,” Sir Norman said.

This time, she did look up. Her dark eyes, no longer veiled by their lashes, fixed on him with an unnerving intensity. “And?”

“He attempted to provoke me. He made threats against the girl, Eleanor.”

A flicker of something, not quite anger, more like cold irritation, passed over her face. “And did you allow yourself to be provoked? Did you strike my husband and force me to have you executed after all?”

“No, your majesty. I remembered your instructions.”

“You did,” she said, and her gaze was searching, analytical. “You are learning. You used my name, my authority, as a shield. That was wise. Caliban’s greatest frustration is that he is a prince only by marriage. His power is a reflection of mine, and he hates it. Reminding him of it is the surest way to enrage him, and also the surest way to check him. For now.”

She rose from her chair and walked to the fireplace, her velvet gown whispering over the stone floor. She stood for a moment, one hand resting on the marble mantelpiece, staring into the flames.

“This is not enough,” she said, her back still to him. “Confirming my suspicions about a greedy captain is useful, but it is not a solution. The duke and my husband grow more audacious. They believe I am merely a woman, that my throne is a pretty chair, and my scepter a bauble. They mistake my patience for weakness. I need to know their plan. Not the whispers and the rumors, but the heart of it. Who are they in league with outside this castle? What is their timetable? When do they mean to strike?”

She turned to face him, and in the flickering firelight, her face was a mask of cold, imperious beauty and absolute power. “You have been a ghost for one day, Sir Norman. You have brought me a cobweb. I require the spider. Find it for me. Find out who Captain Mallory meets on his ‘negligent’ nights. Find out what messages the duke is sending, and to whom. Infiltrate his circle. Make them trust you. You are a cavalier, a supposed enemy of this court; they might see you as a potential ally against me. Use that. Your life is no longer measured in hours, but in the value of the secrets you bring me.”

She gestated for a moment, her dark eyes seeming to pierce right through him. “You did well not to rise to Caliban’s bait. But know this; his threat against the girl was not idle. He will try again. He will look for your weakness, and he will press upon it until it breaks. Your control is your only armor. Do not let it fail you.”

She returned to her desk and picked up a small, sealed scroll. “This will grant you access to the records of the guard, in the castellan’s office. It will name you as my special inquisitor. Use it. Learn everything about Mallory, and about the men who serve with him. The duke is buying loyalty; I want to know who is for sale.”

She held it out to him. As he stepped forward to take it, his fingers brushed hers. Her skin was as cool as marble.

“Go now,” she said, her voice dropping again to that quiet, dismissive tone. “The sun will be rising soon. Ghosts do not walk in the daylight.”

He bowed, took the scroll, and retreated to the shadows of the tapestry. As he pushed it aside to enter the dark passage, he glanced back one last time. Queen Miranda had already picked up her quill again, her head bent over her work, as if he had already ceased to exist. He was dismissed, a tool put back in its box until it was needed again.

He plunged into the suffocating blackness of the corridor, the queen’s scroll a cold weight in his hand. He had another day, another reprieve. But the game had grown more dangerous, the stakes infinitely higher. He was no longer just a spy; he was now an inquisitor, an agent provocateur, sent to walk among wolves, and the only thing standing between him and the headsman’s axe was the whim of an icy queen and the strength of his own nerve. And somewhere in the castle, the dwarf was waiting, and watching, and plotting.
