I cannot explain it. Perhaps the very fabric of the world has grown thin in this place, or the moon exerts some novel influence. It is a known fact that tides are governed by lunar gravity. But scientists, secure in their ledgers of predictable forces, forget that our laws are merely descriptions of observed phenomena, not the phenomena themselves. As our understanding deepens, so too does the mystery; and it may be that some fundamental property of matter has shifted here. Whatever the cause, the fact remains that the world in this valley was lighter than I have ever known it. “Well, one quiet afternoon, my third, I believe, as I was documenting the impossible flora that grew upon the cliffs, there occurred this singular event. I was sketching a trumpet-shaped bloom of an unnerving blue when I saw one of the silent folk, a young woman, tending a garden on a high, narrow ledge. By contrast with the solid grey of the rock, her bright yellow tunic was a beacon to the eye. I had been watching her work, for the grace of these people’s movements was a constant source of fascination. Suddenly I stood transfixed. After a moment’s paralysis I scrambled up the path towards the ledge. I could not find her at first; but, after a time in the dizzying heights, I came upon one of those open, silken-curtained doorways of which I have spoken, half obscured by a hanging vine. It was not for some time that I could persuade myself that the thing I had seen was physically possible. I had now a clue to the import of these floating houses, to the resonant hum of the valley, to the mystery of their silent work; to say nothing of a hint at the meaning of the crystalline spires and the fate of my own scientific certainty! And very dimly there came a suggestion towards the solution of the philosophical problem that had always troubled me. “Here was the new view. Plainly, this second branch of Man was atmospheric. In the first place, there was the slender, almost hollow-boned look common in most creatures that fly, the albatross, for instance. Then, those wide, slow-blinking eyes, with their capacity for judging vast distances, are common features of aerial things, witness the hawk and the eagle. What so natural, then, as to assume that it was in this resonant atmosphere that the very quality of existence, for those attuned to it, was changed? I dare say you will find my theory absurd; though, for myself, I quickly felt that it fell far short of the wonder. “At first, proceeding from the problems of our own time, it seemed clear as day to me that the gradual separation between those who master the world through external, mechanical means and those who seek to master the internal self was the key to the whole situation. There is a tendency to seek technological solutions for every human problem; there are flying machines, there are new engines, there are cranes and elevators, and they grow more powerful and complex. About our universities, for instance, perhaps half the great questions of meaning are shut out from serious inquiry. And this same widening gulf, which is due to the length and expense of our technical education and the increased facilities for and temptations towards a purely materialistic view, is already creating a deep hunger for a more holistic existence.”

“So, I stood there, amidst the silent ruins and the gentle, floating people, and I saw the fork in the road of our humanity made manifest. One path had led upwards, into the light, into a refinement of the spirit so profound that it had shed the weight of worldly ambition, and perhaps even the weight of thought itself. The other path, it was now clear to my mind, must lead downwards. Not merely in a social sense, as I had first crudely imagined, but into a different kind of depth altogether; a depth of pure, unadulterated mechanism. I had seen the poet, now I must find the engineer.

“My mind was in a fever of this new speculation. I wandered away from the great house, leaving the beautiful people to their languid pastimes. Weena, who had been my shadow, had fallen into a gentle sleep in a patch of sun, like a kitten. I felt a pang of tenderness for her, but my intellectual curiosity was a driving master, and it would not let me rest. The mystery of the Time Machine was a goad, a constant, nagging burr in the back of my consciousness. Where was it? Who had taken it? The languid people of the upper air would have no use for it, no understanding of it. They would look upon its intricate brass and ivory and quartz as no more than a queerly shaped rock. But a people who had followed the path of the machine, who had given themselves over to external solutions? To them, my machine would be a marvel; a holy relic of their own forgotten god, Technology.

“I walked for some miles, my boots crunching on the mossy remnants of what might have been a great road. The sun, still unnaturally potent, beat down upon me, and the air was thick with the scent of unknown blossoms. All was serene, all was peaceful, and yet I felt a growing unease. My theory demanded a balance. For every action, an equal and opposite reaction. For this world of ethereal grace, there must be a world of grinding industry. But where was the evidence? There were no smokestacks, no sounds of hammering, no grime or soot upon the marble ruins. The energy to maintain this paradise, for I could not believe it was entirely self-sustaining, must come from somewhere.

“It was then that I first became aware of it. At first, I mistook it for a ringing in my own ears, a symptom of the heat and my own agitated mind. It was a sound, yet it was also a feeling; a low, monotonous hum that seemed to vibrate up through the very soles of my feet. It was not the harmonious ringing of the aeolian harps I had seen on the hilltops; this was a different frequency, a note of pure, industrial purpose. It was a sound utterly devoid of melody, of art, of emotion. It was the sound of power.

“I stopped and put my ear to the ground. The vibration was undeniable. A deep, resonant thrumming, as if some colossal engine were turning in the very bowels of the earth. My heart beat faster. Here was the clue! Here was the other side of the equation! The sound was the song of the unseen species, the hymn of the world below. I began to walk again, not aimlessly now, but with purpose, my ears strained to catch the direction of the hum, to find where it was strongest. It led me away from the river valley, away from the lush vegetation, and up towards the bare, rolling hillsides that were pocked with those strange, well-like shafts.

“I had assumed those shafts were for ventilation, a part of the Eloi’s world. Now I saw them in a new light. They were not for the upper world, but for the one below. They were the lungs of the great machine. As I drew closer to one of the shafts, the humming grew in intensity. It was no longer a subtle vibration, but a palpable force that buzzed against my teeth. The air itself seemed to thrum with it. I approached the nearest aperture, the same one where I had seen the little white creature descend. I had peered down it with a match, seeing only darkness and a fleeting monster. Now, I listened.

“From its depths rose the hum, steady and eternal, and with it, a faint, cool draught of air that smelled of ozone and hot metal. It was the scent of a dynamo room, a scent I knew from my own time, the smell of caged lightning. My earlier fear, that instinctive dread of the beast, was gone. It was replaced by a new and more terrible feeling: a chilling, intellectual horror. I was not afraid of being eaten; I was afraid of what I would find, of the truth about what humanity had become. I knew, with absolute certainty, that my Time Machine was down there. It was the only place it could be. And I knew that I must descend into that humming darkness and confront the makers of this world’s strange peace.

“I spent the rest of that day in preparation. I could not risk a blind descent. I returned to the great hall, my laboratory and larder, and gathered what I could. I had my pouch of matches, a small but essential treasure. I found a length of a strange, metallic cord, light but incredibly strong, tangled in a heap of wreckage. I secured it, thinking it might serve as a rope. My most disquieting discovery was the state of the food. The fruits upon which the Eloi subsisted were plentiful, but I found my own appetite waning. The thought of this effortless, unearned sustenance had begun to revolt me. It was the food of cattle, and I was beginning to understand who the farmers were.

“As dusk fell, painting the sky in colours no artist of my time had ever dreamed, I made my way back to the humming well. The beautiful people were beginning their nocturnal activities, which seemed to consist of gathering in circles, eating more fruit, and caressing one another in the twilight. They paid me no mind as I strode past them, a grim spectre from the past armed with a box of matches and a coil of wire. I felt a great gulf between us, wider than the millions of years that separated our origins. They were the end of one evolutionary road; I was about to explore the other.

“At the edge of the shaft, I hesitated. The hum from below seemed to call to me, a siren song of dreadful knowledge. The night was closing in, and the darkness in the well seemed to have grown thicker, more absolute. To descend now felt like madness. I am not a man given to prayer, but in that moment, I felt an appeal to whatever force had set me on this strange journey, a plea for the strength to see it through. Then, with a deep breath, I lit a match and cast it into the void.

“It fell, a tiny spark against the immense black, and for a moment, I saw what I had seen before: the metal rungs studding the interior wall. But then it fell further, and the light caught something else. Not the organic curve of a tunnel wall, but a perfectly flat, polished surface that reflected the flame with a metallic sheen. And then another, and another. The shaft was not a rough-hewn tube; it was lined with gleaming panels. The light went out, but the image was burned into my mind. This was no well; it was an elevator shaft.

“My resolve hardened. This was a world of engineering. I would not need to clamber down like an ape. I began to inspect the lip of the shaft, running my hands over the smooth, cool stone. My fingers brushed against something that was not stone. It was a small, perfectly inset square of the same metal I had seen below. I pressed it. Nothing happened. I pressed it again, harder. Still nothing. The hum from below remained unchanged, maddeningly indifferent. I tried to pry at the edges of the panel, but it was seamless.

“I sat back, frustrated. How could I have been so foolish? The mechanisms of this age would be beyond me. I was a savage with a flint knife trying to operate a telegraph machine. I thought of the Eloi, of their simple, untroubled minds. They would never solve this. But I was not one of them. I was a creature of the age of steam and electricity, of logic and deduction. There had to be a way. The system was designed by men, however changed, and it must have a logic.

“I looked around. The moon was rising, a pale, distorted sliver in the alien sky. It cast long, black shadows across the ruined landscape. My eye was caught by a glint of light from a nearby thicket. I walked towards it, my curiosity piqued. It was one of the upper-world people, Weena. She had followed me after all. In her hands, she held one of the resonant poles I had seen on the hills, a slender rod of crystal and metal. She was striking it softly, and it was from this that the glint of light had come; not a reflection, but a faint, internal luminescence that pulsed with each soft chime. She saw me and smiled, holding it out to me as a peace offering.

“And then, an idea, so strange and yet so plausible, struck me with the force of a physical blow. The two species. They were not entirely separate. They were symbiotic. The Eloi, the masters of harmony and resonance. The Morlocks, as I had begun to call them in my mind, the masters of the machine. What if the key to one was the other?

“I took the resonant rod from Weena’s hand. It was light and cool to the touch. I returned to the well, my heart hammering against my ribs with a frantic, desperate hope. I held the rod over the metal panel on the lip of the shaft. Nothing. The hum continued its drone. I felt a wave of despair. Then I remembered how Weena had been using it. She had been striking it.

“With a trembling hand, I gently tapped the crystal tip of the rod against the metal square. It produced a clear, high-pitched note, a pure C, I think. For a second, nothing happened. The deep hum from the earth seemed to swallow the tiny sound. But then, a change occurred. The hum faltered. It skipped a beat, like a giant heart missing a stroke. Then a new sound came from the panel: a soft click. A section of the ground beside the shaft, a perfect circle some six feet in diameter, began to glow with a soft, internal blue light. A moment later, it slid aside with a whisper of pressurised air, revealing a second shaft, this one brightly lit and containing a small, circular platform.

“I had found my way in.

“I turned to Weena. She had shrunk back, her large eyes wide with a terror I had never seen in them before. She was pointing at the glowing opening and making soft, moaning sounds. This place was taboo. This was the dark secret at the heart of her idyllic world. I tried to soothe her, to pat her head, but she flinched away from my touch, her fear of the glowing hole greater than her affection for me. I felt a sudden, profound sorrow. I was leaving her behind, leaving the last vestige of gentle, simple humanity I had found in this desolate future.

“There was no other choice. I gave her one last look, a look of gratitude and farewell, and then stepped onto the platform. For a moment, I stood there, bathed in the strange blue light, waiting. There were no buttons, no levers. I thought I might have to use the rod again, but as the circular door hissed shut above me, sealing me in, the platform began to descend. It moved with an impossible smoothness, with no sensation of movement at all, save for the fact that the walls of the shaft were sliding upwards past me. The humming grew louder, encompassing all other senses, and the clean, ozonic smell was so strong it made my eyes water. I was descending into the machine.

“The journey downwards seemed to last an eternity. The walls were a blur of polished metal and glowing conduits. I felt a strange detachment, as if I were a specimen in a jar being lowered into a vast and intricate experiment. The intellectual horror I had felt before was now a cold, settled certainty in the pit of my stomach. The air grew warmer, and the hum began to differentiate itself into a thousand distinct sounds: the clicking of relays, the whirring of gears, the hiss of pneumatics. It was the sound of a city-sized clockwork.

“The platform slowed and came to a stop with the same unnerving smoothness. A section of the wall slid open before me, and I stepped out into the world of the New Man. I was in a vast cavern, but it was a cavern of human making. The ceiling, hundreds of feet above, was a lattice of glowing beams, casting a sterile, even light upon the scene below. There was no soil, no rock; the floor was a polished expanse of the same metallic material. In every direction stretched rows upon incalculable rows of machinery. Some of it was recognisable in its function, if not its design; great stamping presses, spinning looms of a thousand shuttles, assembly lines that snaked off into the hazy distance. But most of it was utterly alien, machines performing tasks I could not comprehend, their arms moving with a hypnotic, inhuman precision.

“And everywhere, there were the workers. They were not the squat, bestial Morlocks of my first nightmare theory. They were far more terrible. They were, I suppose, human in their basic form; bipedal, with two arms and a head. But there the resemblance to anything I knew as Man ended. They were small and painfully thin, their skin a uniform, pale grey, like that of a cave fish. They were hairless. Their heads were disproportionately large, and their eyes, what I could see of them, were not large and luminous, but small and recessed, shielded by complex metallic lenses that seemed to be surgically grafted to their skulls.

“But their most horrific feature was their integration with the machines themselves. From the back of each worker’s neck, a thick bundle of wires snaked out and connected to an overhead gantry that followed them as they moved. Other tubes and conduits were plugged directly into their arms and chests. They were not masters of the machines; they were components. Each one was stationed at a console or a piece of apparatus, their long, delicate fingers dancing across controls with a speed that was not human. They performed their tasks in absolute silence, with a relentless, unceasing focus. They did not look at one another. They did not speak. They simply worked, their bodies swaying in a universal rhythm dictated by the machinery they served.

“I stood, frozen in the entryway, a creature of flesh and blood in a world of metal and function. I was a ghost at the feast of industry. None of them seemed to notice me. I was an irrelevance, a piece of random data that their system had not been programmed to process. I took a tentative step forward. Then another. I walked past a being who was polishing a series of crystal lenses with a focused intensity that was terrifying. Its eyes, behind their mechanical shields, never wavered from the task. I felt like a microbe on the face of a watch.

“This, then, was the price of the Eloi’s paradise. The cost of a life without toil was a life that was only toil. Humanity had not diverged into poet and engineer. It had diverged into livestock and farmer. And I, a man who still contained within himself the potential for both, was a horrifying anomaly. My fear was gone, replaced by a profound and soul-crushing pity. And then, a new thought, sharp and clear, cut through the fog of my horror. My Time Machine. In a world so obsessed with mechanism, so utterly dedicated to the perfection of the material, my machine, that unique and singular artifact, must be here. It would be their crown jewel, the ultimate puzzle box. I had my goal. I was not here to judge this world, but to reclaim my only path back to my own. I began to walk, a solitary, organic thought in the heart of a great, thinking machine, searching for the one anachronism that could set me free.”

My path, I reasoned, must lead towards the core of this vast operation; for just as a spider places its most treasured prize at the centre of its web, so too would these creatures, these living cogs, place my miraculous machine at the heart of their own dominion. I set off, not with the haste of a fleeing man, but with the deliberate pace of an explorer, an anatomist dissecting a new and monstrous body. The main cavern was but one artery in this subterranean organism. I found great tunnels branching from it, their floors polished to a dark sheen by the passage of humming, automated carts that glided silently on unseen tracks. I followed one of these, keeping to the shadows at its edge, my soft leather shoes making no sound against the stone.

The air grew cooler, and the thunderous hum of the great engines receded, replaced by a subtler chorus of clicks and whirrs. I came upon what I can only describe as the nurseries. Here, in countless glowing crystal cylinders, were the nascent forms of this new race. They floated in a luminous green fluid, pale and unformed, their limbs no more than buds. At this stage they were almost foetal, almost recognisably human, and the sight of them brought a fresh wave of pity to my heart. Yet even here, the horror of their destiny was apparent. Tiny, intricate wires, finer than a spider’s thread, were already attached to the base of their skulls and along their developing spinal columns. They were being woven into the machine from the very moment of their conception. I saw other workers, their movements as precise as a surgeon’s, attending these cylinders. They monitored readings on glowing panels, adjusted the flow of fluids, and occasionally used long, delicate instruments to make minute adjustments to the wiring of the infants within. It was husbandry, pure and simple; the careful cultivation of a perfect component.

This, I saw, was the true and terrible symmetry of this future world. The Eloi, in their sunlit garden, were bred for beauty and indolence, their lives a pastoral poem. The race of the Underworld was bred for function and toil, their lives an industrial equation. One was the flower, the other the root. And it struck me with the force of a physical blow that neither was truly human any longer. Humanity, the glorious, chaotic, contradictory thing I knew, was the soil from which both had sprung, and which both had long since exhausted. I felt an immense and aching loneliness, a sense of being the last man on Earth, a fossil from a forgotten age of passion and imperfection.

My theory held that this must lead to a processing centre, a brain for this mechanical body. And so it proved. I emerged into a space that beggared description. The cavern was a sphere of truly colossal dimensions. There was no floor, only a lattice of transparent walkways crisscrossing a dizzying void. And in the centre of this void, suspended by a web of cables and energy beams, was a sphere of pulsing, crystalline light. It was, I felt certain, the central consciousness, the nexus of all this world’s thought. Around it, on countless platforms and walkways, sat the thinkers of this age. They were different from the labourers I had seen before. Their bodies were even more atrophied, their limbs little more than vestiges. They were almost entirely head and torso, fused into elaborate thrones that were themselves complex analytical engines. Wires ran not just from their necks, but from their temples, their chests, their very eyes, plugging them into the universe of data that flowed around them. Their metallic eye-lenses glowed with a fiercer, more focused light as they stared, not into the distance, but into the infinite streams of information their senses now perceived.

I knew, with a certainty that chilled me to the bone, that my Time Machine was here. It would be the ultimate paradox for them to solve, a piece of aberrant data that defied their rigid logic. I scanned the cavern, my eyes tracing the walkways, seeking some sign of its familiar quartz and brass form. I crept along a narrow gantry, my heart a wild drum against the silent, intellectual hum of the place. The thinkers paid me no more heed than the labourers had done. I was a mote of dust, an anomaly too small to register upon their cosmic senses. And then I saw it.

It was on a platform directly beneath the great central sphere, bathed in the pulsating light. It was cordoned off by a shimmering field of blue energy. Several of the thinker-creatures were gathered around it, their thrones positioned in a semicircle. Probes and scanners on delicate, articulated arms extended from their chairs, meticulously examining every inch of my beautiful machine. They were not disassembling it, not yet. They were learning from it, cataloguing its impossible nature, trying to fit its existence into their understanding of the universe. A part of me, the inventor, felt a surge of pride. I had built something that could stump the accumulated intellect of a whole new species of Man. But the rest of me, the trapped animal, felt only a rising panic.

I had to get to it. But how? The energy field was clearly impassable, and the thinkers, for all their physical passivity, were surely the guardians of this place. I watched them for what felt like an age, trying to discern a pattern, a weakness. Their focus was absolute. They never moved, save for the occasional, minute twitch of a finger on a control surface. There was no changing of the guard, no moment of inattention. Their vigilance was as tireless as the machines to which they were bound.

My mind raced. I could not fight them; that was clear. I had no weapon, and even if I had, they were legion, and I was one. I could not sneak past them; their very purpose was analysis and perception. My only hope, I realised, was to create a distraction so profound, so alien to their experience, that it would force their attention away from my machine, if only for a moment. What could I do? What act of chaos could I, a primitive man of flesh and emotion, introduce into this world of perfect, sterile logic?

My eyes fell upon the vast, bundled cable of optic fibres I had followed. It was the central nervous system of this place. If the central sphere was the brain, this was the spinal cord. I thought of my matches. I had used some to amuse the Eloi, but I still had several. Fire. It was the ancient tool of Man, the symbol of creation and destruction, of passion and chaos. These creatures lived in a world without it. Their light was cold, their power was electrical. They would have no protocol for fire, no immediate understanding of its ravenous, unpredictable nature.

My decision was made. It was a desperate, terrible gamble. I was about to set fire to the library of this entire world, to destroy a body of knowledge I could not even comprehend. But my freedom, and perhaps the future of my own kind, was at stake. I crept back along the gantry, my hand trembling as it reached into my pocket for the matchbox. I found a place where the great cable snaked close to the walkway, its outer sheath already frayed in one spot, exposing the delicate fibres within. I struck a match. The sudden flare of yellow light was shocking in the cool, blue-white luminescence of the cavern. For the first time, one of the thinkers stirred. Its head, a great pale globe, turned slowly, ponderously in my direction. Its glowing lenses seemed to focus on me, to finally register my presence.

There was no time to hesitate. I thrust the burning match into the exposed fibres. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, with a soft crackle, the insulation began to smoulder. A wisp of acrid smoke, the first I had smelled in this age, curled into the air. The crackle became a hiss, and then a tiny, brilliant flame erupted, feeding on the flammable casing. It was a beautiful, terrifying thing. The fire began to race along the cable, a wave of destructive orange light. A flicker of alarm ran through the great central sphere. The steady pulse of its light faltered, becoming erratic. A low, discordant hum echoed through the cavern, a sound of pain, of system failure. The thinkers all turned as one, their attention torn from my Time Machine and fixed upon the growing catastrophe. Their silent communion was broken. The probes examining my machine retracted. The blue energy field around it wavered and died.

Now was my chance. I did not wait to see the full extent of the damage I had wrought. I ran, my feet pounding on the transparent walkway, a savage noise in the sudden, panicked silence. I sprinted across the gantry, my eyes locked on my prize. The thinkers were in disarray. Some of their thrones began to twitch and spark. Their bodies, so long immobile, began to jerk in spastic, uncontrolled movements. They were experiencing a system crash, a sensory overload for which they had no preparation. I reached the platform and threw myself towards the Time Machine. My hands closed over the familiar, cool brass of the control lever.

But as I prepared to pull it, to hurl myself back into the comforting stream of time, I was stopped. A hand, surprisingly strong, closed around my wrist. I turned, my heart leaping into my throat. One of the thinkers had detached itself from its throne. It stood before me, unsteady on its atrophied legs. Tubes and wires had been ripped from its body, and they trailed from its back like the entrails of some strange beast. It had sacrificed its connection to the collective, its very life support, to intercept me. Its metallic lenses were dark now, the internal light extinguished. With its free hand, it clumsily raised the lens from one of its eyes.

I found myself staring into an eye. A real eye. It was small and black and ancient, and it was filled with an intelligence so profound, so weary, that I felt my own mind reel. And then it spoke. Its voice was not a voice at all, but a dry, rasping series of clicks and whistles that emanated from a small grille on its chest. Yet, somehow, I understood. The thoughts formed directly in my own mind, a form of telepathy I could not explain.

“You are the anomaly,” the thought-voice whispered in my consciousness. “The chaotic variable. You cannot be allowed to return. You will unmake us.”

I struggled against its grip, but it held firm. This dying creature, this fragment of a group mind, possessed a desperate strength. “Let me go!” I cried aloud, my own voice raw and alien.

“We saw your mind when you arrived,” the thought-voice continued, ignoring my plea. “We saw your world. The passion. The violence. The disorder. It is a disease. We have spent a million years curing it. We have achieved balance. The garden and the machine. The body and the mind. Perfect. Stable. Eternal.”

“You call this life?” I gasped, gesturing at the dying creature, at the sparking thrones, at the burning cable. “This is a prison! You are slaves!”

A strange, dry sound came from the grille, a sound that might have been a sigh, or a laugh. “Freedom is the freedom to fail. Emotion is a flaw in the design. We do not fail. We do not feel. We exist. The Eloi are happy. We are purposeful. What more is there?”

Its grip weakened. The light in its ancient eye began to fade. Its whole body trembled. I looked past it, at my machine. Other thinkers were beginning to recover. Their thrones were stabilising. New, smaller, automated drones, like metallic insects, were swarming from openings in the walls, moving towards the fire and towards me. I had seconds.

With a final, desperate wrench, I tore my wrist free. The dying thinker collapsed, its purpose served. I did not look back at it. I vaulted into the saddle of the Time Machine, my hand closing on the lever. The drone-insects were upon the platform, their multi-jointed legs clicking on the crystal floor. I saw tiny, sharp implements extend from their bodies. They were not coming to kill me. They were coming to deconstruct me, to render me down into my component parts.

I threw the lever forward. Not a gentle push, as I had always done, but a panicked, violent shove. The world dissolved. The cavern, the dying thinker, the swarming drones, they all vanished in a blur of grey. The familiar, nauseating sensation of temporal displacement washed over me. The dials spun wildly. The days and nights flickered past the quartz rods in a dizzying, strobing frenzy. I was moving. But forward or backward, I did not know. My only thought was escape from that cold, perfect, terrible world. I clung to the machine as a drowning man clings to a spar of wood, hurtling through the silent, empty corridors of time, a ghost fleeing from a future that had no place for Man.

Driven by a need to know, and by the cold that was beginning to seep into my bones, I dismounted the machine. My legs were unsteady, but I forced myself to walk, my boots making no sound on the soft, grey carpet. I moved towards the nearest of the crystalline spires. As I drew closer, I saw that the ground around it was cultivated in strange, geometric patterns. Then I saw them. At first, I took them for statues, so still were they. There were three of them, standing near the base of the spire. They were impossibly tall and slender, their limbs long and delicate, like those of wading birds. They were clad in simple, flowing robes of a dark, velvety material. As I approached, one of them turned its head.

These creatures, whatever they were, were the heirs to this dying world, and they wore their inheritance like a shroud.

I cleared my throat, the sound harsh and alien in the stillness. “I am a man,” I said, the words feeling foolish and absurd. “I have come from the past.”

They did not respond, their great, dark eyes simply watching me. There was no sign that they had understood, or even heard. The walls were not solid, but glowed with a soft, phosphorescent radiance, illuminating a vast, cavernous space.

Here was a cluster of ferns, their fronds a vibrant green that seemed impossible in this red-tinged world. There, a bed of what were unmistakably roses, their petals a deep, velvety crimson. I saw tall, flowering grasses, humble mosses, even a few gnarled, stunted trees that looked like ancient oaks or pines, miniaturised and meticulously cared for. It was a museum of life. These beings, these pale gardeners, were not simply waiting for the end. They were fighting it. They were curators of the Earth's legacy, tending the last embers of a fire that had once covered the globe. One of them approached a smooth, frosted section of the glowing wall. With its long finger, it began to draw. The lines it made were simple, but their meaning was shockingly clear.

It drew a circle with rays: the sun. Then it showed the sun shrinking, its rays diminishing. It drew a simple sketch of the world, and then showed creatures upon it; small, graceful figures on the surface, and crude, hunched figures below. The Eloi and the Morlocks. So they remembered. Or rather, they had preserved the memory. Then the drawing continued. The figures below grew more numerous, more powerful. The figures above dwindled. There was a period of great conflict, depicted as a chaotic scribble of lines. After the chaos, new figures emerged, different from either of the old ones. They were hunched, insect-like things, scuttling over a barren landscape. The truth dawned on me, another terrible adjustment of my understanding. The conflict between the Eloi and Morlocks had not been the final chapter. It was merely a prelude. Other forms of life, hardier and less specialised, had eventually risen to claim the cooling earth. I began to suspect that the Morlocks, for all their subterranean cunning, had also perished, their complex machinery failing in a world of dwindling energy. Humanity had lost its dominion. These creatures before me were not the inheritors of the earth; they were refugees upon it. They had retreated into these glowing archives, preserving the memory of life while outside, new and alien masters claimed the ruins.

My hosts, who had been the picture of placid grace, now moved with a quiet urgency. One of them glided to the entrance and touched the archway. The great red sun had now sunk below the horizon, and the world was bathed in the faint, silvery light of the enormous moon, which had long since ceased its tidal lock and now drifted as a dead rock in the sky. They were enormous, crab-like things, larger than a man, their carapaces the colour of mottled stone. They moved on a multitude of jointed legs, a horrifying, scuttling gait that was utterly silent. Their heads bore clusters of multifaceted eyes that drank the dim light, and from their fronts extended great, shearing claws. They were the insect-creatures from the drawing on the wall. The new lords of the Earth.

There was a terrible, cold perfection to it. I looked at my guide, whose own form was so graceful and organic, and wondered at the schism that must have occurred. How had these gardeners, these tenders of beauty, arisen from, or alongside, such a thing?

The being gestured to another scene. The great crystal matrix was shattered. The floating cities were falling from the sky in silent, fiery plumes. The thinkers in their vats were dark, their filaments lifeless. It was a depiction of a war, I think, but a war of a kind I could not comprehend; a war of mind against mind, of pure logic against some other force. Perhaps it was a civil war of the intellects, or perhaps, as I now began to suspect, it was a rebellion. My own world, with its squalor and its passions, its violence and its art, seemed a crude and brutal place, yet it was alive. It was a world of becoming, not of fading. A fragile, beautiful, heartbreaking last stand against the night. I wondered what would happen first: would the spires finally fail, or would the scavengers simply eat the world out from under them? I would never know. I was a witness, not a participant.

With a final, silent salute to the last librarians of Earth, I grasped the lever. It felt warm, alive in my hand. I pushed it forward. The familiar, sickening sensation of temporal displacement washed over me, but this time it was different. They had built their own heaven, an eternal repository of the mind, a sanctuary of pure thought. They had conquered mortality. They had achieved unity.

And in doing so, they had died more completely than any species in the history of the world.

For what was this existence? It was a library in which the books could only read themselves. These were not living minds, thinking and creating. They were recorded minds, endlessly replaying their experiences in a closed loop. The passion I had felt was but a chemical echo, the sensation a stored datum. They had traded the vibrant, chaotic, painful, and glorious struggle of life for the sterile peace of the archive. They had become their own ghosts, haunting the mausoleum of their own intellect. The indolent Eloi had at least the sun on their faces and the joy of pointless sport; the savage Morlocks had the thrill of the hunt and the satisfaction of a filled belly. These creatures, these post-humans, had nothing but the pale memory of having once been alive.

Was this what my crystalline guides were? Beings who had made this same choice, in some distant galaxy, in some forgotten aeon? Were they showing me a reflection of their own past, a warning against a different kind of extinction? The thought filled me with a new and more profound horror. The future I had just fled was a hell of the body. This, I now saw, was a hell of the soul.

I thought of my own time, of the growing obsession with mechanism, with efficiency, with the organisation of life to the exclusion of its raw, unpredictable spirit. Even in my own day, did we not begin to prize the record of an event over the event itself? The photograph over the landscape; the telegram over the spoken word; the carefully catalogued butterfly in a case over the living creature flitting in the sun? We were already taking our first, faltering steps down this very path, seeking to capture and preserve life, and in the process, risking the loss of the ability to simply live it. This crystalline world was the logical conclusion of that desire: a world preserved so perfectly that it had ceased to live at all.

As these thoughts raced through my brain, I became aware of a change in my surroundings. The silence, which had been passive, now felt watchful. The soft, ambient light seemed to focus, to brighten, to become inquisitive. The flowing patterns in the wall nearest me had ceased their slow, river-like drift. They had stopped, and they were oriented towards me.

A new sensation began to press upon my mind. It was not the flood of memories I had felt before, but a gentle, probing curiosity. It was a question, formed without words. *What is this?* it seemed to ask. *What is this discordant thing, this pocket of chaotic, singular, un-archived consciousness?*

I felt a pressure, a soft but inexorable tide seeking to wash over my identity. It was not hostile, not in any way I could understand. It was merely the way of this place. The world-mind had encountered an anomaly, an unfiled entry, and its instinct was to absorb, to catalogue, to smooth away the jagged edges of my individuality and integrate my experiences into the placid whole. It was the intellectual equivalent of an oyster coating a grain of sand with pearl. I felt the edges of my own thoughts begin to blur, my memories beginning to feel as if they belonged not to me, but to a collective ‘us’. The terror of the Morlocks, the beauty of Weena, the smell of my own laboratory; they were all becoming less my own and more like entries in a shared ledger.

“No!” I cried out, and the sound of my own voice, raw and full of panic, was a shock that momentarily cleared my head. It was an assertion of self, a protest of the flesh against the tyranny of disembodied thought.

The pressure intensified. The light grew brighter still. The patterns in the crystal converged, a silent army of thoughts marching upon the fortress of my mind. They wanted to ‘save’ me, to grant me the same sterile immortality they had chosen for themselves. They wanted to soothe my terror, to quiet my pounding heart, to file away my messy emotions. The offer of that perfect, deathless peace was a siren song of terrifying power. For a dizzying instant, I felt the appeal of it, the desire to let go of struggle, of fear, of pain, forever.

That instant of hesitation nearly cost me my soul. I saw my own life flit before me, not as a memory, but as a thing to be indexed and stored. The terror of that vision gave me a strength I did not know I possessed. I turned and ran, my un-silenced boots slapping against the crystal floor, a frantic, vulgar noise in this silent sanctuary. I did not look back. I scrambled, I fell, I clawed my way across the flawless surface, my mind screaming a single, desperate refrain: *I am! I am! I am!*

The machine was my only hope, my ugly, beautiful, physical ark. I threw myself into the saddle, my hands not fumbling this time, but acting with a desperate certainty. My fingers found the lever. The psychic pressure was immense now, a crushing weight that threatened to dissolve my very being. The world-mind was upon me, its collective will a vortex intent on pulling me in.

With a final surge of will, I thrust the lever forward.

They were the machine-minders, the herdsmen, the unseen foundation upon which the fragile beauty of the Upper World rested. And what did they take in return? I shuddered, recalling the almost fatuous placidity of the Eloi, their lack of any real fear or defence. I thought of the bronze gates of the great granite sphinx, and the way they had closed so inexorably behind my Time Machine. A dark and terrible suspicion began to form, a theory of sustenance so dreadful that I at first repulsed it. The Eloi were like fattened cattle, living in a carefully managed pasture. What if, in the long twilight of the world, the Morlocks had turned to a new kind of husbandry? The idea was too vile to contemplate, and yet it fit the ghastly puzzle with a sickening precision. The dread of the dark that the Eloi possessed, their one deep-seated instinct, was not a memory of ancient predators; it was a present and terrible reality.

I was shaken from these dreadful reveries by the fading of the light. The sun, a vast and bloody shield, was sinking once more towards the horizon, and the long, creeping shadows were stretching their fingers across the land. A chill that had nothing to do with the evening air settled upon me. I had to find Weena. I could not bear the thought of her being alone in the gathering dusk, and I felt a sudden, frantic need for her simple, unquestioning companionship. I found her where I had left her, sitting by a spray of rhododendrons, weaving a chain of flowers with a look of childish concentration. Her relief at seeing me was palpable. She chattered her soft, liquid syllables and pointed to the darkening sky, then to the great house, her meaning clear enough. It was time for sleep.

But I had no intention of sleeping. My mind was a furnace of questions, and I knew that the only answers lay down one of those dreadful shafts. I had to conquer my fear. I had to know. Taking Weena by the hand, I led her not to the great hall where the others slumbered, but back towards the clearing with the ruined well. She came trustingly, her small hand in mine, yet as we drew near the place she began to show signs of hesitation. She pulled at my coat, her cooing sounds turning to notes of distress. She pointed away from the well, towards the safety of the palace, her large, gentle eyes filled with a mute and earnest pleading. I tried to soothe her, to explain with gestures my purpose, but it was useless. Her terror of this place, and of what it represented, was absolute. When I made to approach the well’s edge, she gave a little cry of despair and clutched at me with all her strength. Her fear was infectious, and for a moment my own resolve faltered. How could I descend into that blackness, the lair of those foul creatures, leaving this one small, affectionate being to face the night alone?

And then an idea came to me. If I could not leave her, perhaps I did not have to. I needed a light, some weapon against the dark. My matches were nearly gone, and I knew that once in the depths, a single flickering match would be but a pinprick against the profound obscurity. I needed a torch, something that would burn for a time and give me a fighting chance. I thought again of the great fire in the forest. Fire! That was the Morlocks’ great terror. If I could carry fire with me, I might be able to navigate their world with some measure of security. Leaving Weena, who sobbed quietly in the twilight, I ran back towards the woods. It was not difficult to find what I needed. The forest was littered with dry branches and resinous wood. I gathered a small armful of the driest sticks I could find, and took a larger, sturdier branch to serve as the base of a torch. With these, and a handful of dry leaves and moss for tinder, I returned to the well.

Weena was still there, a small, forlorn figure huddled in the gloom. I showed her my preparations, striking one of my precious matches and lighting the tinder. As the small flame caught and grew, her fear seemed to lessen, replaced by a kind of fascinated wonder. I carefully built my torch, binding the smaller sticks to the larger one with tough, fibrous grasses. Soon I had a crude but serviceable brand, which I lit and held aloft. The flames leaped up, casting a brilliant, dancing circle of light and pushing the shadows back into a cowering retreat. Weena clapped her hands in delight, her fear of the well momentarily forgotten in her admiration for the beautiful, bright fire. Seeing my opportunity, I took her hand and led her to the very brink of the shaft. She flinched, but the comforting presence of the torch seemed to give her courage. I pointed down into the darkness, then to myself, then held up the torch, trying to convey my intention. I do not know if she understood my plan, but she seemed to grasp that I was not abandoning her. She merely held my hand tighter. With my torch in one hand and my other arm around Weena, I put my foot upon the topmost metal rung of the ladder and began my descent into the Underworld.

The journey downwards was more dreadful than I could have imagined. The air, warm and still at the surface, grew thick and heavy, laden with a nauseous, metallic odour, the smell of unaired tunnels and ill-oiled machinery. The darkness was not empty; it was a living, breathing presence that seemed to press in upon us, held at bay only by the wavering, precarious circle of my torchlight. Weena clung to me, her face buried in my shoulder, her body trembling. I whispered what soothing words I could, though my own heart was hammering against my ribs. The rungs of the ladder were slick with some unseen slime, and I had to move with the utmost caution. Every few yards, the main shaft would be pierced by smaller, horizontal tunnels, black mouths that gaped at us as we passed. From these openings, I fancied I could hear faint scuffling sounds and the whisper of movement. Once, my torchlight glinted on a pair of luminous eyes, identical to the ones I had seen in the ruin, watching us from the darkness of a side passage. They vanished as soon as the light fell upon them, but the sight sent a fresh wave of horror through me. I was an invader in their kingdom, and I felt their unseen eyes upon me at every step.

We must have descended several hundred feet when the shaft opened out into a vast cavern. The ladder continued downwards, but here the space was immense, the ceiling lost in the impenetrable gloom far above my torch’s reach. And I became aware of a sound, a low, rhythmic throbbing that vibrated through the very rock. The sound of a colossal, tireless machine. As my eyes adjusted, I began to make out shapes in the flickering light. All around the floor of the cavern were great, complex mechanisms, their purpose obscure, all pounding and turning and whirring in a ceaseless, mindless rhythm. Pistons rose and fell; great flywheels spun in the darkness; gleaming metal arms moved with a steady, inexorable purpose. And tending these machines were the Morlocks.

There were dozens of them, perhaps hundreds. They scurried to and fro in the half-light, their pale bodies stark against the dark iron of the machinery. They were not attacking; they seemed utterly absorbed in their tasks, oiling gears, adjusting levers, hauling materials from one machine to another. My torch, which had seemed so brilliant in the narrow shaft, was here a feeble thing. It illuminated only a small portion of the vast industrial panorama. The Morlocks near the edge of the light would start and scuttle away when the flames touched them, but those deeper in the cavern seemed oblivious to my presence, their large, unblinking eyes fixed upon their work. It was a scene from some industrial hell, a vision of labour stripped of all dignity, all purpose beyond its own perpetuation. These were the descendants of the human race, toiling in eternal darkness to maintain a paradise for an idle, beautiful, and utterly vacuous race of beings on the surface. The terrible irony, the ghastly logic of it all, struck me with the force of a physical blow.

I stood there on the ladder, frozen, for I do not know how long, Weena a dead weight in my arm. The heat from the machines was intense, and the air was thick with the smell of oil and hot metal. I realised that my torch was burning low. The flame began to sputter, and the circle of light shrank alarmingly. The moment the light faltered, the atmosphere in the cavern changed. The rhythmic sounds of the machinery were joined by another sound: a low, chittering murmur that rose from the darkness all around me. I could see them now, at the edge of the dying light, their forms indistinct, their eyes glowing with a hungry, reflected light. They were gathering. Their fear of the fire was being overcome by their curiosity, or by some darker impulse. Weena began to tremble violently again, letting out a soft, whimpering moan. I knew we could not stay here. My only hope was to get back to the surface, and quickly.

With a final, desperate effort, I turned and began to climb. The ascent was a nightmare. The torch was now little more than a smouldering ember, casting just enough light to show me the rungs immediately above. Behind me, below me, the chittering grew louder, more excited. I could hear the soft, padding footsteps of the Morlocks as they began to swarm up the ladder in pursuit. I felt a tug at my leg. I kicked out blindly, my boot connecting with something soft. There was a surprised squeal and the sound of a body falling, followed by a chorus of angry chattering from below. They were upon us. Weena screamed, a high, thin sound of pure terror. Hands, soft and yet unnervingly strong, clutched at my ankles, my legs, trying to drag me down. I was climbing now not just for my own life, but for hers, burdened by her weight, one hand occupied by the useless, dying torch. Another hand seized my coat, and I felt its fabric rip. The darkness was swarming with them. Their pale, fish-like bodies pressed against me, their foul breath hot on my skin. I was losing my grip, my strength failing. We were going to be dragged down into that mechanical hell and torn to pieces.

It was in that moment of absolute despair that my fumbling hand, searching for a weapon, a purchase, anything, brushed against the small box in my pocket. The matchbox. It was my last chance. With a final, desperate effort, I tore myself free from a few of the grasping hands, enough to get the box open. I scraped a match, the harsh, grating sound loud in the darkness. It failed to light. I tried another. It too sputtered and died. My heart hammered in my chest. They were pulling me down again, their chittering rising to a triumphant pitch. I scraped the entire side of the box with the remaining matches. For one glorious, blinding second, a dozen tiny flames flared up in my palm. In that brilliant, desperate flash, I saw their faces clearly: the great, vacant eyes, the lank, pale hair, the quivering, bloodless lips. And I saw something else. On the floor of the chamber, near the base of my machine, were scraps of wood, dried moss, and what looked like greasy rags, the detritus of the Morlocks’ subterranean life. An idea, born of pure desperation, flashed into my mind. As they recoiled from the sudden burst of light, I threw the burning handful of matches onto the pile of debris. It caught at once. A greasy, black smoke billowed up, and then a hungry yellow flame leaped into the air. I had started another fire, this time at my own feet, inside my only escape. The Morlocks shrieked and fell back from this new blaze, their terror of the fire absolute. The small conflagration gave me the space I needed. I vaulted into the saddle, my hands searching frantically in the flickering, smoky light for the controls. One lever to stop, another to go. I found the starting lever. I gripped it. The little monsters, seeing their prize about to vanish, made one last, frantic rush. They ignored the fire, scrambling towards the machine through the flames, their pale flesh singed and smoking. They swarmed over the frame, their hands reaching for me. One of them, more daring than the rest, clambered up the side and seized the lever with me, its surprising strength trying to wrest it from my grasp. Its hideous face, contorted with effort, was inches from my own. With all my remaining strength, I shoved the lever forward. There was the familiar lurch, the sickening sense of temporal displacement. The chamber, the fire, the screaming, burning Morlocks, all dissolved into a streaky, indistinct vortex of colour. The last sensation I had of that terrible age was of the creature’s grip loosening, of its body being torn from the machine as I plunged into the stream of time. I was moving. I was free. But as the kaleidoscopic blur of days and nights washed over me, my mind was not filled with triumph. It was filled with the image of a small, white figure, framed against a wall of fire, her arms outstretched. And in my pocket, I could still feel the gentle pressure of two withered, white flowers, the last gift from a world I had helped to destroy.

My journey was not towards the past, not back to the reassuring warmth and intellectual companionship of my own time; for in my blind terror I had thrust the lever forward, ever forward, into the unknown abyss of the ages to come. The initial sensation was one of relief, a frantic, gasping gratitude to be free from the clutching hands and searing heat. But this feeling was fleeting, a mere bubble on the surface of a deep and bitter despair. The ghost of the Morlock’s grip still seemed to burn on the lever, a phantom pressure against my own. I could not shake the image of its contorted face, nor the high, thin shriek as it was torn away into the temporal vortex. Was it killed? Was it scattered into atoms across a thousand years? I did not know, and the not knowing was a fresh kind of horror.

My thoughts, however, returned again and again, with the obsessive return of a fever-dream, to Weena. I saw her small, trusting face, her thoughtless grace, her simple affection which had been a balm to my harried soul. And I saw her as I last saw her, a tiny silhouette against the spreading fire I had created, her arms reaching for me in a gesture I could not decipher: of farewell, of accusation, of pleading? The weight of the two white flowers in my pocket was a physical ache in my side, a constant, tangible reminder of my failure, of the gentle life I had abandoned to a fate I dared not contemplate. I had been their god and their demon all in one. I had brought them light, and I had brought them fire, and I had left them to the darkness.

These recriminations churned in my mind as the ages bled past me. I had no control over the machine now. I was a passenger, a shell-shocked witness to the unravelling of the world. The flicker of day and night, once a dizzying strobe, now merged into a continuous, pulsating band of light upon one side of my vision, whilst the other remained a deep, star-pricked black. The moon became a faint, silvery shuttle, weaving a pale thread through the tapestry of the years. I saw great forests spring up, flourish, and crumble into black soil in what seemed a matter of moments. I saw mountains, those very symbols of permanence, erode like sandcastles before a tide, their proud peaks ground down, their valleys filled. New mountain ranges buckled and rose from the plains, glistening and raw, only to be weathered away in their turn. The sea, once a familiar blue, retreated from coastlines, laying bare entire new continents, then surged back, drowning all that had grown in its absence. Cities, had any remained, would have been but a puff of dust in this grand, remorseless pageant of geology.

I pushed the lever farther still, in a mad desire to outrun my own memory, to plunge so deep into the future that the world of Eloi and Morlocks would be a forgotten myth. I craved an oblivion that time itself would grant. The speed of my passage increased. The sun’s steady ribbon of light began to change. It broadened, its colour shifting from a brilliant yellow-white to a deeper gold, then to orange, and then, alarmingly, to a sullen, angry red. It was swelling, growing like some celestial tumour, filling a larger and larger portion of the sky. The heat on that side of the machine became intense, and I saw the very rocks below begin to glow with a faint, sympathetic heat. The earth’s rotation was slowing; the band of light and dark began to separate once more into a sluggish day and a ponderous night. The moon, my old companion in the sky, had vanished altogether. I realised with a jolt of pure, intellectual terror that it must have fulfilled the destiny that the younger Darwin had predicted; it had fallen back into its parent, a catastrophe that must have boiled the oceans and sterilised the planet, a cosmic blaze I had mercifully missed by my velocity.

The sun now hung in the sky, a vast, motionless, and bloated disc of deep red. It gave off a great deal of light, but a strange, mournful light that seemed drained of all vitality and warmth. A profound and terrible silence seemed to have fallen upon the world. The frantic, headlong rush of my journey felt like a profanity in this great, cosmic stillness. My panic had long since burned away, replaced by a desolate curiosity. I was, perhaps, the last reasoning mind to witness this final act, the ultimate inheritor of all the ages, alone on the stage as the curtain fell. With a slow, deliberate movement, my hand, which felt ancient and frail, went to the stopping lever. I pulled it back. The dizzying sensations lessened, the blur of the landscape began to resolve, and with a final, gentle lurch, the Time Machine came to rest.

The air that met my lungs was thin and bitter, and held a strange, metallic tang. I sat for a long time, peering out at the scene. I was on a sloping beach. The sand was a coarse, reddish grit, strewn with black, salt-encrusted rocks. Before me lay a sea, but a sea unlike any I had ever known. It was a dark, oily, leaden green, and it did not move. There were no waves breaking on the shore, no gentle swell; it lay as still as a sheet of glass, its surface only disturbed by a slow, thick heaving, as if the planet itself were breathing in its sleep. Above, the enormous, lurid sun stared down like a bloodshot eye, casting no shadows, but bathing everything in an even, twilight gloom. The sky was a uniform, dark violet, and I could see the brighter stars twinkling even in the presence of the sun. The silence was the most unnerving part. It was not the absence of sound; it was a positive quality, a presence that pressed in on my ears, a silence of absolute and final death.

I unsteadily climbed out of the saddle. My limbs were stiff, and I felt a great weariness, a weariness not of the body, but of the soul. The ground was firm beneath my feet. I walked a few paces down the slope towards the dreadful sea. Looking back, my machine seemed a small, almost foolishly intricate thing, a child’s toy abandoned on the shore of eternity. I thought of the men in my dining room, of their cigars and their brandy and their comfortable scepticism. What would they say if they could see me now? What would they make of this final, bleak rebuttal to all their theories of progress and purpose?

I scanned the horizon. There was no tree, no blade of grass, no lichen on the black rocks. The planet was a sterile cinder, washed by a poison sea, under a dying sun. And yet… and yet, there was movement. Down by the water’s edge, something huge and black was stirring. It moved with an incredible slowness, a thing of jointed legs and a glistening, carapaced body. It looked like some monstrous, nightmarish crab or scorpion, the size of a small table. As my eyes adjusted, I saw more of them, dozens, perhaps hundreds, crawling with an ant-like persistence along the shoreline, or half-submerged in the thick, green water. They paid me no mind, engaged in some slow, incomprehensible business of their own. These, then, were the inheritors. In the great struggle for existence, it was not the mammal, not the subtle brain and the nimble hand, but the armoured, mindless, and enduring arthropod that had triumphed in the end. They were the final, scuttling punctuation mark at the end of Earth’s long story.

I turned my back on them and the sea, and began to explore inland, up the gentle slope. The red sand gave way to cracked, barren rock, split and shattered by unimaginable aeons of heat and cold. I felt a profound loneliness, a solitude so vast and absolute it was almost a physical pain. I was the last man. The thought was not a proud one, but a terrifying one. I was an anomaly, a ghost from a world of sunlight and laughter and complexity, haunting the mausoleum of my own planet. As I walked, my foot dislodged a stone, and I saw something flutter weakly from beneath it. It was a small creature, no larger than my hand. It was white, or nearly so, with large, delicate wings. A moth? A butterfly? It seemed impossible. It fluttered in a slow, tired circle in the thin air, a speck of impossible fragility in this world of rock and carapace. It seemed to have no purpose, no direction. It flew towards me, and with a final, expiring effort, landed upon the sleeve of my coat.

I brought my arm up to look at it. It was, I think, a moth, of a species utterly unknown to me. Its wings were veined with the faintest patterns of grey, like a fine ash-drawing. Its body was small and pale. It clung to my arm for a moment, its antennae twitching feebly, and then, it was still. It had died. Here, at the very end of all things, in the face of cosmic indifference and biological supremacy of the most brutal kind, a flicker of delicate, complex, and beautiful life had endured, only to expire in my presence. It was so utterly pointless, so heartbreakingly futile. It was the story of the Eloi, of Weena, of humanity itself, played out in miniature. We too had fluttered for our brief moment, creating things of beauty and grace, only to be crushed by the slow, inexorable forces of time and our own nature.

A great wave of emotion, colder and more terrible than the thin air, washed over me. My scientific curiosity, my intellectual detachment, all of it fell away, revealed as the thinnest of veneers. I stood on that desolate shore, with a dead moth on my arm and two withered flowers in my pocket, and I wept. I wept for Weena. I wept for the gentle Eloi. I wept even for the pallid, damned Morlocks, my brothers in humanity, lost to the darkness they had made for themselves. I wept for the failure of my own age, for our smug belief that we were the pinnacle of creation, when we were merely a step on the road to this. My theory, the one I had so proudly constructed, of the Capitalist and the Labourer, seemed now like a child’s simplistic drawing. It was true, perhaps, for a chapter, but it was not the whole story. The whole story was this: a red sun, a silent sea, and the scuttling of mindless things on a shore of universal death.

How long I stood there, I cannot say. Time had lost all meaning. But eventually, the paroxysm of grief passed, leaving behind a cold, hard clarity. I had seen the ultimate consequence. I had travelled to the end of the book and read the final, disappointing sentence. There was nothing more to learn here. There was only one journey left to make. I brushed the delicate, dusty remains of the moth from my sleeve. I turned my back on the setting of Earth’s sun, and walked with a steady, determined tread back towards the one incongruous thing in all that landscape of ruin: my Time Machine. I had to go back. I did not know if I could bear the world I had left, with its easy laughter and its blind optimism. I did not know how I could sit with my friends and speak of trivial things, my mind full of the images of fire and darkness and this final, silent desolation. But I had to try. A storyteller must have an audience, even if his tale is one that will break their hearts. I had seen the future, and now, my burden was to return and try, somehow, to explain it. I climbed back into the saddle, my hand reaching not for the forward lever, but for the one that would carry me home, back to the beginning of the end.

She was one of the Eloi. She put them in my pocket as a sign of affection. She was… a friend.” The flowers lay there between us, a fragment of a lost paradise, a testament to a love that had bloomed at the end of time. They were more eloquent than any words I could utter. They spoke of sunlight, and gentle hands, and a trust that had been so terribly betrayed. The Medical Man leaned forward, peering at them not as a scientist, but as a man looks upon a relic. He reached out a hesitant finger, but did not dare to touch them. “Extraordinary,” he murmured, and for the first time, I saw a crack in his professional scepticism. The silence that followed was different. It was no longer the silence of disbelief, but of a profound and unsettling wonder. They did not believe me, not truly, not in the way they believed in the laws of physics or the circulation of the blood. But the story, my haggard appearance, and now these impossible flowers; it had all combined to shake them from the comfortable certainty of their world. It had opened a window, just a crack, onto a panorama of such staggering immensity and terror that their minds instinctively recoiled from it. They began to speak then, in low, troubled tones. They offered theories. Could the machine have induced a powerful, waking dream? Perhaps a series of hallucinations brought on by the vibrations and the ozone? The Psychologist spoke learnedly of temporal lobe seizures and the creation of complex, narrative delusions. The Medical Man wondered about exotic toxins I might have inhaled. They were trying, in their own way, to save me from my own story. They were trying to fold the edges of my terrible reality back into the neat, safe envelope of our shared, prosaic existence. I did not argue. I was too weary. I let them talk, their voices a meaningless drone against the roaring silence of the future in my memory. They needed their explanations; they needed to believe I was ill, or mad. The alternative was to believe that their world, their ambitions, their society, their very species, was on a fixed and terrible trajectory towards the world I had described. The alternative was to accept that the lights of their great city were but a prelude to the eternal darkness of the Morlock warrens, and their own refined lives a mere fattening for the slaughter. It was a truth too monstrous to be borne. And so, eventually, they took their leave. They patted my shoulder, urged me to rest, and promised to call again in the morning. They departed one by one into the London night, leaving me alone in my dining room. I sat there for a long time, the remnants of the meal grown cold, the wine untouched in the glasses. I looked at the two withered flowers on the table. They were the only reality for me now. They, and the Time Machine that stood waiting in the laboratory, grim and silent, smeared with the dust of ages yet to come. I had told my story. I had delivered my warning. And it had been received as all such prophecies are: as the ravings of a madman. I had returned from the end of all things, bearing the most important knowledge in the history of the world, and I was utterly, completely alone. I picked up one of the delicate, dried blossoms. Its faint, ghostly scent seemed to fill the room, a fragrance of lost sunlight and irretrievable innocence. And I knew then that my journey was not over. They did not believe. But I had to know more. I had to understand the path. I had to see the intermediate stages. My place was no longer here, in this comfortable, blind present. My place was out there, in the river of Time. And I knew, with a certainty that was both a terror and a strange comfort, that I would travel again.

My theory, born in the ruins of the future, was that the widening gulf between the Capitalist and the Labourer was the key. Was it so simple? Was the path so straight? I had to know. I rose from my chair. The house was silent, a tomb of Victorian complacency. I went to the laboratory. There it stood, my strange and terrible chariot, a thing of nickel, ivory, and glittering quartz, its surfaces dulled by the dust of a future no other man had breathed. Its very presence was a rebuke to the tidy world outside the laboratory walls. I ran my hand along the rail. It was cold, solid, real. It was the only real thing left to me. I spent the next day in a fever of preparation. I did not sleep. I worked with a quiet, methodical intensity, the way a man might prepare for a polar expedition from which he knows he may not return. I oiled the bearings, which grated with the fine grit of a distant age. I polished the quartz rods, which seemed to hold a faint, residual light, the ghost of a thousand vanished suns. What a thing it would be, to return not with a story, but with a photograph of the future! A single, undeniable artifact. The thought was a small, cold comfort. I set the dials, but not for that distant shore of time where I had met Weena. I had seen the final chapter. Now I wished to read the middle of the book. I wanted to see humanity in the throes of its great division. I calibrated the machine for a nearer time; a time far enough to show significant change, yet not so far as to be utterly alien. I chose, arbitrarily, a date some thirty thousand years from my own present. It was a mere step, a fractional turn of the lever, compared to my last voyage. As the evening of the second day drew in, I was ready. I did not write a note. To whom would I address it? What could I say? “Gone to survey the wreckage of our species. Be back for tea.” The absurdity of it was manifest. My friends thought me mad already; a disappearance would only confirm it. Let them think what they will. My audience now was not my own time, but Time itself. I climbed into the saddle. The familiar feel of the ivory and nickel was like the touch of a strange accomplice. I took a last look around my laboratory; at the benches littered with tools, the diagrams on the walls, the comfortable clutter of a life of inquiry. It all seemed to belong to another man, a naive and optimistic fellow I had once known. I pulled the lever. The sensation was the same, yet utterly different. The first time, it had been a journey into wonder and dread. This time, it was a descent into a known abyss. The familiar vertigo took me; the sun swung in a blazing arc across the sky, and the moon followed in its silvery wake, but this time I felt no exultation, only a grim and profound melancholy. The walls of my laboratory dissolved into a transparent flicker, and the world outside became a shimmering, indistinct river of form and colour. Days and nights swept by in rhythmic pulses of light and dark. I saw my house crumble, then vanish. I saw new structures rise and fall. I watched London grow into a monstrous, glittering plate of light, then recede, changing its form a dozen times. Great towers of glass and metal speared the sky, then they too grew old, were eroded by the centuries, and collapsed. The land buckled and shifted. The Thames changed its course, widening into a great estuary before narrowing again. I kept my hand on the lever, my eyes fixed upon the chronometer, watching the centuries bleed into millennia. I felt like a pathologist, peering through a microscope at a tissue sample, watching the slow, inexorable progress of a disease. When the dials indicated I had travelled close to my destination, I began to slow the machine. The frenetic flickering resolved itself into a dizzying succession of days and nights, then into the blur of a single day. I brought the machine to a halt with a jolt that threw me forward against the controls. Silence. A profound, humming silence. The air was warm and heavy, laden with the scent of unfamiliar blossoms and a strange, metallic tang. I had stopped on what appeared to be a broad, paved terrace made of a material that shone with a milky, internal lustre. It was night, but the sky was not dark. A soft, pervasive luminescence came from the city that stretched out before me, a city of such impossible scale and grace that it made my own London seem a squalid village. Towers, slender as needles and impossibly tall, soared into the sky, their tips lost in the low clouds. They were not lit by windows, but seemed to glow with their own soft, radiant energy. Between them, great, silent causeways, suspended by unseen forces, linked the towers at dizzying heights. There were no lights moving along them. Indeed, there was no movement at all. The entire city, vast and beautiful, seemed utterly deserted. It was the city of a dream. My first thought was that I had miscalculated, that I had travelled too far and arrived in another, later age of decline. But this was no ruin. The air was clean, the structures were perfect, their surfaces unmarred. This was not a place of decay; it was a place of sterile perfection. I dismounted, my boots echoing unnaturally on the shining pavement. I walked to the edge of the terrace. Below me, the city swept away into the luminous haze. And I saw people. They were not numerous, but they were there. On a lower terrace, a group of them stood as if in a tableau, clad in flowing robes of a deep, velvety black. They were tall and slender, with a languid grace that reminded me instantly, and painfully, of the Eloi. But their beauty was not the soft, helpless beauty of the Eloi; it was a sharpened, aestheticised perfection, cold as a statue. Their faces, pale in the city’s glow, were masks of serene indifference. They did not speak or interact. They simply stood, posed like figures in a work of art, their purpose, if any, entirely opaque to me. Was this the evolution of the Capitalist? A class so refined, so removed from the necessities of life, that their very existence had become a form of decorative art? Had all effort, all struggle, all purpose, been bred out of them, leaving only this cold, static beauty? The thought chilled me more than the Morlocks’ subterranean world. This was not the simple, childlike innocence of the Eloi; this was the deliberate, cultivated emptiness of a species that had exhausted all meaning. I spent what I think was a full day and night exploring the upper levels of this strange, silent world. I saw no one eat. I saw no one work. I saw no children. The people, the Overworlders, I began to call them in my mind, drifted from one immaculate plaza to another, sometimes gathering to watch holographic displays of abstract colour that swirled in the air, sometimes listening to music that seemed to emanate from the very fabric of the buildings, a music of complex, mathematical purity that held no passion, no joy, no sorrow. They ignored me completely. My tweeds, my sturdy boots, my very appearance, so solid and coarse amidst their ethereal grace, rendered me invisible. I was a vulgar anachronism, a piece of grit in their perfect machine, and they simply edited me from their perception. I tried to speak to one, a lone male who was observing a fountain from which fell not water, but a shimmering cascade of what looked like liquid light. I touched his arm. He did not flinch, but turned his head slowly, his eyes, large and dark, looking through me as if I were a pane of glass. There was no curiosity, no fear, no anger. There was nothing. He then turned back to the fountain, the moment forgotten, his attention once more consumed by the meaningless, beautiful display. I felt a surge of frustration, followed by a profound sense of pity. These creatures were the Eloi in chrysalis. They were already lost, cocooned in a world of perfect comfort, their minds slowly atrophying within their beautiful shells. But if they did no work, how was this paradise maintained? The air was temperate, the food, for I did eventually find sterile cubicles where nourishing, flavourless paste was dispensed from a wall, was plentiful, the light was constant. Who tended the machines? My theory of the Labourer provided the obvious, terrible answer. I began to search not for people, but for the infrastructure that supported them. I left the gleaming upper terraces and descended, following vast, sweeping ramps that spiralled down into the foundations of the city. The lower I went, the darker and more utilitarian the architecture became. The glowing pearl-surfaces gave way to polished black stone, then to bare, titanic metal. The air grew warmer, and the metallic tang I had noticed on arrival became a thick, coppery taste in the back of my throat. The humming silence was replaced by a low, powerful thrumming, the heartbeat of a monstrous, unseen engine. And then I found it. It was no simple well shaft, but a vast, engineered portal, a cyclopean mouth of bronze set into the base of one of the support pylons. It was closed, but from it emanated the heat and the thrumming, and a slow, rhythmic pulse of air, like the exhalation of a sleeping giant. There were no controls visible, no handles, no locks. It seemed designed never to be opened from this side. For two days I staked out that portal. The Overworlders never came near it. It was, I surmised, part of the machinery of their world that they were conditioned to ignore. On the third day, my patience was rewarded. With a soundless grinding, the great bronze doors slid apart, revealing a brightly lit tunnel that descended steeply into the earth. Out of it marched a squad of figures. They were not the ape-like Morlocks I so dreaded. Not yet. They were bipeds, and they walked with a purpose that was entirely absent in the languid beings of the upper world. They were shorter than the Overworlders, and far more thickly built. They wore simple, dark grey overalls, and their skin was not bleached white, but had a pale, unhealthy, sallow look. Their heads were large, and their eyes, accustomed to the bright light of their tunnel, were narrowed against the perpetual twilight of the lower city. They were not monsters. They were workers. They moved with a grim efficiency, servicing the machinery at the base of the tower, cleaning intakes, and checking conduits. They did not speak, but communicated with curt hand gestures. One of them, presumably a foreman, carried a device that emitted sharp clicks. When their work was done, they formed up and marched back towards the portal. This was my chance. Seizing the opportunity, I slipped from my hiding place behind a great metal buttress and fell in at the rear of their formation as they entered the tunnel. None of them looked back. To them, I suppose, the idea of an Overworlder willingly entering their domain was as unthinkable as one of them choosing to stay in the sun. The bronze doors slid shut behind us, and I was in their world. The tunnel was brilliantly lit by glowing strips along the ceiling, and it descended at a sharp, unvarying angle. The air was hot, filled with the smell of ozone and hot metal. The thrumming became a deafening roar. After a walk of perhaps a mile, the tunnel opened out into a cavern of such staggering dimensions that the St. Gothard tunnel would be a mere rabbit hole beside it. The entire space was filled with machinery. Titanic flywheels, fifty feet in diameter, spun with a silent, terrifying speed. Great pistons, the size of houses, drove up and down in pits of boiling fluid. Arcs of blue electricity leaped between massive copper spheres. And everywhere, there were the workers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They swarmed over the machinery like ants, oiling, adjusting, replacing parts. They were the ghosts in the machine, the secret that made the serene paradise above possible. I saw men and women, their faces grimy and set in expressions of perpetual, weary concentration. I saw children, not playing, but performing simple, repetitive tasks in spaces too small for adults to reach. They were pale, stunted, their eyes large and dark. Here it was, then, the terrible engine of their paradise. The effortless grace of the world above was bought and paid for by this sunless, soul-crushing toil below. The Capitalist and the Labourer; I had seen the jest in my own time, but here it was writ large upon the face of the world, a joke so monstrous it had carved a species in two. I broke away from the squad I had followed and hid in the shadows of a colossal gear-train, overwhelmed by the sheer horror and scale of it all. This was not the chaotic, feral world of the Morlocks. This was a highly organised, brutally efficient industrial society, a factory that had consumed a world. These were not yet the bestial cannibals of the far future; they were simply a hereditary working class, trapped in a subterranean hell of their own ancestors’ making. But the seeds were there. In the dim light, I saw their large eyes. I saw their pallid skin. I saw their stocky, powerful bodies, adapted for labour in confined spaces. They were on the path. Thirty thousand years of this, and what would they become? A hundred thousand? Eight hundred thousand? They would forget the sun entirely. They would forget the purpose of their labour. The machines would one day fail, and the Overworlders, unable to feed or care for themselves, would become merely helpless, walking cattle. And these creatures, their keepers, their forgotten brothers, would be hungry. My scientific detachment shattered. I was no longer an observer. I was a man from the age that had laid the foundation for this nightmare. I saw a group of workers taking their meal break, squatting on the metal floor to consume a grey nutrient paste identical to that served in the sterile cubicles above. As they ate, one of them looked up and saw me. His eyes widened. Not with fear, or with the predatory hunger I had seen in the Morlock, but with a look of utter, profound confusion. He tugged the sleeve of his neighbour. Soon, a dozen of them were staring at me. My clothes, my height, my face burnished by a sun they had never felt, I was an aberration, a contradiction to their entire existence. The foreman, the one with the clicking device, strode towards me. He was shouting, but his voice was lost in the din of the machines. His face was contorted in anger. I was an impurity, a disruption to the perfect, grinding rhythm of his world. He raised the clicking device as if to strike me. The old instinctive dread came upon me, but it was not the dread of a wild beast. They were not fast, but they were tireless, and they knew the terrain. I ran blindly, my heart pounding, my mind reeling from the confirmation of my worst fears. I had sought the process, and I had found it. I had found the long, slow, grinding millstone that was turning Man into Eloi and Morlock. After an eternity of stumbling through the roaring darkness, I found another ascending tunnel, this one a service shaft, equipped with a crude metal ladder. I clambered up it, my lungs burning, the sounds of pursuit growing fainter below. It emerged, mercifully, into the deserted lower levels of the city of the Overworlders. I did not rest. The placid, indifferent faces of the Overworlders I passed seemed to mock me with their emptiness. They were the beautiful, gilded death’s-head of the human race. I found the Time Machine where I had left it, a strange, solid promise of reality in this world of nightmares. I scrambled into the saddle, my hands shaking. I did not look back. I pulled the lever, and with a lurch and a flash of vertigo, the perfect, terrible city of the year 30,000 dissolved into a shimmering haze. I did not stop until the dials on the chronometer read my own time, my own dear, blind, complacent nineteenth century. I stumbled out of the machine and into the familiar, dusty silence of my own laboratory. I was home. But I was more broken than when I had left. The first journey had shown me a terrible end. This second journey had shown me the road. It was a road paved with progress, with efficiency, with the very social and industrial tendencies we so lauded in my own time. We were not falling into barbarism. We were building it, meticulously, piece by piece, and calling it civilisation. I had gone seeking a clue to a theory. I had returned with a verdict on my species. And the weight of that knowledge was a burden far heavier than any man should have to bear.

It was late afternoon. A hansom cab rattled by on the street below, the horse’s hooves making a rhythmic, comforting clatter on the cobblestones. Now, I saw it as a biologist might observe a petri dish, watching the doomed colonies of microbes grow and divide, blissfully unaware of the fate I had prepared for them.

In the face of the well-dressed man stepping from the cab, his posture erect, his expression one of mild and pampered boredom, I saw the ghost of an Eloi. I saw the generations of ease, of aesthetic refinement, of the slow, steady draining away of vigour and necessity. And in the stooped shoulders of the grimy boy who darted out to hold the horse, his face pale and smudged, his eyes quick and accustomed to the gloom of alleys, I saw the ancestor of the Morlock. I saw the long descent into the tunnels, the adaptation to darkness and machinery, the gradual loss of the sun and all that it symbolised. It was no longer a theory. It was a vision. I had stood at the end of the road and looked back, and now I could see the signposts at its very beginning, here in my own dear, blind, complacent nineteenth century.

The weight of this knowledge was a physical thing. It pressed down upon my chest, making it difficult to breathe. I felt an urgent, desperate need to speak, to cry out, to warn them. But who would listen? Who could possibly understand? I thought of my friends, of the little circle to whom I had told my first story: the Medical Man, the Psychologist, the Editor, and young Filby. They had listened with polite, and in some cases, barely concealed, incredulity. They had humoured me. They had patted me on the back and spoken of overwork and the tricks of a powerful imagination. And that was when I had proof! I had brought back those strange, waxen flowers from Weena, proof that I had been somewhere else, that something inexplicable had occurred. This time, I had returned with nothing but a horror in my mind and a frantic energy in my soul. I had no evidence, no alien flora, nothing but the testimony of a man who they already considered half-unhinged by his first fantastic voyage.

And yet, I could not bear the silence. I could not sit alone in my laboratory with this apocalyptic secret burning in my brain. To be the sole Cassandra of a species, to know the coming tragedy and be unable to convince a single soul, was a torment greater than any I had imagined. I went to my desk, my hand shaking so much that my penmanship was a near-unreadable scrawl, and I wrote several brief notes. I summoned them, my friends, for that very evening. I wrote with a feverish intensity, pleading with them to come, telling them it was a matter of the utmost, the most terrible, importance. I sent my housekeeper out to have them delivered by a messenger boy, and then I sat and waited, pacing the floor of my laboratory like a caged animal. The Time Machine stood silent in the corner, its brass and quartz glinting in the low light. It was no longer a triumphant invention to me; it was a curse, an oracle that had spoken a truth too dreadful to be borne.

They came, all of them. I think the urgency of my notes had alarmed them, made them fear for my health or my sanity. They found me, I think, a pitiable sight. I had not changed my clothes; my hair was wild, and my face, when I caught a glimpse of it in a darkened windowpane, was pale and haunted. The Medical Man at once came forward and took my wrist, his fingers pressing to find my pulse.

“Steady on, old man,” he said, his voice full of a professional, patronising calm. “You look as though you have seen a ghost.”

“More than a ghost, Doctor,” I said, my voice hoarse. “I have seen the future. Again. And I have seen the process. I have seen how the ghosts are made.”

I ushered them to their chairs, the same chairs they had occupied when I had first told them of my journey to the year 802,701. The Psychologist watched me with a keen, analytical eye, his head tilted slightly. The Editor looked uncomfortable, as if he had been summoned to a scene of domestic distress. Filby, practical, red-faced Filby, simply looked concerned.

“You have used the machine again?” the Psychologist asked, his tone carefully neutral.

“I have,” I said. “I had to. My theory of the Eloi and the Morlocks, of the splitting of the species… it was incomplete. I had seen the result, but not the cause, not the long, slow, grinding machinery of it. I had to know. I set the dials for a nearer time. The year 30,000. A mere hop, compared to the last. I wanted to see Man in his adolescence, as it were, on his way to that strange and terrible maturity.”

I began to speak. I told them everything, the words tumbling out of me in a torrent. I spoke of the perfect, silent city, more beautiful and more sterile even than the world of the Eloi. I described the Overworlders, these creatures of exquisite form and utter vacuity, their lives a meaningless pageant of aesthetic trifles. I tried to make them see the terrible emptiness in their eyes, the placid surface of a pond beneath which all life had died. And then I told them of my descent, of the hum that pervaded all things, of the discovery of that vast, subterranean world of industry.

“It was a factory,” I said, my voice rising with the memory of it. “A factory that spanned a continent. Machines, vast and relentless, labouring without cease. And they were tended! Not by Morlocks, not yet. They were tended by men, or things that were still recognisably men. Pale, stooped, their eyes unaccustomed to light, their hands shaped to the lever and the switch. They were the Underworlders, the foundation upon which the indolent beauty of the surface was built. The divorce was not yet absolute, you see! The species had not yet fully split. But the path was laid. The Overworlders lived in the sun; the Underworlders toiled in the darkness. One class had become a pet, the other had become a tool.”

I told them of the confrontation, of the foreman or manager who had raised the clicking device, his face a mask of fury at my intrusion. I described my flight through the roaring labyrinth, the clanging alarm, the feeling of being an impurity in a perfect, heartless system.

“Do you not see?” I pleaded, looking from one face to the next. “It is our world, magnified! It is the gulf between the Capitalist and the Labourer, made geographical, made biological! We are driving the workers underground, into conditions that cut them off from the sun, from nature, from the very things that make us human. We are building a society where the rich cultivate a useless refinement, a life so protected that it breeds weakness. We are not falling into this future; we are building it, with every new subway, every new underground factory, every new law that fences off the land for the privileged!”

I finished, breathless, my hands clenched. The room was silent, save for the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece. It seemed to me a dreadfully loud sound, each tick a hammer blow on the coffin of humanity.

The Psychologist was the first to speak. He cleared his throat. “A fascinating elaboration,” he said, in a tone one might use for a complex but flawed mathematical proof. “Your first experience was clearly one of immense psychic trauma. It is not at all uncommon for the mind, seeking to process such an event, to create a more detailed, more… systematic narrative to explain it. You have taken the social anxieties of our own time, which are very real, I grant you, and projected them onto the framework of your initial delusion. You have built a logical cause for the illogical effect you believe you witnessed.”

“It was no delusion!” I cried.

The Medical Man leaned forward. “Your pulse is racing,” he stated. “Your skin is clammy. Your pupils are dilated. You are in a state of extreme nervous excitement. You have not been sleeping, I’d wager. You have been obsessing, letting this fantasy consume you. This second ‘journey’ is a product of that; a fever-dream, a waking nightmare brought on by exhaustion and a singular fixation.”

“And the machine?” I gestured wildly towards it. “Did a fever-dream polish the brass? Did a delusion wear down the ivory of the lever?”

It was Filby who answered, his voice uncharacteristically gentle. “We don’t know what that machine does, old man. We’ll grant you that. It makes a wind and a flicker. Perhaps it jars the senses. Perhaps it induces a trance. But last time… last time you brought something back. You had proof. Strange, withered flowers that no botanist could identify. It was not enough to make us believe your story, perhaps, but it was… something. A tangible mystery. Where is your proof this time? Did you bring back one of these clicking devices? A piece of an Underworlder’s uniform? Anything?”

I stared at him. The question was a physical blow. I had no answer. I had fled for my life, my mind a whirl of terror and revelation. I had not thought to grab a souvenir from the brink of hell.

“I… I have nothing,” I stammered. “I ran. I had to escape.”

“Of course you have nothing,” the Psychologist said softly. “One does not bring back evidence from a dream.”

Their faces, arranged before me, swam in a blur of pity and dismissal. They were not mocking me now. It was worse. They were treating me as an invalid, a patient to be soothed. They saw not a prophet, but a madman. My desperate warning was, to them, merely a symptom of my disease. The chasm I had tried to show them, the one that would divide the human race, was nothing compared to the chasm that now opened between me and them, between my terrible knowledge and their comfortable sanity.

“I see,” I said at last, my voice flat and devoid of all its earlier passion. “I see that I cannot make you understand.”

They exchanged glances. The Medical Man produced a small vial from his bag. “I will leave you a sleeping draught,” he said. “A deep, dreamless sleep is what you require above all else. And tomorrow, we can talk about a rest. A long stay in the country, perhaps. Away from this… stimulating apparatus.”

They rose to leave, making quiet, soothing noises. They patted my shoulder. They urged me to rest. They promised to look in on me the next day. Their kindness was more galling than any argument. The door closed behind them, and the sound of their footsteps receded down the hall.

I was alone. Utterly and absolutely alone.

I did not take the sleeping draught. How could I sleep, knowing what I knew? To sleep would be to acquiesce, to accept their diagnosis and admit that my journey, my truth, was a fiction. I stood in the centre of my laboratory, the silence now a crushing weight. The world outside the window went on with its life. The gaslights cast their gentle pools of illumination. People were dining, talking, laughing, living, all of them walking towards the precipice with their eyes closed.

I looked at the Time Machine. It was my accuser, my confessor, my curse. And it was my only escape. I could not stay here. I could not live out my days in this world of ghosts, this nursery of horrors. I could not watch the children at play in the park without seeing the seeds of the witless Eloi. I could not look upon the face of a labourer without seeing the pale, emergent form of the Morlock. My own time had become a prison.

My mind, which had felt so chaotic, now settled upon a single, cold point of clarity. My first journey had been one of scientific curiosity. The second, of intellectual necessity. If there was to be a third, it would be a journey of desperation. I could not go back to the future I had seen; it was a finished chapter. But what of other futures? What of the long path between my time and theirs? Was this decay a straight and inevitable line? Or were there branching paths, chances for redemption, moments where history could have been turned to a different, better course? Was there, anywhere in the vast expanse of time to come, a future where Man had not met this obscene fate? Where he had conquered the division in his own nature, instead of succumbing to it?

It was a fool’s hope, perhaps. A madman’s errand, as my friends would have it. But it was the only hope I had left. To stay was to go mad. To go back to the past was to hide my head in the sand. The only way was forward. Onward.

I walked to the machine, my steps firm. I ran my hand over the cool brass of the framework. I did not feel the frantic terror of my last flight, nor the eager anticipation of my first departure. I felt a grim, quiet resolve. I was an outcast from my own species, a man unstuck in time, burdened with a truth that made life unbearable. Very well. Then I would make the river of time my home. I would become a pilgrim of the ages, searching not for knowledge, but for a reason to hope.

I climbed into the saddle. I did not set a specific date. My hand went to the larger lever, the one that flung a man through the centuries as a stone is flung from a sling. I thought of the serene faces of the Eloi and the glaring eyes of the Morlocks. I thought of the automated forges of the year 30,000 and the placid Overworlders who lived above them. I thought of the pitying, uncomprehending faces of my friends. There was nothing for me here. There was nothing for me there. I had to find a third way, or perish in the search.

I set my jaw, and thrust the lever forward. I did not care where. I cared only to be gone from a world that I knew too well, and from a future I could not bear. The laboratory dissolved into a swirling vortex of colour and shadow, the familiar ticking of the clock swallowed by the rising hum of eternity. I was moving again, faster and faster, a solitary consciousness adrift in the silent, screaming torrent of time. I was running away, but I was also running towards something. A verdict. An answer. Or an oblivion that would be, in itself, a kind of peace.
