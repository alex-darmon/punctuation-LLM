An odd certainty took him that his watch had just unwound itself. But Dr. Aris Thorne was no believer in errant timepieces. He stood staring at his own wrist. Had it really spun backwards? He looked about the laboratory again, but noticed nothing further than the usual clutter of beakers and burners. Then he distinctly heard a faint, reversed sigh, as though a breath were being drawn into lungs that had just expelled it. All men, however steeped in logic, retain some residue of superstition. The feeling that is called “primal” came upon him. He backed away from the workbench, turned towards the door, and set down his satchel. Suddenly, with a gasp, he perceived a wisp of steam from a kettle re-enter its spout, leaving the air quite clear.

He stared at this in confusion. It was an empty moment, a lacuna in the world’s proper functioning. He would have dismissed it as a trick of the eye, but a touch arrested him, and a voice speaking quite close to his ear.

“Aris!” said the Voice.

“Eh?” said Thorne, his shoulders stiffening.

“Don’t make a sound,” said the Voice. “I am a Displaced Man.”

Thorne made no answer for a space, simply stared at the kettle. “Displaced Man,” he said.

“I am a Displaced Man,” repeated the Voice. The article he had laughed at in the morning’s Chronicle rushed through his brain; a sensationalist piece about temporal anomalies. He does not appear to have been either very much terrified or very greatly shocked at the moment. Comprehension came later.

“I thought it was all rubbish,” he said. The thought uppermost in his mind was his own confident dismissal of the theory. “Are you here now?” he asked.

“Yes,” said the Displaced Man. “And no.”

“Oh!” said Thorne, and then roused himself. “I say!” he said. “But this is nonsense. It’s some new kind of projection.” He spun around suddenly, and his hand, reaching for the empty air, met a shoulder of solid tweed. He recoiled at the contact and his face paled.

“Keep steady, Aris, for pity’s sake! I need a place to anchor. Stop!” The hand gripped his arm. He struck at it.

“Aris!” cried the Voice. “Aris! Keep steady!” and the grip became iron. A frantic desire to break free took possession of Thorne. The hand released him, and he was suddenly shoved sideways, stumbling against a stool and crashing into a rack of glass tubes. He opened his mouth to shout, and a dusty rag was thrust against his lips. The Displaced Man had him pinned, but his legs were free and he kicked and struggled savagely.

“Listen to me, will you?” said the Displaced Man, holding him fast in spite of a vicious kick to the shin. “By Heaven! you’ll have me undone in a minute!”

“Let go, you devil!” bawled Thorne into the rag.

Thorne thrashed for another moment and then went limp.

“If you shout, I will simply not be here,” said the Displaced Man, removing the rag. “I am a Displaced Man. It is not spiritualism, and not a hoax. I am truly displaced. And I need your help. I do not wish you harm, but if you behave like a panicked fool, I must. Do you not remember me, Aris? Cole, from Trinity College?”

“Let me stand up,” said Thorne. “I’ll not move from this spot. And let me think for a minute.” He righted the stool and sat down heavily. “I am Cole, from Trinity College, and I have detached myself from the stream. I am just a normal man; a man you once knew; made asynchronous.”

“Cole?” said Thorne.

“Cole,” answered the Voice, which now seemed to emanate from a shimmering patch of air by the window. “The quiet one who lodged opposite you, with the spectacles and the passion for celestial mechanics, who won the chancellor’s medal for physics.”

“I am bewildered,” said Thorne. “My mind is a chaos. What has this to do with Cole?”

“I _am_ Cole.”

Thorne thought. “It’s impossible,” he said. “But what madness must occur to make a man asynchronous?”

“It’s no madness. It’s a process, a resonance, dreadfully intelligible, ”

“It’s ghastly!” said Thorne. “How in God’s name, ?”

“It is ghastly enough. But I’m exhausted and weakening, and I am so cold… Great Heavens! Aris, you are a man of science. Accept the evidence. Give me some brandy and let me sit down here.”

Thorne stared at the shimmer as it moved across the room, then saw his heavy oak reading chair slide a foot across the floor and come to rest by the cold stove. It groaned, and the leather cushion compressed the better part of an inch. He rubbed his face and gripped the edge of the stool. “This is worse than ghosts,” he said, and gave a short, sharp laugh.

“That’s better. Thank God, you’re regaining your senses.”

“Or losing them entirely,” said Thorne, and squinted. “Give me some brandy. I’m half dead.”

“It did not feel so. Where are you? If I stand up will I pass through you? _There_! all right. Brandy? Here. Where do I hand it?”

The chair groaned again and Thorne felt the decanter drawn away from him. He let go by an act of will; his every nerve screamed against it. It came to rest, held by nothing, ten inches above the arm of the chair. He stared at it in profound perplexity. “This is, this must be, a delusion. You have induced a waking dream.”

“Nonsense,” said the Voice.

“It’s frantic.”

“Listen to me.”

“I proved empirically only last week,” began Thorne, “that temporal displacement would require, ”

“Never mind what you’ve proved! I’m fading,” said the Voice, “and the world is a cold place for a man with no purchase on it.”

“Food?” said Thorne.

The decanter of brandy tilted itself. “Yes,” said the Displaced Man, setting it down with a rap. “Have you a thick coat?”

Thorne muttered some exclamation under his breath. He walked to a closet and produced a heavy wool greatcoat. “This do?” he asked. It was taken from him. It hung limp for a moment in mid-air, shuddered violently, stood full and solid as if on a form, and then sat down in his chair.

“Boots, a hat, gloves would be a mercy,” said the Unseen, curtly. “And food.”

“Anything. But this is the most lunatic situation I have ever been in, in my life!” He rummaged in a locker for the articles, and then went to his small pantry. He came back with a loaf of bread and some cheese, pulled up a small table, and placed them before his guest.

“Never mind a knife,” said his visitor, and a hunk of cheese floated in the air, with a sound of vigorous chewing. “Displaced!” said Thorne, and sat down on the stool again.

“I always like to feel something solid around me before I eat,” said the Displaced Man, with a full mouth, eating greedily. “Strange habit.”

“I suppose that hand is all right,” said Thorne, gesturing at the coat.

“Trust me,” said the Displaced Man.

“Of all the bizarre and impossible, ”

“Precisely. But it’s a stroke of luck I landed in _your_ laboratory to find my footing. My first piece of good fortune! Anyhow I meant to stay in this building to-night. You must allow it! It’s a damnable nuisance, this shimmering, isn’t it? The edges of me grow indistinct. It’s only the solid matter I’ve anchored to, and only for as long as I can concentrate… I’ve been in this room for an hour, flickering.”

“But how is it done?” began Thorne, in a tone of exasperation. “Confound it! The entire business; it’s illogical from start to finish.”

“Quite logical,” said the Displaced Man. “Perfectly logical.” He reached over and the brandy decanter levitated again. Thorne stared at the devouring greatcoat. A beam of lamplight, catching the shimmer around the collar, cast a distorted, watery shadow on the wall behind.

“What was that flicker?” he asked. “The light on the wall?”

“A resonance,” said the Displaced Man. “A harmonic of my own time bleeding through. Curse it! It gets worse when I am fatigued.” He secured the brandy decanter and poured a generous measure, the liquid sloshing into a glass held by the gloved, but still unseen, hand. Thorne watched, his scientific mind warring with the sheer impossibility of the spectacle. “It’s unreasonable from beginning to end,” he repeated, almost to himself.

“Quite reasonable,” said the Displaced Man, the voice muffled by food. “Perfectly reasonable, if you grant the premise.” He swallowed. “The premise being that time is not a river, but a book. A book with pages one may, with sufficient force, press through.”

“Press through?” said Thorne, leaning forward. His fear was giving way to an intense, almost painful curiosity. “You built a machine.” It was not a question.

“Of course I built a machine! A chronal resonator. Based on my own theories of aetheric vibration. You’ve read my papers on the subject?”

“The ones that were roundly dismissed by the Royal Society? ‘Speculative fancy,’ they called them.”

A dry, humourless laugh came from the greatcoat. “The fools! The blind, earth-bound fools! I did it, Thorne! I generated a field of sufficient intensity to thin the stratum between moments. To create a… a permeability.” He paused, and the coat seemed to shiver. “I only meant to send a test object through. A brass weight. But there was a feedback loop, a power surge from the city grid. The entire apparatus went white hot. I was standing too close.”

“And it sent you… here?”

“Not here. Not anywhere, precisely. It tore me out of my proper place. I am unstuck, Thorne. A ghost in the world’s machinery. I flicker between my own time and this one. For the first few hours, it was a nightmare of overlapping sensations. The smell of my laboratory’s ozone and your wretched pipe smoke. The sight of my own hands on the controls and this very brandy decanter. The sound of my machine’s scream and the ticking of that clock on your mantelpiece.”

Thorne glanced at the clock. It was silent; he had not wound it in a week. He felt a cold prickle on his neck.

“It has taken me all my strength, all my will, to cohere here, in this room, in this moment. To find a single point of anchorage. And I am fading. I can feel the pull of my own time, like a tide dragging me back to the rocks.” The voice was strained, losing its arrogant edge and taking on a note of raw desperation.

“But what do you want with me?” asked Thorne. “I am a physicist, yes, but my work is in optics. This is… this is metaphysics.”

“I want your laboratory,” said the Displaced Man, bluntly. “I want your equipment. And I want your brain. You are not a fool, Thorne. You saw the possibilities where others saw madness. I need help. I cannot manipulate fine instruments; my hands pass through them half the time. I cannot stay coherent long enough to perform the calculations. I am a prisoner of my own magnificent discovery.”

Thorne stood up and paced the small room, his mind a riot of conflicting thoughts. It was madness. It was a hoax. It was a dream. But the weight of the greatcoat in the chair, the smell of brandy in the air, the sheer, frantic sincerity of the Voice; it was all horribly, tangibly real.

“Who else knows of this?” Thorne asked, stopping before the chair.

The greatcoat was still for a moment. “There was… an associate. A man named Jennings. A man with more ambition than brains, and more greed than either.”

“Is _he_ displaced too?”

“No. He was a safe distance away. Watching. He saw what happened. He saw me vanish.” The gloved hand of the coat clenched into a fist. “And he has my notebooks, Thorne. All my calculations. All my schematics. He has the key, and he is too stupid to understand the lock!”

“Then what is the danger?”

“The danger?” The voice rose, sharp and angry. “The danger is that he will try to replicate it! The man is a brute, an animal. He thinks he can weaponise it, sell it to the Admiralty. He doesn’t understand the delicacy, the catastrophic risk! If he tears another hole like the one that took me… he could destabilise this entire sector of spacetime! Or, more likely, he will simply blow himself and half of London to kingdom come. And in the meantime, he is hunting me.”

“Hunting you?” Thorne said, bewildered. “How can one hunt what cannot be properly seen?”

“With a copy of my own work! I designed detectors to measure chronal stress. Crude things, but effective. He is likely using one now. He knows I must have grounded myself somewhere. He knows I am weak. He will be searching for the epicentre of the disturbance. Me.”

Suddenly, from the coat, came a sharp intake of breath, followed by a low groan. The hunk of cheese, which had been resting on the table, abruptly slid to the floor.

“What is it?” said Thorne, startled.

“The strain… I am losing purchase.” The voice was faint, distant. The solid-looking form in the chair seemed to waver, its outlines blurring into a watery shimmer that distorted the lamp-light behind it. “Brandy… quickly.”

Thorne snatched up the glass from the table, his hand trembling. Where was the man’s mouth? He pushed the glass towards the collar of the coat. It was met by an unseen, frantic grasp, and a good deal of the liquid splashed onto the woollen fabric, where it beaded for a moment before vanishing. The form in the chair seemed to solidify again, the shimmer receding.

“Curse it,” the Displaced Man breathed, his voice stronger. “It is like trying to hold onto a cliff-face with one’s fingernails. Every moment is a struggle.”

Thorne stared, his decision made in that instant of panicked helplessness. The scientist in him had won. This was not merely a lunatic in his rooms; this was the greatest scientific discovery, and the greatest scientific peril, in human history. He was a part of it now, whether he liked it or not.

“My laboratory is at the university,” Thorne said, his voice low and steady. “It is better equipped than this small private study. We would need to get you there.”

“Yes,” said the Voice from the coat. “Yes, that is exactly right. Your main laboratory. I have seen the catalogues of your equipment. You have a high-frequency induction coil, and a sufficiently powerful generator.”

“How could we move you? A man in a greatcoat and hat, walking with no face. The first policeman who saw us would raise the alarm.”

“We go now. At night. The streets are empty enough. Who will look twice at a man bundled against the cold? You will walk beside me. Talk to me. As if we are two colleagues returning late from the club.” The plan was audacious, insane, but it was a plan. “I need more than this, though.” The coat gestured towards its empty collar. “A scarf. Something to hide the… absence.”

Thorne went to his wardrobe and pulled out a thick woollen scarf. “Wrap it high,” he said, holding it out.

The scarf was taken from his hands. It coiled in the air like a serpent, wrapping itself around the collar of the greatcoat, tucking itself in with an unnervingly human dexterity. Then a hat, Thorne’s own bowler, floated up from the locker and settled on top. The illusion was ghastly, but almost complete. A man, heavily bundled, sat in the chair. Only if one looked closely would one see the strange, impossible void within the scarf, the utter blackness where a sliver of face should be.

“We will need my notebooks,” said the Displaced Man. “We cannot proceed without them. Jennings has them at his residence. Number 14, Purbright Terrace.”

“Steal them?” Thorne was aghast. “I am a Fellow of the University, not a burglar!”

“You are a man of science on the verge of the abyss!” the voice snapped, its impatience returning. “What are a few social niceties now? He stole them from me. We are merely recovering my property. And my life.” The figure stood up, the chair creaking under a departing weight. It stood at a height with Thorne, a broad, solid shape in the lamplight. “Are you with me, Thorne? Or will you leave me to fade into nothing, and leave that fool Jennings to blunder his way to armageddon?”

Thorne swallowed, his throat dry. He looked at the faceless man before him, this impossible creature of science and nightmare. “I am with you,” he said, and the words felt like a sentence.

“Good. Then there is no time to waste. His residence is not far. We will go there first.” The Displaced Man took a step, the floorboards groaning. He seemed steady, for now. “Stay close to me. If I begin to shimmer, engage me in conversation. Talk about your work, anything. The focus helps me cohere.”

Thorne grabbed his own coat and hat, his heart pounding a frantic rhythm against his ribs. He turned down the lamp, plunging the room into shadow, leaving only the faint glow of the dying embers in the hearth. He opened the door to his rooms, peering out into the silent, gas-lit corridor of the apartment building. It was empty.

“Now,” whispered the Voice, a sound at once beside him and inside his own head.

They moved out into the corridor, Thorne’s own footsteps echoing loudly in the silence. Beside him, another set of footsteps, muffled by the boots, fell into a steady rhythm. It was the most terrifying walk of his life. He could feel a faint coldness at his side, a subtle displacement of the air. He dared not look too closely, for fear of seeing a walking greatcoat with nothing inside, held up by sheer, desperate will.

Down the stairs they went, the Dispassed Man’s hand, or the glove where his hand should be, gripping the banister. Thorne saw the wood depress slightly under the pressure. They reached the ground floor, a hall of polished marble and potted ferns. The night porter was dozing in his chair, a newspaper fallen across his lap. Thorne held his breath. They had to walk right past him.

“Talk to me, Thorne,” the voice whispered, a hint of strain in it. “Now.”

“The… the refractive indices,” Thorne began, his voice a strained croak. He cleared his throat. “The refractive indices of crystalline structures under electrical stress… I have been postulating a fourth-dimensional aspect to their matrix.” He was spouting nonsense, but his mind had gone blank.

“Fascinating,” the Displaced Man said, his voice a low conversational rumble, designed for the benefit of the sleeping porter. “And does this conform to Maxwell’s equations?”

They were past. The heavy oak doors of the entrance were just ahead. Thorne fumbled with the latch, his fingers clumsy with dread. He pushed the door open, and the cold night air of the London streets washed over them. The gas lamps cast long, dancing shadows. A lone hansom cab rattled by in the distance. They were out.

“Purbright Terrace,” said the Displaced Man, his voice now crisp and commanding. “Lead the way. And for God’s sake, try to look natural.”

Thorne, a man whose life had been governed by logic, reason, and the predictable behaviour of light, found himself walking through the sleeping city with a man who was not entirely there, on their way to commit a burglary. He felt a hysterical laugh bubbling in his chest. He was either going mad, or he was on the cusp of a new reality. He did not know which was more terrifying. The greatcoat beside him shimmered faintly under a gas lamp, and Thorne began talking, desperately, about the behaviour of polarised light in quartz, his voice a thin thread of sanity in the vast, impossible night.

had not the ghost of an idea. “Natural?” he croaked, then swallowed. “Yes. Quite.”

Purbright Terrace proved to be a row of respectable, even handsome, brick houses, their windows dark and unblinking, their porticoes standing in pools of shadow cast by the infrequent gas lamps. It was profoundly silent. The only sound was the scuff of Thorne’s own leather soles on the flagstones and the unnervingly solid, yet muffled, tread of the boots beside him. He could feel the coldness of the Displaced Man as a distinct pressure, a pocket of winter in the mild spring night.

“Which one?” Thorne whispered, his voice sounding absurdly loud.

“Number seventeen,” said the Voice, low and strained. “My house. Or it was.”

Thorne stopped. “Your house? Then why in God’s name are we skulking about like burglars?”

“Because I am no longer its master. And because another may be in residence. A man named Jennings. A colleague of mine.” There was a universe of venom in the word “colleague.” “He coveted my work. It appears he has now acquired my property as well. We must go round the back. The laboratory extension overlooks the garden.”

They crept down a narrow, cobbled alleyway that ran behind the houses. The air was thick with the smell of damp brick and coal smoke. High walls, topped with broken glass, loomed on either side. Number seventeen had a tall wrought-iron gate, securely locked.

“There is no way through,” Thorne stated, his relief palpable.

“For you, perhaps,” the Voice replied. There was a faint grating sound, and Thorne watched, his mouth agape, as the bolt on the inside of the gate slid back with a loud, metallic clack. The gate swung inward an inch. “After you.”

Thorne hesitated, then pushed through, his heart a wild drum against his ribs. He was trespassing. He was an accomplice. The thought was so alien, so contrary to his entire existence, that for a moment he felt dizzy. He found himself in a small, well-tended garden. A square of lawn, a few rose bushes, and at the far end, a single-story brick structure with a large skylight in its roof. A pair of French doors faced them, their glass panes reflecting the distant city glow.

“The laboratory,” the Voice confirmed. “The doors will be bolted, top and bottom. The windows, however… The latch on the far left is faulty. I was always meaning to have it repaired.” A gloved hand, holding itself in the air with a slight, unnerving tremor, pointed. “I will need to get inside.”

“How?”

“You will provide a diversion, should one be needed. But with any luck, the house sleeps.”

Thorne watched, transfixed, as the greatcoat and hat seemed to melt away, deposited in a heap by a rose bush. There was a rustle of clothing, then nothing. A complete and utter absence. He could hear faint breathing, and the almost imperceptible crunch of a boot on the gravel path as the unseen presence moved towards the building. He saw a faint depression in the soft earth of a flowerbed beneath the window. Then, a dark shape, the shape of a gloved hand, materialised against the glass. It fumbled at the edge of the window frame.

There was a tiny click.

The window slid upwards a few inches, silently. A moment later, it slid fully open. A cold draught washed out from the dark interior. Thorne waited, every nerve screaming. The silence stretched. One minute. Two. He began to think the Displaced Man had simply vanished, dissolved into the ether, leaving Thorne alone in a stranger’s garden.

Then the French door nearest him gave a slight shudder. The top bolt slid back with a soft, oily sound. Then the bottom. The door swung open.

“Quickly,” the Voice whispered from the darkness. “And quietly.”

Thorne slipped inside, his eyes struggling to adjust. The air was thick with the scent of ozone and something else, a sharp, metallic tang like hot wire. Vague, complex shapes loomed in the gloom; coils of copper, vast glass vacuum tubes, and frameworks of brass and steel. It was a physicist’s nightmare, a wonderland of arcane apparatus.

A match scraped, and a tiny bead of flame bloomed in the centre of the room, held aloft by nothing. It moved to a gas-jet on the wall, and with a soft pop, a single fishtail of flame illuminated the scene. Thorne blinked.

In the centre of the laboratory stood a monstrous device. It was a great cage of brass rings, all centered upon a multifaceted crystal the size of a man’s fist, which was held in a delicate-looking clamp. Wires, thick as snakes, ran from the cage to a bank of accumulators against the far wall. The entire construction sat upon a heavy mahogany table, which was bolted to the floor.

“My God,” Thorne breathed.

“The Harmonising Resonator,” said the Voice, with a note of bitter pride. “The source of, and the intended solution to, my predicament. It generates a resonant frequency that displaces matter along a fourth spatial axis. A controlled, localised displacement. Or, it was meant to be controlled.”

A pair of boots and trousers became visible, walking towards the machine. “I was calibrating the focal field when it happened. A power surge. The entire field collapsed inwards, taking me with it. I am not invisible, Thorne. I am… out of phase. Displaced. My own atoms are vibrating at a frequency that is not quite in harmony with the rest of the universe. Light passes through me. Most matter, too, if I am not… focused.”

“Focused?”

“It takes a conscious effort of will to remain solid. To interact. To hold my own form together. It is exhausting. Infuriating. And it is growing weaker. I am beginning to… fray at the edges.”

Thorne stared at the empty space from which the voice came. “Fray?”

“Sometimes I feel myself becoming diffuse. Less solid. My thoughts scatter. That is why I need the machine. I must recalibrate the crystal, generate a counter-frequency, and pull myself back. All the way back.”

The boots stopped beside the great brass cage. There was a sharp intake of breath. “No.”

“What is it?”

“The crystal,” the voice was a strangled whisper. “It is cracked. Look.”

Thorne stepped closer. He peered at the large, complexly-cut crystal in its clamp. A hairline fracture ran across one of its largest facets, almost invisible save for the way it broke the faint light from the gas-jet. And something else. A fine, dark powder had been dusted into the crack.

“Sabotage,” the Voice spat. The word was pure poison. “That devil. Jennings!”

At that exact moment, a floorboard creaked in the house beyond the laboratory.

Thorne froze, his blood turning to ice. The Voice was utterly silent. They both stood, unmoving, in the flickering gaslight, listening. Another creak, closer this time. The soft shuffling of slippers on a wood floor. A line of yellow light appeared under the door that connected the laboratory to the main house.

“He is here,” the Voice hissed, so low it was barely audible. “He has been waiting.”

The doorknob began to turn.

“Stand behind the accumulators,” the Voice commanded, a sudden, sharp authority in the whisper. “Do not move. Do not make a sound.”

Thorne, his scientific mind utterly eclipsed by pure, primal instinct, scurried into the shadows behind the bank of electrical equipment. He peered through a gap in the wiring. The door swung open, revealing a tall, thin man in a dressing gown, holding a heavy porcelain chamber pot. He had a narrow, clever face, with a neatly trimmed beard and spectacles perched on his nose.

“I heard you, Alistair,” the man said to the empty room. His voice was calm, almost conversational. “I knew you would come back. You cannot resist the pull of your own magnificent failure.”

There was no answer. The man, Jennings, smiled thinly. He set the chamber pot down carefully on the floor.

“No need for this absurd silence, Alistair,” he continued, walking slowly towards the central apparatus. “I know you are here. I can practically smell the desperation. Your condition is worsening, is it not? The displacement is becoming unstable.” He ran a gloved finger along the brass ring of the Resonator. “A pity about the crystal. Such a delicate piece of work. One clumsy moment, and… catastrophe.”

“You did this, Jennings,” the Voice, Alistair’s voice, said. It seemed to come from a spot just to the right of the machine. Jennings turned his head slightly.

“I merely corrected your oversight,” Jennings replied smoothly. “You were going to publish. To claim it all for yourself. After all the work I put in. The calculations. The theoretical framework. You called me your colleague, but you treated me as your assistant.”

“You were a glorified bookkeeper!” Alistair’s voice was a furious snarl. “You understood nothing of the true physics!”

“I understood enough to see this,” Jennings said. He reached into the pocket of his dressing gown and produced a small, metal canister with a nozzle, not unlike those used for spraying perfume. “I understood that a man who is merely displaced is still made of matter. And matter, my dear Alistair, can be… decorated.”

With a sudden movement, Jennings squeezed the canister. A fine, greyish mist shot out, clouding the air around the Resonator. Where it drifted, nothing happened. But then Jennings aimed it to the right, and the effect was instantaneous and horrific.

A human form, sketched in swirling grey dust, appeared as if by magic. It was the shape of a tall, broad-shouldered man, his arms raised in a defensive posture. The powder clung to him, outlining his invisible body, his invisible clothes, every fold and crease. He was a statue made of smoke, a furious, shimmering ghost.

“You viper!” the dust-figure roared.

“Now we can speak on equal terms,” Jennings said with a triumphant smirk. He took a step back, pulling a heavy iron poker from the stand beside the cold fireplace. “I have lodged your notes with the Royal Society. Under my name, of course. They are calling it the ‘Jennings Displacement’. A theoretical triumph. All that was needed was the final, practical proof. Your return, and your subsequent… dissipation, will provide it beautifully.”

The dust-figure of Alistair lunged. It was a movement of shocking speed, but Jennings was ready. He swung the poker, not at the figure, but at the floor in front of it. The iron rod struck the flagstones with a deafening clang, and the figure recoiled.

“You see, I am not a fool,” Jennings said, panting slightly. “I have no wish to fight a man I can barely see. But your machine… your machine is vulnerable.” He jabbed the poker towards the delicate glass tubes and wires surrounding the main apparatus.

“Don’t!” Alistair cried, his voice full of anguish.

Thorne watched from the shadows, his mind a riot. This was madness. A duel between a man with a poker and a man made of dust. But Alistair was trapped. If he tried to get to Jennings, Jennings would destroy the machine, his only hope of salvation.

“I have a proposition, Alistair,” Jennings said, his confidence growing. “You will come with me. You will allow me to document the final stages of your decay. Your name will be a footnote in the paper, I promise you. ‘With thanks to my late assistant, Alistair Finch’. A fitting epitaph.”

The dust-figure was trembling with rage. The grey powder swirled and eddied around it. “I would rather disperse into nothing than give you the satisfaction, you thief!”

“Then I shall have the satisfaction of watching you try to stop me,” Jennings said, and he raised the poker high, aiming for the fractured crystal at the heart of the Resonator.

It was in that moment that Thorne moved. He was not a man of action, he was a man of thought, but the sight of such calculated, scientific cruelty broke something in him. He grabbed the heaviest object he could find in the darkness of his hiding place, a thick glass insulator, cold and dense in his hand.

“Jennings!” Thorne shouted.

Jennings spun around, his face a mask of shock. He had forgotten there might be another. In that instant of surprise, Thorne hurled the insulator with all his strength. It was a clumsy, desperate throw, but it was true enough. It sailed across the room and smashed into the porcelain chamber pot Jennings had set by the door.

The pot exploded with a noise like a gunshot. Shards of white porcelain flew across the room. Jennings cried out and staggered back, shielding his face.

“Now!” roared the dust-figure of Alistair.

He did not run at Jennings. He ran at the machine. His dusty, shimmering hands plunged into the brass cage, straight towards the clamp holding the crystal. Jennings, recovering, saw the movement and screamed in fury, swinging the poker wildly. “No! It is mine!”

The poker connected with the brass cage. There was a tremendous clang, a shower of sparks, and the entire apparatus groaned. One of the great glass vacuum tubes shattered, imploding with a hollow cough. Alistair’s dusty hand closed around the fractured crystal, yanking it free from its clamp.

“Thorne! The gas!” Alistair yelled.

“Don’t you?” Davies leaned forward, his voice dropping. “We’ve spent the morning taking statements, Mr. Thorne. Dozens of them. A milkman whose dray was wrecked by an unseen force. He reached into a drawer in his desk and pulled out an object wrapped in a handkerchief. He unwrapped it carefully. It was a shard of glass from the inn window.

“My constable found this on the sill,” Davies said. “It has blood on it. Your blood?”

Thorne looked at his hands. There was a small, clean cut on his elbow from where he had shattered the pane. “I… I broke the window. I panicked.”

Thorne stared at him, mute.

“We tested it,” Davies continued, his voice relentless. “The physician found it to be… peculiar. He’s a man of science, like yourself. Thorne’s mind was a maelstrom. Transparent blood cells. It was a scientific nightmare. It was proof. Alistair was not a ghost; he was a biological anomaly. Davies was not a fool; he was a methodical, intelligent man piecing together an impossible puzzle. And Thorne was sitting at its centre. To tell the truth was to be branded a lunatic. To remain silent was to be an accomplice to whatever madness Alistair would unleash next.

He thought of the crystal. He thought of Alistair’s voice, sharp with command. “Break it! Now!” He had been a tool. A pawn. Abandoned at the first sign of trouble. A hot surge of anger, sharp and clarifying, cut through his fear.

“His name,” Thorne said, his voice quiet but firm, “is Alistair Finch.”

Davies leaned forward, his eyes alight with sudden, intense focus. “Go on.”

And so Thorne began to talk. He told him everything. Of the chance meeting in a lonely country lane, the disembodied voice, the story of a scientific experiment gone horribly wrong. He told him of the desperation, the paranoia, the slow, corrosive effect of invisibility on a man’s soul. He did not mention the crystal; some deep, primal instinct told him to hold that back. It felt like a secret too dangerous to speak. But he described Alistair, his brilliance, his arrogance, his terrifying new state of being.

He spoke for nearly an hour. Davies listened without interruption, his expression unreadable. When Thorne finally fell silent, exhausted and empty, the sergeant stood up and walked to the window, looking out over the grey slate roofs of the town.

“Alistair Finch,” he said to the glass. “He matches the description of the man whose purse was stolen. A gentleman scientist, staying at The Royal Arms. His rooms were found torn apart this morning.” He turned back to Thorne. “I believe you, Mr. Thorne. Not because I wish to, but because the facts, however insane, are beginning to fit your story. God help us all.”

He came back to the table. “You say he is a man of science. That he needs a laboratory. We have checked every known laboratory in the district. They are all secure.”

“He will make one,” Thorne said, a dreadful certainty dawning on him. “He will find a place. A large, empty house. Somewhere he can work undisturbed.”

Davies nodded slowly. “Like The Grange.” He looked at Thorne, and for the first time, Thorne saw not a policeman, but a man burdened by a terrible new knowledge. “You will have to help us find him, Mr. Thorne. You are the only one who knows how he thinks.”

“I?” Thorne was aghast. “But I am his victim!”

“You are his colleague,” Davies corrected gently. “And you are now the only man in Burdock who truly understands the nature of the beast we are hunting.” He picked up the telephone on his desk, his movements suddenly brisk and decisive. “Get me the chief constable,” he said to the operator. “And prepare a carriage. Mr. Thorne and I are going for a ride.”

The day was already dying as their carriage rattled towards the outskirts of the town, towards the high stone walls of The Grange. Thorne sat numbly, the world outside the window seeming distant and unreal. He was no longer a prisoner, but he was not free. He was an unwilling participant in a manhunt for a monster he had helped create, a monster who was once his friend. And he knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that Alistair would be waiting. Not for the police, but for him. The thought was terrifying. And yet, beneath the terror, there was another feeling, a strange, dark flicker of anticipation. The curtain was rising on the next act of their incredible, horrifying play.

Thorne’s blood ran cold. He was being toyed with, a mouse in a cage with a cat that had tired of the chase and now preferred a more intellectual form of cruelty. With a shaking hand, he found the bolt, slid it back with a screech of protesting metal, and the door swung inwards a few inches. Cool, clean night air, smelling of damp earth and sanity, washed over his face. It was the smell of a world to which he no longer belonged.

He renewed his grip on the unfortunate Davies and dragged him out, over the threshold and onto the gravel of the drive. He let the policeman down as gently as he could beside a great, overgrown rhododendron bush. Only then did he turn and look back at The Grange. It was a black silhouette against a sky of bruised purple and charcoal, a place of silent, waiting menace. No lights showed. No sound emerged. Yet he felt watched.

He shook himself, a dog trying to shed water. The carriage. The driver. He had forgotten. He ran, stumbling on the uneven gravel, around the curve of the drive to where they had left the vehicle. “Trouble, sir?” the man asked, his voice thick with a provincial accent.

“My… my colleague has been taken ill,” Thorne stammered, his mind racing for a plausible lie. “He fainted. A weak heart. We must get him to a doctor at once.”

The driver looked from Thorne’s wild eyes to the dark mass of the house and back again. He was a man accustomed to the oddities of his fares, but suspicion was plain on his face. “Fainted, you say? Heard a bit of a crash from in there. Sounded more than a faint.”

“He struck his head as he fell,” Thorne said quickly, the lie coming more easily now. “Please, there is no time to waste. Help me get him into the carriage.”

Together, they half-carried, half-dragged the still unconscious Davies to the carriage and bundled him onto the seat. He was a pitiable sight, his uniform askew, his face pale and slack in the faint light of the carriage lamp.

“Where to, sir?” the driver asked, climbing into his box. “Police station?”

“No!” Thorne said, too quickly. He moderated his tone. “No, Dr. Anderson’s surgery on High Street. It is closer. The man needs medical attention, not a report sheet.”

The driver shrugged and cracked his whip. The carriage lurched into motion, its wheels crunching on the gravel as it turned away from The Grange and back towards the distant, comforting lights of Burdock. Thorne sank back against the worn leather of the seat, his body trembling with a mixture of exhaustion and adrenaline. He stared out of the window, but he did not see the passing hedgerows. He saw only a pair of red eyes, burning with a fanatical light, and a disembodied voice that echoed in the chambers of his skull. “You’ll come back.”

The journey was a blur of disjointed thoughts. He had abandoned Davies to a country doctor with a hastily concocted story of an unexplained fainting spell and a fall. He had given a false name. He had paid the driver handsomely for his trouble and his silence, and then he had walked, almost fled, through the back streets of Burdock to his own home.

His house, a respectable brick villa in a quiet, tree-lined road, had never seemed so fragile, so vulnerable. He let himself in, his hand shaking so much he could barely fit the key in the lock. The familiar scent of beeswax and old paper did little to soothe him. The silence within was different from the silence of The Grange; it was a hollow, expectant silence. He found himself listening, holding his breath, for a footstep that was not his own, for the creak of a board upstairs.

He went at once to his study, a room lined with books, his sanctuary of reason and logic. He locked the door behind him and leaned against it, his eyes closed. Reason. Logic. What place did they have in a world where a man could shed his own visibility as a snake sheds its skin? He crossed to the decanter on his side table and poured a large measure of brandy, his hand still unsteady. He drank it in two swallows, the spirit burning a welcome fire in his belly, but it did not touch the ice in his soul.

He sat down heavily in his worn leather armchair. The events of the morning, his confident dismissal of the wild rumours of an invisible man, seemed like a memory from another man’s life. He had been so certain, so smug in his scientific rationalism. And Alistair had shattered it. Alistair. Alistair Finch. Not some ghoul or phantom, but a man he had known, a man with whom he had shared tutorials and debated the finer points of optical physics. A prodigy, certainly. Intense. Unstable, perhaps. But this? This was a transgression against the very laws of nature.

Thorne got up and began to pace the confines of his study, his mind a riot of frantic, circular thoughts. How? The question hammered at him. What process, what devilish combination of chemistry and physics, could achieve such a thing? He remembered Alistair’s obsessive work on refractive indices, his wild theories about altering the optical properties of living tissue. Thorne had dismissed them as brilliant but impractical fantasies. He had been wrong. Horribly, terrifyingly wrong.

A floorboard creaked in the hall outside.

Thorne froze, his heart leaping into his throat. He stared at the locked door, his ears straining. Was he here? Had Alistair followed him? The thought was a physical blow. He imagined the unseen presence on the other side of the door, listening to his frantic heartbeat.

“Alistair?” he whispered, the name dry and scratchy in his throat.

There was no answer. Only the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall and the frantic drumming of his own pulse. He stood there for what felt like an eternity, a man besieged in his own fortress. The fortress felt terribly flimsy. He thought of the windows, the chimney, the dozen ways an invisible man might gain entry. The house was no longer a sanctuary; it was a trap.

He forced himself to move. He went to his desk, lit an oil lamp, the flare of the match making the shadows leap and dance. He pulled out a sheaf of old papers, notes from his university days. He found what he was looking for: a monograph by Alistair Finch, “On the Theoretical Manipulation of Pigment and Refraction in Organic Structures.” He read the dense, frantic prose, the pages filled with complex equations and speculative leaps that he had once marked as “Ingenious but unsound.” Now, they read like a madman’s gospel, the blueprint for a miracle or a monstrosity.

He spent an hour like that, lost in the labyrinth of Alistair’s genius, the brandy bottle close at hand. The science was both appalling and beautiful. It was a complete, internally consistent system. Alistair had found a way to bleach every cell, every particle of tissue, to a state of perfect transparency, to lower its refractive index to that of air. It was not magic. It was a process. Sane and intelligible enough, as a voice in another story might have said. But the implications were insane.

The grandfather clock in the hall struck eleven. The sound made him jump. He had been hiding for hours. Hiding from what? From a man who was likely still miles away, in a cold, dark house. A man who was wounded, alone, and… waiting.

“You’ll come back. You need answers.”

Alistair was right. He could not live with this. He could not simply go to the police and tell them the truth; they would think him mad. He could not pretend it had not happened. He was bound to Alistair by the chains of shared knowledge, of a past collegiality that now seemed like a dreadful curse. He was the only one who understood. And that understanding was a terrible responsibility.

A grim resolve began to settle over him, displacing the frantic terror. He was a man of science. This was a scientific problem, albeit a grotesque and unprecedented one. He had to face it. He had to understand it fully. And he had to stop it.

He stood up, his movements now steady and deliberate. He went to a locked cabinet in the corner of the study and took out a heavy, military-issue revolver, a relic from his late father. He checked that it was loaded, the cold, heavy weight of it in his hand a small and inadequate comfort. He found a bullseye lantern and a fresh tin of oil. He put on a heavy tweed coat against the chill of the night.

He unlocked his study door and walked into the hall. The house was still. The silence no longer felt expectant, merely empty. He let himself out into the night, locking the door carefully behind him. He did not look back.

The walk to The Grange was long and cold. The streets of Burdock were deserted now, the gas lamps casting long, lonely pools of light on the slick cobblestones. He felt utterly alone, a solitary figure moving towards a destiny he had not chosen. The flicker of anticipation he had felt earlier was gone, replaced by a cold, hard dread that sat like a stone in his stomach.

He reached the high stone walls of the estate. The iron gates stood slightly ajar, just as he and Davies had left them. He slipped through, the lantern in one hand, the revolver in the other, its grip slick with the sweat of his palm. The drive was a tunnel of blackness, the trees on either side looming like monstrous sentinels. The house appeared before him, a hulk of deeper black against the night.

He did not hesitate at the door but pushed it open and stepped inside, into the vast, echoing hall. He raised the lantern, its beam cutting a swathe through the oppressive darkness, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air, the ghostly shapes of shrouded furniture, the grand staircase climbing into shadow.

“Alistair!” he called out, his voice sounding small and thin in the immense space. “I’m here. I’ve come back.”

For a long moment, there was only silence. Then, a faint sound from above. A creak. A shifting of weight.

“I knew you would,” the Voice said. It came from the top of the stairs, from the deepest part of the shadows, and it was filled with a triumphant, chilling satisfaction. “A man of your intellectual curiosity could not possibly stay away. Come up, Thorne. Come up to my parlour.”

Thorne began to climb the stairs, the lantern beam preceding him, the revolver held tight against his chest. Each step echoed in the profound stillness of the house. He reached the landing, a wide, bare expanse of floorboards.

“Where are you?” Thorne asked, turning slowly, the beam of his lantern cutting arcs through the dark.

“Close,” said the Voice. “Always close.” A sudden draft made the lantern flame flicker and dance. “I am in need of your assistance, my dear colleague. I find myself in a rather… uncomfortable position.”

“I can imagine,” Thorne said, his voice tight. “What do you want from me, Alistair?”

“Civilisation,” the Voice said, and there was a strange, raw hunger in the word. “I want food. I want wine. I want a fire. Thorne stared into the darkness where the voice seemed to originate. “And if I refuse?”

Thorne’s mind reeled. His brandy? He looked back down the staircase, towards the town, towards his locked study. He had been there all along. Watching him. The realisation was more terrifying than the revolver in his hand. He was not a visitor. He was already a prisoner.

“The larder is through the kitchens, at the back of the house,” said the Voice, its tone now calm, commanding. “And do bring a glass. I am a man, not an animal. Despite all evidence to the contrary.”

Thorne did not hesitate, or rather, his legs obeyed the command before his mind had finished its frantic protest. He turned, the lantern held low now, and descended the grand staircase, each footfall a soft clap in the echoing void. The chill of the great hall seemed to have intensified, a damp, grave-like cold that had nothing to do with the night outside. He was not a visitor, he was a prisoner. The thought was a block of ice in his gut. His brandy. The Invisible Man had been in his own study, watching him, perhaps for hours, a silent, unseen audience to his private anxieties.

The knife lifted. It sawed awkwardly at the ham, then at the bread. A crude sandwich was assembled in mid-air. Thorne stared, his scientific mind rebelling, searching for an explanation, a trick of the light, a hallucination brought on by stress. But the evidence was irrefutable. The food was disappearing. The whiskey level in the glass was lowering.

“You are wondering how,” said the Voice, its mouth clearly full. “Of course you are. You, of all people. Always so damned logical.”

“It’s impossible,” Thorne breathed. “A body, all its tissues, its pigments, its blood… to make it transparent… the refractive index would have to be altered to match that of air perfectly. Every single cell. It is the work of a madman.”

“A genius!” the Voice snapped, and a crust of bread fell to the floor. “It is not madness, it is physics! Pure, glorious physics, taken to its absolute conclusion. Do you think I did not consider the pigments? The haemoglobin? That was the very devil of the problem. Not just to bleach them, but to change their very properties, to make them cease to refract or reflect light at all. It took me four years, Thorne. Four years in a filthy little room in London, poisoning myself with radiation, fiddling with reagents that would strip the skin from a lesser man’s hands. It is a process, you fool, a chemical process! I found a way to lower the refractive index of every tissue in the body, not to that of air, but to a state of absolute non-interaction with the visible spectrum. I do not bend light around myself. Light passes _through_ me. As if I were a vacuum.”

A second sandwich was being assembled. The ham was devoured with animal-like speed.

“But your blood,” Thorne said, seizing on the detail from before. “You said it becomes visible.”

“Ah, yes. A flaw. A damnable, irritating flaw,” said Alistair. “The process only works on living tissue. The moment blood leaves the living vessel, the moment it begins to coagulate and die, it reverts. It becomes plain, red, visible blood. A scraped knuckle, a shaving cut… it becomes a beacon. And this…” a sound of pain, a sharp intake of breath. “This is rather more than a scraped knuckle. I was shot, Thorne.”

“Shot?” The word was a cold shock.

“In the arm. A blasted gamekeeper. I was… acquiring some necessary equipment from a private laboratory out near Wendover. The fool had a shotgun. Pellets. It tore the flesh. I’ve been bleeding for hours, staining everything I touch. That is why I need you. I need a surgeon. I need a confederate.”

Thorne’s mind spun. A thief. A housebreaker. This was not some noble, misguided experiment. It was squalid criminality. “You want me to treat a gunshot wound? On a man I cannot see?”

“Precisely!” The whiskey glass was drained and then slammed down on the table with a crack. “You are a man of science. A man of steady nerves. You will manage. But first, more substantial comforts. This house must have clothing. A dressing-gown, perhaps? The night air is murderously cold when one is… unadorned.”

Thorne stood frozen. He was being asked to be a valet, a cook, a doctor, an accomplice to a fugitive. The sheer audacity of it was breathtaking.

“Do you not hear me, Thorne?” The Voice was rising again, losing its momentary satisfaction, an edge of menace creeping back in. “I am not asking. I am in a position to insist. Your policeman friend is likely still wondering what invisible force sent him tumbling down your front steps. I assure you, my methods can become far more direct.”

A chair from the corner of the landing scraped across the floor and came to rest near the table. It creaked as an unseen weight settled into it. “Now, find me a dressing-gown. And for God’s sake, see if there is a cigar box anywhere. A man cannot think without a proper smoke.”

The absurdity of the request, piled upon the terror of the situation, almost made Thorne laugh. A hysterical giggle bubbled in his throat, but he choked it down. He turned numbly and walked towards a large mahogany wardrobe that stood against the far wall. Its doors were stiff. He pulled, and they opened with a groan of ancient wood, releasing a faint scent of cedar and mothballs. Inside hung a few threadbare suits and, at the very end, a heavy woollen dressing-gown in a dark, claret red.

He took it from the hanger. “This?” he asked, holding it out.

The garment was taken from his hands. It hung in the air for a disorienting second, a limp form without substance, then it began to move. The arms filled out, the shoulders broadened. It buttoned itself up the front, the small toggles twisting and settling into their loops. The robed figure, its form now horribly solid, its head and hands still a complete void, sat back down in the chair. It was a grotesque parody of a man, a headless, handless creature from a nightmare, draped in cheap finery.

“Better,” said the Voice from the centre of the robe. “Much better. Now. The cigars.”

Thorne searched the sideboard in the dining room again, his hands shaking. He found a wooden box containing a handful of dry, brittle cigars. He also found a box of matches. He brought them upstairs.

A spectral hand met his and took the offering. Thorne recoiled from the touch, a brief contact of cold, firm fingers. He watched, morbidly fascinated, as a cigar was placed into the air where a mouth should be. He heard a biting, snipping sound. A match was struck, the flare of it briefly illuminating nothing at all before it was brought to the tip of the cigar.

The end glowed red. And then the true horror began. As Alistair drew on the cigar, the smoke did not simply pour from an invisible mouth. It was drawn into a ghostly, swirling shape, a temporary, three-dimensional sketch of a pharynx and lungs. Thorne saw the smoke trace the path down a throat, bifurcate, and cloudily expand into two great lobes, a nebulous ghost of anatomy hanging within the empty dressing-gown. Then, on the exhale, a thick plume of smoke billowed out from the void, creating a momentary, churning vortex where a head should be.

“A blessed habit,” said the Voice, its tone thick with satisfaction. The whirling smoke-cast of his mouth and throat became visible with every puff. “One of the few simple pleasures left to me. Now, Thorne, we must talk. We have great things to do, you and I.”

“We?” Thorne said, finding his voice at last. It was thin and reedy.

“Of course, we. Did you think I blundered into your life by accident? I chose you, Thorne. I have followed your work on optical densities for years. He did not look at the dressing gown in the chair, but his senses were stretched to their utmost, trying to locate the man within it. He was acutely aware of the faint creak of the wicker, the sound of breathing, the almost imperceptible displacement of air. He took a sip of the spirit; it burned his throat, a welcome, grounding sensation in a world that had become unhinged.

“The beginning of what?” he asked, his voice flat. He moved to the mantelpiece and leaned against it, putting a few feet of distance between himself and the occupied chair. “Of this madness?”

“Of a new age for man,” said the Invisible Man. His voice had lost some of its frantic edge, replaced now by a tone of professorial authority, the sound of a lecturer before his prize student. The tumbler of whiskey he held tilted again. “Of a power that will remake the world. And you, Kemp, you are the first to be brought into the secret. The very first. You should feel honoured.”

“Honoured,” Kemp repeated, without inflection. He stared into his glass. “Tell me, Griffin. If I am to be your… confederate. How is it done? You spoke of a process. My mind rejects it. It’s a physical impossibility. A body’s refractive index cannot be made equal to that of air. The pigments, the blood…”

“Ah, the pigments!” The voice was animated, almost joyful. “That is the crux of it! You think in terms of chemistry, Kemp, and that is right, so far as it goes. But you do not think of physics. Of light. What is visibility? It is the absorption and reflection of light. If a body neither absorbs nor reflects light, it is invisible. Simple, is it not?”

“Profoundly complex,” said Kemp.

“Only in the application! I found a way. Four years of my life, Kemp, working in a filthy London lodging house, starving half the time, running from my landlord. I found the reagents. I found a method to bleach the corpuscles of the blood, to drive the pigment from the body, and most importantly, to lower the refractive index of every tissue, every cell, every scrap of protein and fat, until it was precisely that of the air. It required a radiator, a current of my own devising. A vibration in the ether, you might say, that catalyzed the change.”

A log in the grate, not quite extinguished, popped softly, and Kemp started.

“Keep your nerve,” said the Voice, with a hint of amusement. “I began with a scrap of wool. I placed it in the field of the radiators, injected it with the serum. And it vanished. Gone! I could feel it, a little tuft of nothing, but my eyes saw only the wall behind it. It was the most triumphant moment of my life.”

The dressing gown shifted. “Then I tried something more complex. A cat.”

Kemp looked up sharply. “A cat?”

“My landlord’s cat. A stray she had taken in. A wretched, scrawny thing. It was a perfect subject. I chloroformed it, strapped it into the apparatus. It was more difficult; the process is agonizing, you see. The living tissue fights it. The cat woke halfway through. It screamed. A horrible sound, as if its very soul were being flayed from its body. Its fur vanished first, then its skin. I could see the muscles twitching, the heart beating, a frantic, visible pump in a body of shimmering glass. Then that too faded. And it was gone.”

“My God,” whispered Kemp.

“Not quite gone,” Griffin continued, his voice meditative. “A small detail I had not anticipated. The tapetum lucidum. The reflective layer in the back of its eyes. It would not change. And the iris pigment. So there it was. An invisible cat, but for two faint, spectral discs of green fire that floated in the air, and the pinkish membranes of its eyelids. It escaped, of course. For days I heard tales in the neighbourhood of a phantom. Two glowing eyes that appeared in bedrooms at night. Children screaming. I believe they stoned it to death eventually, in a brickyard. They saw the eyes hovering, you see, and threw bricks until the screaming stopped and the eyes fell.”

Kemp felt a wave of nausea. He was a vivisectionist, in a manner of speaking; he was a man of science who understood the necessity of experiment. But this was a different order of thing entirely. This was not discovery; it was monstrous cruelty, casual and unfeeling.

“You killed it,” he said.

“I was proving a principle!” the Voice snapped, the professorial tone vanishing. “Do you think I had time for sentiment? I was on the brink of the greatest discovery in the history of the world! I was hounded by creditors, my landlord was threatening to throw me out, my professor, the one who stole my initial research, was sniffing around. I had no choice but to proceed to the final stage.”

“Yourself,” said Kemp, his mouth dry.

“Myself,” said Griffin. “I locked my door. I had sold my last few possessions for the chemicals. I had enough for one trial, one subject. The pain, Kemp… you cannot imagine. It is a fire that burns you from the inside out, a fire that consumes your very substance. I felt my bones turn to crystal, my flesh to clear jelly. I writhed on the floor, biting my knuckles to keep from screaming and bringing the whole house down on me. And the cold! An unbearable, penetrating cold, as if I had been plunged into the abyss. It is the price of transparency. The body can no longer hold its own heat. It radiates away instantly.”

The disembodied voice seemed to fill the room, a chilling testament to an ordeal Kemp’s mind struggled to picture. He saw, in his imagination, a man writhing and twisting, fading like a watercolour in the rain.

“When it was over,” the Voice went on, more quietly now, “I was… as I am now. I stood up. I looked in the mirror and saw nothing but the grimy wallpaper of my room behind me. I was free. I was a god. I laughed. I stood there, naked and freezing and utterly triumphant, and I laughed. And then the old woman, my landlord, began hammering on the door, smelling the chemicals, shouting about arrears and damages. And in my moment of victory, my moment of divine power, she set the house on fire.”

“Fire?”

“With a paraffin lamp. The fool. She thought to smoke me out, I suppose. The whole place went up like a tinderbox. All my notes, my apparatus, my formula, the means to reverse the process. All of it, gone. I barely escaped with my life, a naked, invisible man running through the burning hallways, leaving no footprints in the soot. I was out on the streets of London, with nothing. Less than nothing.”

The whiskey glass was set down on the table with a sharp click. The story, Kemp realised, was no longer a scientific lecture. It was a confession. A justification. A prelude to something worse.

“So you came here,” Kemp said, trying to piece it all together. “To Iping.”

“Not at first. I blundered about. I stole clothes from a theatrical supply shop, an absurd costume, bandages, a false nose. I made my way to Iping to find a quiet place to work. To try and reconstruct my notes. I took a room at the inn, the Coach and Horses. I had three books, you see. My notebooks. All my calculations, my initial formulae, written in a cipher of my own devising. They are the key. The only record of how to make the process work, how to control it, how to reverse it.”

“And you lost them,” Kemp stated.

“They were stolen!” the Voice snarled, and the chair creaked violently as the Unseen man surged to his feet. The scarlet dressing gown rose, its empty arms gesturing wildly. “Taken from my room by the fools at the inn! The publican, the doctor, the whole village of gaping idiots! I had to flee. They hounded me, shot at me. Look at this!”

A bandaged hand appeared, wrenching at the collar of the robe. Kemp saw the raw, puckered flesh of a gunshot wound on the shoulder, a ghastly, visible island on an unseen continent. The blood on the bandage he had first seen was from this.

“This is what they did to me. To me! For the crime of being brilliant. For the crime of being different. I ran, I hid in the bracken, I was freezing, starving, bleeding. And then I remembered you, Kemp. A fellow man of science. Living here, in Port Burdock. A man who would understand. Who would help. It was my first piece of luck in an age.”

The dressing gown sat down again. The fury seemed to have passed, leaving a chilling calm.

“Those books are everything, Kemp. With them, I can perfect the process. I can learn to reverse it, should I choose. But more importantly, I can replicate it. Think of it. An invisible army. A single invisible man can terrorize a city. What could a dozen do? Or a hundred? We could walk into any bank, any treasury, any council chamber. We could take what we want, kill who we want. There would be no defence. No escape. It would be a new world order, Kemp. My world order.”

Kemp stared, horrified. The last vestige of scientific curiosity died within him, replaced by a cold, profound dread. This was not a scientist seeking aid. This was a madman seeking an accomplice for world domination. The story of his suffering was not a plea for sympathy, but a recruiting speech. He thought of the terrified villagers, the stoned cat, the burning house. They were not unfortunate accidents; they were the first casualties of a war Griffin had already declared on the whole of humanity.

“You’re insane,” Kemp said, his voice barely a whisper.

“I am sane!” the voice thundered, and Kemp flinched as if struck. “It is the world that is insane! The world that lets genius starve while fools prosper! I am simply taking what is owed to me. And you will help me. You have no choice.”

“What do you want from me?” Kemp asked, his mind racing. He had to play for time. He had to think.

“For now, shelter. Food. A place to rest and heal. You are my visible partner, Kemp. My face to the world. You will go about your day as normal. And tomorrow, you will help me get my books back from Iping. They are at the inn. The Coach and Horses. We need them.”

“How?”

“You will contrive a reason to go there. A medical visit, perhaps. You will get into that room and you will retrieve them for me. Three small, leather-bound notebooks. You will bring them back here. And then, our real work can begin.”

The voice was filled with a terrible, eager glee. Kemp looked at the empty space above the chair, where a man’s head should be, and felt a terror more profound than any simple fear of violence. It was the terror of being shackled to a destructive idea, a force of brilliant, focused insanity. He thought of his quiet, orderly life, his research, his comfortable home. It was all gone. He was in a cage with a tiger he could not see.

“And if I refuse?” Kemp asked, though he knew the answer.

The air in front of him seemed to chill. The dressing gown was perfectly still.

“I am a reasonable man, Kemp,” the Voice said, softly, and the softness was more frightening than the shouting. “But I am also a desperate one. I am here, in your house. I can go anywhere. I could follow you to the ends of the earth. If you betray me, I will kill you. That is a simple fact. But it will not be a simple death. I will have all the time in the world to make it interesting. I could hunt you for days. I could remove you from the world piece by piece. A finger one day. An ear the next. I could whisper in your ear as you sleep. I could turn every shadow into a torment. You would go mad long before you died. Do you understand me?”

Kemp’s blood ran cold. He did. He understood perfectly. He was not being asked for help; he was being enslaved.

He swallowed. “I understand,” he said, the words tasting like ash.

“Good.” The tone was businesslike again. “Then it is settled. You are my partner. Now, I am exhausted. This wound… it is draining me. I must sleep. I will take your bedroom. You will sleep in the spare room. Do not try anything foolish, Kemp. Remember, I sleep very lightly. And I do not need to see to find you in the dark.”

The dressing gown rose from the chair. Kemp watched, mesmerised, as it walked across the room towards his bedroom door. It moved with the confident stride of a man who owned the very ground he walked on. The door opened, the robed figure passed through, and the door clicked shut behind it.

Kemp stood frozen by the mantelpiece for a full minute, the whiskey glass clutched in his hand. The silence in the study was immense, but it was not empty. The house was still occupied. He was a prisoner in his own home. He looked at the chair where the creature had sat, the tumbler of whiskey still on the small table beside it, a tangible remnant of an impossible conversation.

The Reign of Terror. An invisible army. The words echoed in his skull. This was not just a matter of his own survival anymore. Griffin was not a mere criminal; he was a potential plague, a new and terrible power about to be unleashed upon an unsuspecting world. The police could not fight him. Armies could not stop him.

But Kemp could.

The thought came to him with absolute clarity, cutting through the fog of fear. Griffin had a weakness. An Achilles’ heel. He was brilliant, but he was also arrogant and overconfident. He trusted Kemp, or at least, he trusted Kemp’s fear. And he needed his books. Without those three little notebooks, his grand plan was nothing but a madman’s rant. The key to his power was also the key to his undoing.

Kemp’s resolve hardened. He would play the part. He would be the cowed, obedient partner. He would go to Iping. He would get the books. And then, he would not bring them back. He would destroy them. He would find a way to trap Griffin, to stop him, to neutralize this monstrous threat before it could ever begin.

He was a man of science. He believed in reason, in order, in the predictable laws of nature. Griffin was an aberration, a violation of that order. It was his duty, not just as a citizen but as a scientist, to correct this anomaly.

He walked silently to the door of his study and looked down the hall towards his closed bedroom door. Behind it slept a monster. A monster who thought he had found a pawn. He was wrong. He had found an enemy. Kemp put down his glass, his mind now cold and analytical. The first move had been made. The next move was his. The war had begun.
