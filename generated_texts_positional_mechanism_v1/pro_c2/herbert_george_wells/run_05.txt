Professor Alistair Finch had the distinct impression that the ticking of his grandfather clock had stuttered. But Professor Finch was a man who trusted pendulums. He stood staring at the polished mahogany case. Was that a genuine skip in the rhythm? He listened again, but heard nothing further than the steady, sonorous tick-tock. Then he distinctly saw a book, left near the edge of his desk, slide to the floor. All men, however dedicated to the sciences, retain some primal unease. The feeling one might call “unsettling” came upon him. He closed the door to the hallway, came forward to his great oak desk, and set down his spectacles. Suddenly, with a jolt, he perceived a single, dusty sunbeam from the window shuddering in mid-air, its motes of dust dancing in a frantic, repeating loop. He stared at this in confusion. It was an ordinary ray of light, a simple phenomenon, but it was behaving impossibly. He would have waved a hand through it, but a touch on his shoulder arrested him, and a voice speaking quite close to his ear. “Professor?” said the Voice.

“Ah!” said Finch, turning sharply. “Who’s there?”

“Keep your nerve,” said the Voice. “I’m a Desynchronised Man.”

Finch made no answer for a moment, simply stared at the space where the voice had come from. “Desynchronised Man,” he said. “I am a Desynchronised Man,” repeated the Voice. The absurd papers on temporal paradox he had been marking that very afternoon rushed through Finch’s brain. He does not appear to have been either panicked or particularly frightened at that moment. The absurdity came first. “I thought that was all theoretical nonsense,” he said. The thought uppermost in his mind was a student’s flawed equation. “Are you shimmering?” he asked.

“Yes,” said the Desynchronised Man. “Oh!” said Finch, and then shook his head. “I say!” he said. “But this is preposterous. It’s a parlour trick.” He stepped forward suddenly, and his hand, extended towards the shimmering patch of air, met a cold wrist. He recoiled at the contact, and his face paled.

“Keep steady, Finch, for mercy’s sake! I need your help. Stop!” The hand gripped his own. He tried to strike at it.

“Professor!” cried the Voice. “Finch! Keep steady!” and the grip became solid, unwavering. A frantic desire to be free of this wrongness took possession of Finch. He opened his mouth to shout, and a copy of the Philosophical Transactions was thrust before his face. He dredged his memory, sifting through long-forgotten debates in dusty lecture halls. A face came to him; pale, intense, with eyes that burned with a furious, impatient intelligence.

A blinding white light, a deafening silence. And then, I was standing in my laboratory, and Thorne was screaming. He was not screaming at me. He was screaming at an empty space where I had been.”
The disembodied voice was heavy with the memory of it. “He couldn’t see me. He couldn’t hear me, not properly. My voice was a whisper from nowhere. He ran. Bolted like a frightened rabbit. I was alone. And… different. I looked at my hands, and I could see the workbench through them. A faint, shimmering outline was all that remained. I was there, and I was not. I was desynchronised.”
Finch rubbed his temples, his mind straining to hold the concepts. “So you are not invisible. You are… out of phase.”
“Precisely! A man of science, after all. My body is not in this precise moment with you. It flickers, a quantum of a second ahead or behind. Light passes mostly through me. Sound waves struggle to find purchase. When I try to speak, my words emerge slightly before or after I think them, a faint echo. The effort of holding myself together, of remaining coherent enough to interact with the world, is exhausting. It makes me solid, in a way. The more I concentrate, the more I exist. But it drains me. As if to prove his point, the tweed jacket seemed to flicker, the whole shape of it wavering violently for a second. The glass of brandy on the table beside it rattled, and the distorted patch of light on the wall pulsed like a dying heart.
“Steady, man!” said Finch, half rising from his stool.
“It’s the exhaustion,” the Voice came again, weaker this time. “Two days. Hiding in sheds and alleyways. Stealing scraps. The cold… the cold is the worst. It seeps into you from a place that isn’t here. And the world… it’s become a nightmare. People walk through me. Carriages drive over me. Finch was silent for a long time. The room was thick with the smell of pipe smoke, brandy, and the ozone-like scent of a broken law of nature. He looked at the clothes sitting in his chair, a grotesque effigy animated by a desperate, brilliant, and utterly reckless man. He was a scientist. His whole life was dedicated to the rational, the observable, the provable. And here, in his study, was the ultimate irrationality, demanding to be observed, begging to be proven.
“How?” Finch said at last, his voice a croak. “How can I possibly help you? Your laboratory is gone, your equipment seized.”
“No!” the voice was suddenly sharp, regaining its strength. “Thorne wouldn’t have let them take it. He’s not that stupid. He would have moved it. Hidden it. He would be trying to understand it himself. He knows what it can do. He knows what _I_ am. He is the only other person on Earth who knows I exist.” A shimmering hand clenched into a fist. “We need my notes. We need my equipment. And for that, we need to find Thorne.”
“We?” said Finch, a cold dread creeping up his spine. “There is no ‘we’, Croft. This is your folly. I am a victim of circumstance, an unwilling host to a madman’s tale.”
“Are you?” The voice was soft, menacing. “You are the man who wrote the theory. I am the man who provided the proof. We are linked, Finch, whether you like it or not. And you will help me. You will give me shelter. You will give me your resources. Because if you do not, what choice do I have? A man who can walk through walls, who can stand unseen in a locked room… he can be very persuasive. I do not wish to be your enemy, Finch. Finch looked at the door. He thought of the police constable, of the shotgun blast in the night. He thought of the headlines about Croft’s disappearance, the hints of scandal and theft. He felt like a man in a dream who knows he is dreaming but cannot wake up.
“What do you want me to do?” he asked, his shoulders slumping in defeat.

Finch nodded slowly. He felt trapped, a fly in a web woven from threads of theoretical physics and mad ambition. He walked to his pantry and returned with a blanket. “Will this… help?” he asked, holding it out.

“Finch,” the voice murmured, thick with a strange, unnatural drowsiness.
“I am here,” Finch said, standing helplessly in the middle of his own room.

His own paper. He remembered writing it, the thrill of the intellectual chase. ‘On the Potential Granularity of Spatio-Temporal Existence’, he had called it. “That was poorly handled,” said Croft’s voice, now directly behind him. Finch spun around, a cry stifled in his throat. There was nothing there. “She is a complication we did not need.”

“What was I to do?” Finch whispered, his nerves screaming. “What would you have me do? I cannot keep this up, Croft. I cannot live like this, a prisoner in my own home, lying to everyone I know!”

“You will do what is necessary,” Croft replied, his voice devoid of sympathy. “As I have. Now. Thorne’s estate is near Burwash. We will need a map. We will need to know the layout of the house. He keeps the laboratory in the old west wing. I need you to procure a vehicle. We will go tonight.”

“Go?” Finch laughed, a brittle, broken sound. “I am not going anywhere. This is your madness, not mine.”

“Is it?” The air grew cold again. A book on a nearby hall table lifted an inch into the air, hovered for a moment, and then slammed back down with a crack. “Our nosy housekeeper has already started the clock, Finch. Soon there will be more visitors. The constable, perhaps, following up on her concerns. How long do you think your story about a London doctor will last? You are implicated. Your only path to freedom is through me. Get me what I need, and I will be gone from your life. Fail me… and I will make you a ghost as well.”

Finch stared at the space where the voice originated. He saw not an empty hall, but the ruin of his life. He saw a future of whispers and suspicion, of locked rooms and terrified lies. Or he saw a future of collaboration with a monster of his own intellectual making. There was no good choice. There was only a choice between two different kinds of damnation.

“A map,” he said, his voice a dead monotone. “There are ordnance surveys in the study.”

“Excellent,” said the Desynchronised Man. “You see, Finch? We are making a plan.”

Finch did not reply. He turned and walked, like a man in a trance, back towards the study, back towards the source of his undoing. As he passed the spot where Croft had spoken, a tweed-clad arm, semi-translucent and shimmering at the edges like a heat haze, solidified for a fraction of a second and rested a heavy, chilling hand on his shoulder.

“We will do great things yet, you and I,” the voice murmured in his ear. The hand dissolved. Finch shuddered, but he did not stop walking. He had made his choice. Or rather, the choice had been made for him.

had a choice. He did not look back. To look back was to acknowledge the phantom at his heels; to admit that the solid world of fact and reason he had inhabited his entire life had fractured, and a monster had slipped through the cracks. The study was his sanctuary; a place of leather-bound reason and quiet contemplation. Now, entering it felt like a profanation. The familiar scent of old paper and pipe tobacco was curdled by the faint, chilling presence that followed him.

“The ordnance surveys,” said the Voice, which seemed to emanate from a point near the large terrestrial globe. “You keep them in the bottom drawer of the mahogany cabinet. The one for East Sussex. Quickly now.”

Finch moved with the stiff, jerky motions of an automaton. He knelt, his knees cracking in the silence, and pulled open the drawer. There they were; neatly folded maps, creased with the memory of past academic excursions and country walks. He thought of a time he had taken this very map, the one for Burwash and the surrounding Weald, on a bird-watching trip. The memory was so clear, so wholesome, it felt like a report from another man’s life. He pulled it out, his fingers trembling.

“Spread it on the desk,” Croft commanded.

Finch obeyed, unfolding the map beneath the green-shaded glow of his desk lamp. The familiar cartography, the contour lines and symbols for churches and public houses, looked alien and sinister. It was no longer a map of a place, but a blueprint for a crime.

“There,” said the Voice, and Finch felt a sudden drop in temperature at his shoulder. He flinched away. “Thorne’s estate. It is called ‘The Grange’. Just north of the village, see? Set back from the road by a long drive. Isolated. Perfect.” A finger, vague and watery like a distortion in glass, traced a line on the paper. It left no mark, but Finch could feel the unnatural cold of its passage. “He is a fool, but a meticulous one. He documents everything. His laboratory is here, in the west wing. Ground floor. A converted conservatory, all glass and steel frame. An easy entry.”

“I am a doctor, Croft, not a burglar,” Finch said, his voice a low tremor.

“You are a man who wishes to survive the night,” Croft retorted. “And the nights to follow. Your profession is irrelevant. Your compliance is not. The laboratory is our only concern. He keeps his notes there. And the apparatus. The core of it, at least.”

“What is this apparatus?” Finch asked, staring at the map as if it might offer an escape. “What have you done?”

A dry, rattling sound that might have been a laugh echoed in the room. “Done? I have succeeded where Thorne and all the other fools at the Royal Society failed. They spoke of the ether, of the fourth dimension, as theoretical constructs. I made them practical.” The Voice grew lower, more intense. “It was not about light, Finch, not about pigments or refraction, as that madman Griffin apparently attempted. It was about time. About the fundamental frequency at which matter exists. I built a device, a resonator, that could alter that frequency. Desynchronise it, you see? Shift a body’s temporal signature just slightly out of phase with the local timeline.”

Finch stared at the empty space where the voice originated. “That’s… that’s impossible. It violates every known law of physics.”

“Then the laws are incomplete!” the Voice snapped. “I am the proof. I am standing right here. Or rather, not quite right here. I am a half-second behind, or ahead, I am not entirely certain which. The world perceives me as a shimmer, a heat haze. Sounds I make are slightly displaced. My own senses are… confused. It is a constant, maddening vertigo. Thorne called my theories the work of a charlatan. He had me thrown out of the society. He stole my research. And now he is attempting to replicate it. I need my notes back, Finch. I need the focusing lens from his machine before he destroys himself and half of Sussex with him.”

Frost crept up the glass of the desk lamp. Finch watched it, fascinated and appalled. “And this helps you? Robbing him?”

“It is the only way,” Croft said, his tone flat and final. “I cannot manipulate objects with any real precision. My touch is… unreliable. A ghost in my own machine. I can exert force, yes, but fine motor skills are beyond me. I need your hands. Your solidity. Now. The car. You have a motor car in the garage, a Rover if I recall. Is it fuelled?”

“I… I think so. Yes.”

“We will take it. Go now. Get your coat. And bring a flask of brandy. The night air is murderously cold when one is not entirely corporeal.”

Finch rose from the desk. He felt a strange sense of detachment, as if he were watching himself in a grim play. He went to the hall, took his heavy tweed coat from its peg. His hand brushed against his wife’s summer hat, which still hung there. A wave of grief and loss so profound it was physically painful washed over him. He leaned his head against the wall, his eyes shut tight.

“Do not break, Finch,” the Voice whispered, right beside his ear. “There will be time for sentiment later. After we have succeeded.”

The journey to the garage was a nightmare of small terrors. Every creak of a floorboard, every rustle of the wind in the ivy outside, sounded like an accusation. The garage was cold and smelled of petrol and damp earth. The Rover stood silent and bulky in the gloom. Finch opened the driver’s side door, its squeal loud in the stillness, and slid into the seat. The leather was cold and stiff.

“Check the petrol,” Croft commanded from the passenger seat. The seat cushions did not even depress.

Finch fumbled with the keys, turned the ignition. The engine coughed, sputtered, and then roared to life, the sound a monstrous violation of the night’s peace. The gauge needle climbed to just over half a tank. “It will be enough to get there. But not enough to return.”

“One crisis at a time, Doctor,” said the Voice. “There is a filling station on the Lewes road. We will stop there. Drive. And do not draw attention to yourself.”

Driving the car felt deeply strange. Finch was acutely aware of the empty space beside him, a void that spoke and watched and judged. He imagined what anyone would see: a respectable doctor, out for a late drive. They would not see the gun to his head, the spectral passenger that held his life, his reputation, his entire world, hostage. The beams of the headlamps cut a swathe through the inky blackness of the country lanes.

“You are driving too slowly,” Croft said. “Are you trying to get us caught?”

“I am trying not to crash into a ditch,” Finch snapped, his own voice sounding shrill. He gripped the wheel tighter. He felt he was going mad. Perhaps he already was. Perhaps Croft was a figment, a complex hallucination brought on by stress and overwork. But the cold was real. The disembodied voice was real. The semi-translucent hand that had rested on his shoulder was horrifyingly real.

They reached the main road. The world felt larger here, more populated, more dangerous. The occasional headlamp of an oncoming vehicle felt like a searchlight. After ten minutes, the lonely, electric glow of a petrol station appeared ahead.

“Pull in,” Croft ordered. “Fill the tank. And Finch… compose yourself. You look like a man on his way to the gallows.”

Finch pulled the Rover up beside the pumps. A single attendant, a lad of about twenty in greasy overalls, came out of the small, brightly lit office, wiping his hands on a rag. Finch’s heart hammered against his ribs.

“Evening, sir,” the boy said, peering at him. “A cold one.”

“It is,” Finch managed to say, his throat dry. “Fill it, please.”

He got out of the car, trying to appear casual, his hands shoved deep into his coat pockets. He could feel Croft’s presence, a spot of intense cold, lingering near the passenger door. He prayed the boy would not notice. As the petrol gurgled into the tank, another vehicle pulled in; a police motorcycle.

Finch’s blood ran cold. The constable, a stout, moustached man named Parris, dismounted and walked towards him. Finch knew him. He had treated the man’s wife for bronchitis last winter.

“Doctor Finch,” Parris said, his tone friendly but his eyes sharp. “Burning the midnight oil, are we?”

“Constable,” Finch said, forcing a nod. “Just… a late errand. A patient on the other side of Lewes.” The lie came easily, slick with practice from his encounter with Mrs. Gable.

“Ah,” Parris said, nodding. He looked past Finch, at the car. “Everything alright at the house? Mrs. Gable stopped me in the village this afternoon. Seemed a bit… agitated. Mentioned you had a visitor from London, a specialist? Said she was worried. Heard shouting.”

Finch’s mind raced. He could feel the invisible pressure from the car, a silent, urgent command to hold fast. “Ah, yes. My colleague. Doctor… Smith.” Another lie, bland and unmemorable. “He is a brilliant man, but eccentric. Terribly loud. We had a… a vigorous professional disagreement. Nothing more. I do apologise if it disturbed her.”

Parris continued to scrutinize him. “She seemed to think you were unwell, Doctor. Under some strain.”

“The pressures of the job, Constable. You know how it is.” Finch forced a thin smile. “And Smith’s visit has been rather taxing. He has his own ideas, you see.”

“Is he with you now?” Parris asked, glancing at the passenger seat.

The question hung in the air. Finch’s heart stopped. “No,” he said, the word coming out as a croak. He cleared his throat. “No, he stayed behind. He is… resting.”

“Rest, Finch,” the Voice whispered, a thread of sound only he could hear. “Let him rest in pieces.”

Parris seemed to accept the answer. The petrol pump clicked off. The attendant screwed the cap back on. “That will be ten shillings and sixpence, sir.”

Finch fumbled for his wallet, his fingers clumsy with adrenaline. He paid the boy, his hand shaking so badly he dropped a coin. He bent to retrieve it, his face hot with shame.

“Well, drive safe, Doctor,” Parris said, turning back to his motorcycle. “The roads are treacherous.”

“I will, Constable. Good night.”

Finch got back into the car, his whole body trembling. He did not dare look at the empty seat beside him. He started the engine and pulled away from the station far too quickly, the tires squealing on the tarmac.

“That was close,” Croft’s voice said, laced with a cold amusement. “The nosy housekeeper and the village policeman. You are weaving a tangled web, Doctor. See that you do not get caught in it. Now drive. Thorne’s estate is less than five miles from here.”

The rest of the drive passed in a tense silence. Finch’s earlier fantasy that this was all a hallucination had evaporated, replaced by a grim, bone-deep certainty. He was an accomplice. He was driving a monster to commit a crime. The sign for The Grange appeared, its white letters barely visible in the dark. Finch turned the Rover onto a long, gravel drive flanked by tall, skeletal trees. He cut the engine and the headlamps, plunging them into absolute blackness. The main house was a vast, dark shape against the slightly less dark sky. Not a single light shone from any window.

“Excellent,” Croft murmured. “He is away. Or asleep. It makes no difference.” A faint shimmer distorted the air in the passenger seat as the unseen presence moved. The door clicked open. “Come. The laboratory is around the back.”

Finch got out. The air was bitingly cold, and a low mist was coiling around the bases of the trees. The gravel crunched loudly under his feet.

“Quietly, you fool!” Croft hissed. “Walk on the grass.”

They crept around the side of the immense house. The west wing was, as Croft had said, a large conservatory, a structure of iron and glass bolted onto the older stone building. Inside, strange shapes and the glint of glassware were faintly visible.

“The lock on the side door is a simple tumbler,” the Voice instructed from beside the door. “I have observed him. He is lazy with his security. You have your medical bag?”

“Yes, in the car.”

“Fetch it. You will have a probe or a letter opener. Something thin and stiff.”

Finch’s heart sank. “You want me to pick the lock?”

“I want my notes. Your method of acquiring them for me is of no consequence. Move yourself, man!”

Feeling utterly defeated, Finch retrieved his bag from the car. He found a long, thin steel probe he used for examining wounds. He returned to the conservatory door, his breath pluming in the cold. His hands shook so violently he could barely insert the probe into the lock.

“Steady,” Croft’s voice commanded. “Jiggle it upwards. Listen for the click.”

Finch did as he was told. He was no expert, but the lock was, as Croft had promised, pathetically simple. After a few tense seconds, there was a soft *snick*. The handle turned. He pushed the door open, and they were in.

The air inside was thick with the smell of ozone and strange chemicals, an acrid perfume that reminded Finch of his own university days. Moonlight filtered through the glass roof, illuminating a scene of extraordinary scientific clutter. Wires snaked across the floor like vines. Glass beakers and retorts in all shapes and sizes sat on benches. At the centre of the room stood a massive, complex machine of brass coils, vacuum tubes, and crystalline rods, all aimed at a single point on a steel platform.

“The resonator,” Croft whispered, his voice filled with a kind of reverent awe. “My beautiful, terrible machine. He has copied it almost perfectly.”

“Where are the notes?” Finch asked, wanting only to be done with this.

“The cabinet. Over there. The flat oak one.”

Finch moved towards it. He tried the handle. Locked. “It is locked, Croft.”

“Of course it is locked. Break it.”

“There,” Croft said, a new urgency in his tone. “The ones with my initials. C.E.T.C. Get them all.”

Finch pulled out a thick stack of the books, maybe a dozen in all, and clutched them to his chest. “I have them. Can we go now?”

“One more thing,” the Voice said. It had moved towards the central machine. “The focusing lens. It is the heart of the device. Without it, his copy is useless. I cannot allow him to continue.”

The air around the central apparatus shimmered violently. A ghostly, translucent hand reached for a large crystal set in a brass housing. The hand flickered, solidified for a moment, and then seemed to pass right through the crystal.

“Curse this state!” Croft roared in frustration. “I cannot get a grip on it! Finch, you must do it. Unscrew the housing and take the lens.”

As he twisted the last one, it slipped from his grasp and fell to the tiled floor with a loud clatter.

“Someone is awake,” Finch whispered, his voice strangled.

A door slammed open somewhere inside the house. A man’s voice shouted, “Who is down there? I am armed!”

“Now you have done it,” Finch breathed, staring in horror at the lit window.

“We are not leaving empty-handed,” the Voice of Croft hissed back, no longer a whisper but a venomous snarl. The air in the laboratory grew impossibly cold. “Get the lens, Finch. Get it now!”

Finch’s blood turned to ice in his veins. His shaking fingers scrabbled uselessly at the tiled floor. The final wingnut lay just beyond his reach, a glint of brass in the gloom. Above, the light in the window burned like a malevolent eye. The shouted words echoed in the suddenly silent laboratory, seeming to hang in the air with the dust motes.
“Get it, Finch,” the Voice repeated, a low and dangerous vibration. “Now. Before he comes down.”

It was heavier than he expected, and cold, a deep and unnatural cold that leached the warmth from his fingertips.
“I have it,” he croaked, his throat tight.
“Do not drop it. Your life is worth less than that crystal, I assure you,” Croft’s voice was matter-of-fact, a statement of simple truth. “The notebooks, Finch. Get the notebooks!”

He gathered the stack of black leather books, hugging them to his chest like a shield. His eyes darted about the cluttered room, searching for an escape. “This way,” the Voice commanded from the far side of the laboratory. “There is a service door. For the gardeners.”
A faint disturbance in the air, a shimmer near a wall hitherto unseen in the darkness, was his only guide. He stumbled towards it, tripping over a coil of thick, rubber-coated wire and catching himself on the edge of a workbench. A series of glass vials rattled precariously.
“Quietly, you clumsy oaf!” hissed Croft.
The footsteps from the house had stopped. A new sound reached them; the grating of a heavy bolt being drawn. The main house door was opening.
Finch reached the spot indicated by the invisible man. He ran his trembling hands over a rough plank wall. He could find no handle, no latch, no seam. “There is nothing here,” he whispered, his voice rising in hysteria. “Croft, there is no door!”
“Feel for the latch! It is a simple bar-latch, set low. The man who built this place was a miser. He would not pay for a proper handle.”
A brilliant beam of light suddenly sliced through the laboratory’s grimy windows as the house door swung fully open. It swept across the room, illuminating the chaos, the strange central machine, the splintered cabinet. Finch flattened himself against the wall, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. The beam passed over him, a hair’s breadth from his shoulder. He held his breath until his lungs burned.
His searching fingers found a sliver of cold iron. The latch. He fumbled with it, the mechanism stiff with disuse and rust.
“Hurry!” The voice was a ghost at his ear.
The latch gave way with a groan of protesting metal. Finch pulled the door inwards. It opened onto blackness, and the smell of damp earth and night-blooming flowers. He bundled himself through the narrow opening, the sharp corners of the notebooks digging into his arms. He did not dare look back.
He found himself in a manicured garden, the laboratory wall to his back. Before him lay a wide expanse of perfect lawn, and beyond it, a high stone wall topped with what looked like broken glass. The moon had risen, a thin, cruel sliver that offered little light but made the darkness deeper.
The beam of light from the house door stabbed out again, this time pointing directly at the laboratory. A figure was silhouetted against it, a stout man holding a lantern and what was unmistakably a shotgun.
“I know you are in there!” the man bellowed. “Come out now, and I will be lenient.”
Finch began to run, a low, crouching scramble across the damp grass.
“Stay off the gravel!” Croft’s command was sharp. “To the left, against the hedge!”
Finch veered away from a pale path that snaked through the garden, his shoes sinking into the soft turf. The silence of his passage was a small mercy. He ran, gasping for air, the lens a lump of ice against his hip, the notebooks a leaden weight. The hedge was a wall of yew, tall and dense. He pressed himself into its scratchy embrace as the lantern light began to bob across the lawn. The man was coming to inspect the laboratory.
“He is a fool,” Croft murmured, his voice now strangely calm, almost academic. “He assumes we are still inside. Predictable. Keep moving. Follow the hedge to the north wall.”
They moved in a strange, desperate tandem; the visible man scuttling through the shadows, and the invisible one a stream of cold air and sharp commands beside him. Finch could hear the man, their pursuer, reaching the laboratory door. He heard the door creak open.
“Hello?” the man’s voice called into the building. “I am giving you one last chance!”
A pause. Finch imagined the man peering into the silent, empty room, the evidence of their intrusion laid bare in the lantern’s glow: the splintered cabinet, the missing lens.
“He will see the lens is gone,” Finch panted, his throat raw. “He will know we have fled.”
“Then move faster,” was the unsympathetic reply.
They reached the end of the yew hedge. Before them stood the north wall, a sheer face of moss-slicked stone at least ten feet high. The broken glass on top glittered like dragon’s teeth in the moonlight.
“I cannot climb this,” Finch said, the words a statement of utter defeat. He sagged against the wall, the notebooks slipping from his grasp. He caught them just before they hit the ground.
“You will climb it because you have no other choice,” Croft said. “The main gate will be locked, and he will be watching it. This is our only way.”
A roar of rage erupted from the direction of the laboratory. “Thieves! You will not get away with this!”
The man, whom Finch now thought of as the Proprietor, was no longer being cautious. His footsteps crunched heavily on the gravel path as he began to search the gardens in earnest. The lantern beam swung wildly, casting monstrous, dancing shadows.
“The ivy,” Croft said, his voice tight with urgency. “To your right. There is a thick growth of it. Use it.”
Finch saw it, a dark mass clinging to the stone. It looked ancient, thick as a man’s arm at its base. He shoved the notebooks inside his coat, a clumsy, bulky armour. He reached up, got a handful of tough, woody vine, and pulled. It held.
He began to climb. It was a clumsy, terrifying ascent. His city shoes, so ill-suited for this work, slipped on the damp stone. The ivy tore at his hands and face. The notebooks and the lens in his pocket threw off his balance. He climbed, his muscles screaming, his breath coming in ragged sobs. Below him, the lantern beam was sweeping closer.
“He sees the disturbed ivy!” Croft’s voice was a whip-crack. “Faster, man, faster!”
Finch risked a glance down. The beam was fixed on the base of the wall, right where he had started. The Proprietor gave a shout of discovery. Finch heard a metallic click. The man was cocking his gun.
A wave of pure, animal terror propelled Finch upwards. He scrambled, his fingers raw, his knuckles bloody. He reached the top, his chest heaving. The jagged glass loomed before him. There was a small gap, a section where the masonry had crumbled and the glass had not been replaced. It was barely a foot wide.
He tried to swing his leg over, but his coat, weighted down with his stolen goods, caught on a shard of glass. He was stuck, pinned like an insect, silhouetted against the moonlit sky.
“You damned fool!” Croft’s voice was no longer a whisper, but a furious snarl.
A sudden, immense pressure slammed into his back. It was not a hand; it felt like a battering ram of solid force, a point of immense, focused power. The air was knocked from his lungs in a great whoosh. He was flung forward, through the gap, his coat tearing with a loud rip. He tumbled through the air, a mess of flailing limbs, and landed with a bone-jarring thud in a thicket of nettles and brambles on the other side of the wall.
For a moment he lay there, stunned, the sting of the nettles a strange, distant counterpoint to the agony in his twisted ankle. The notebooks had spilled from his coat and lay scattered around him in the dirt.
Then he heard a new sound, a sound that chilled him more than the shotgun or Croft’s rage. It was the frantic, savage barking of a dog.
“Get up, Finch!” The voice was beside him again, the cold air swirling around him. “He has let the dog out. Get up and run!”
Finch pushed himself up, his ankle screaming in protest. A large, dark shape, a hound of some sort, was baying furiously on the other side of the wall, unable to follow but indicating their position with perfect accuracy. The Proprietor shouted something to the animal.
Finch frantically gathered the notebooks, his hands shaking so violently he could barely grasp them. He was limping badly, each step a fresh agony. He stumbled out of the thicket and onto a dark country lane.
“Which way?” he cried, looking left and right into the oppressive darkness.
“Away from the barking, you imbecile!”
He lurched down the lane, half running, half hopping. The barking grew more distant, but it was replaced by the sound of Croft’s own laboured breathing, a strange, wheezing noise that seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere at once. It was the first human sound Finch had heard him make, besides his voice.
“You are hurt,” Finch stated, surprised at his own observation.
“The exertion,” the Voice rasped. “This damnable state drains the very life from me. I am not a ghost, Finch. I am flesh and blood, though you cannot see it. I have a heart that beats and lungs that burn. Now stop talking and move.”
They continued in silence for what felt like an eternity, Finch’s limp growing more pronounced, the lane stretching endlessly before them. The initial terror was fading, replaced by a dull, throbbing exhaustion and a growing, sullen resentment. He had been threatened, used, and physically thrown over a wall by a man he could not even see. He glanced down at the notebooks in his arms, the reason for this entire nightmare.
Presently, they came to a crossroads. A weathered signpost pointed in three directions: to the village, to the main road, and down a smaller, unkempt track that led into a dark wood.
“The woods,” Croft commanded. “They will not think to look for us there. It leads circuitously back towards my own lodgings.”
Finch hesitated. “I can go no further. My ankle…”
“Do you think I care about your ankle?” The Voice was laced with a chilling venom. “Do you think your petty discomforts register in the slightest? I am fighting for my life’s work, for the very secret of existence, and I am cursed to be shackled to a snivelling clerk with a sprain.”
The injustice of it, the sheer, unadulterated arrogance, ignited a spark of anger in Finch’s exhausted soul. “A clerk who just saved your life’s work,” he retorted, his voice trembling but defiant. “A clerk who is bleeding and bruised and who you threw into a patch of nettles. These books,” he held them out, “and this lens,” he patted his pocket, “I could throw them into the ditch. I could leave you here, a cold patch of air in the middle of a lane.”
The air grew deathly still. The faint chirping of crickets seemed to halt. The coldness around Finch intensified until it was a physical pain, a freezer-burn on his skin. He felt a presence move in front of him, blocking his path.
“You could,” the Voice said, and it was the quietest, most terrifying it had ever been. “But then I would have no more use for you. And things for which I have no use, I discard. Do you know how easy it would be to find a stone, Finch? A heavy one? Do you know how easy it would be to crush your skull in the dark? No one would see me. No one would ever know. They would find your body in the morning and call it a robbery gone wrong.”
Finch stared into the empty darkness where the voice emanated. He saw his own death reflected there, a senseless, invisible violence. The spark of defiance was extinguished, replaced by a cold, abiding dread. He was not a partner in this enterprise; he was a tool, and a disposable one at that.
“The woods,” Finch said, his voice a dry whisper. He turned and hobbled down the track.
The path under the trees was treacherous, a mess of roots and slick mud. Croft offered no further threats, merely directing him with curt, infrequent commands. “Left here.” “Mind the stream.” “Halt.”
They stopped in a small clearing. The moon found a gap in the canopy, illuminating the space in a ghostly, silver light.
“We will rest here for a moment,” Croft said. His breathing was still ragged. “Give me the lens.”
Finch hesitated.
“Give it to me, Finch.”
Slowly, reluctantly, Finch reached into his pocket and drew out the milky crystal. He held it out. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, the lens simply lifted from his palm, suspended in mid-air, and drifted away from him. It came to rest floating about five feet from the ground.
Finch watched, mesmerised, as the lens was turned over and over by unseen fingers. He could perceive the faintest distortion in the air around it, a slight wavering, as if the space itself was being manipulated.

The constables watched him, their hands held ready, as if dealing with a man who might become violent.

Constable Hall looked up at the window. “Stay right there, Mr. Utterson. We’ll need a statement.” He turned back to Kemp. The presence of another witness, however confused, gave a sliver of credence to the impossible. “Sir, explain this. Slowly.”

Kemp took a deep, shuddering breath, forcing himself to be calm, to be the man of science they knew. “His name is Griffin. We were at university together. He has discovered a process, a chemical process, to render living tissue invisible. He came to my house tonight, seeking refuge. He is wounded, desperate, and completely insane. He spoke of a… a Reign of Terror. I refused to help him. I tried to lock him in, to alert the authorities. This was his response.” He gestured to his torn collar, the blood on his face, the unconscious cabman.

Jeffers scribbled in a small notebook, his pencil moving stiffly. “A chemical process,” he repeated, not looking up.

“Yes! He is not a ghost or a spirit. He is a man of flesh and blood. He can be hurt. He can be stopped. But you can’t see him. That is the entire problem. He’s as solid as you or I. When I fought him, I could feel him. Strong. Incredibly strong.”

Hall scratched his helmeted head. “So where is he now, sir?”

“I don’t know. He ran back towards my garden when he heard your whistle. He could be hiding there. He could be halfway across the downs by now. He has no clothes. The cold will affect him. But he is resourceful. And he is murderously angry. At me, primarily, but at everyone now.”

An ambulance bell clanged in the distance. The machinery of order was slowly grinding into motion, but it was a machinery designed for a visible world.

“Right,” said Hall, making a decision. “Jeffers, you stay with the cabman and Mr. Kemp. Get Mr. Utterson’s full account. I’m going to telephone the station, get more men down here. We’ll search the doctor’s property and the surrounding area.” He looked at Kemp. “Sir, I must ask you to come back to your house with my partner. We need to see where this… encounter… began.”

Kemp’s first instinct was to refuse, to run as far from that house as possible. But he knew the constable was right. The evidence was there. “Very well,” he said, his jaw tight. “But be careful. Both of you. Do not underestimate him. Do not for a second believe he is not there just because you cannot see him.”

As Hall ran off towards a public telephone box, Jeffers helped Kemp back towards his house. The front door still hung open. The hallway was dark. Jeffers shone his lantern inside, the beam cutting through the gloom. Everything seemed normal. “He was in your study, you said, sir?”

“Yes. Upstairs. The first door on the right.”

They ascended the stairs, their footsteps echoing in the silent house. Jeffers held his lantern high, his other hand resting on the truncheon at his belt. Kemp followed, every nerve alight with dread. The study door was a wreck, one of the panels kicked through, the lock splintered and broken. Inside, the room was just as Griffin had left it; the overturned table, the smashed glass on the floor, the deep impression on the basket chair, and the faint, lingering smell of cigar smoke and whiskey.

Jeffers let out a low whistle. “Someone made a mess of things, all right.” He swung his lantern beam around. It caught the scarlet dressing gown, lying in a heap by the chair. “Is this his, sir?”

“He was wearing it,” Kemp said. “He took it off to chase me.”

The constable prodded the garment with the toe of his boot. “So he’s… naked now?”

“Completely. It’s the only way to be truly invisible. His blood, however, is not. When it leaves his body and coagulates, it becomes visible. There was a stain on the bed earlier.”

Jeffers shone his light on the disordered bed. Sure enough, a dark, reddish-brown smear was visible on the white sheet, just as Kemp had said. The young constable’s expression hardened. He was still a universe away from believing, but the physical evidence was undeniable. Something profoundly strange had happened in this room. He looked at the basket chair. He reached out a hesitant hand and pressed down on the seat where the invisible man had supposedly sat. The wicker creaked.

“He sat there for hours,” Kemp said, his voice flat. “He ate cold cutlets. He drank my whiskey. He smoked my cigars. He told me how he was going to terrorise the world. And I,” Kemp’s voice broke for a moment, thick with self-recrimination, “I listened to him.”

The telephone rang downstairs, a shrill, piercing sound that made both men jump. It rang again and again, an insistent summons.

“That will be Colonel Adye, the Chief Constable,” Kemp said, his composure returning. “Hall was to call the station. I must speak to him myself.”

They went downstairs. Kemp walked into his drawing room and lifted the receiver. “Kemp here.”

“Kemp?” The voice on the other end was clipped, authoritative. “Adye. Constable Hall has just given me the most extraordinary report. Are you drunk, man, or have you gone mad?”

“Neither, Colonel,” Kemp said, his grip tightening on the receiver. “I am in greater possession of my faculties than I have ever been. Everything your constable told you is true. And it is likely a grotesque understatement of the situation.”

There was a pause. “I’m coming over,” Adye said finally. “Don’t say a word of this to anyone else. I’ll be there in ten minutes. And Kemp… try to pull yourself together.” The line went dead.

Kemp replaced the receiver and stood for a moment, his hand resting on the telephone. Pull himself together. It was sound advice. He was the only person who understood the reality of the threat. His fear was useless; what was needed now was his intellect. He was a scientist. Griffin was a scientist. He had to think of this not as a supernatural horror, but as a problem, an equation to be solved. An invisible, homicidal variable had been introduced into the orderly world of Burdock. He had to help them find a way to solve for X.

When Colonel Adye arrived, he was exactly the man Kemp had expected; a man with a trim, grey moustache and an air of absolute command that came from years in the army. He dismissed the constable and sat opposite Kemp in the drawing room, his eyes sharp and appraising.

“Now, Kemp,” he began, his voice low and serious. “Tell me everything. From the beginning. And do not omit a single detail, no matter how outlandish it may seem.”

And so, for the second time that night, Kemp recounted the story. He told Adye of the voice, the bandage in mid-air, the struggle, the long and terrible confession of Griffin. He described the physics of the invisibility, as Griffin had explained it. He spoke of Griffin’s paranoia, his rage, his colossal ego. He took Adye upstairs and showed him the wrecked study, the bloodstain, the invisible man’s abandoned clothes.

Adye listened without interruption, his face impassive. He touched the impression in the chair. He examined the splintered door. He looked at the half-eaten food that Kemp had brought up, now sitting cold on the small table. “He ate this?”

“Yes. With his mouth full,” Kemp said, the absurd detail making the whole thing feel more real.

“And the cabman?”

“He is a witness. As is my neighbour, Utterson. They saw me fighting with nothing. They saw the consequences.”

Adye paced the room, his hands clasped behind his back. He was a practical man, a man who dealt in evidence, in motive and opportunity. This defied all of it. Yet, here was Dr. Kemp, a pillar of the community, bruised and terrified. Here was a wrecked room. Here was an unconscious cabman in the hospital. The facts were insane, but they were facts nonetheless.

“If we accept this premise,” Adye said, stopping in front of Kemp. “If we accept, for the sake of argument, that there is an invisible man, what do we do?”

“We hunt him,” Kemp replied at once. “But not like a man. We hunt him like a phenomenon. He is naked and barefoot. He will leave tracks on soft ground. He has a human scent; dogs can follow him. He displaces air; he will disturb dust, leaves, smoke. He is solid; he cannot walk through walls. He needs to eat and sleep. He is vulnerable.”

Adye nodded slowly, his military mind processing the tactical implications. “Tracks. Dogs. Good. What else?”

“We must make him visible,” Kemp said, leaning forward intently. “That is the key. If he can be seen, he is just a man. A strong and desperate man, but only a man. We need to alert the public. Not to cause a panic, but to make them our eyes. To have them watch for the unexplained. A door opening on its own. Footprints appearing from nowhere. A dog barking at thin air. And we must tell them to throw things. Flour, dust, paint, anything that will stick to him, that will outline him.”

“Good God,” Adye breathed. “You want me to issue a proclamation telling the citizens of Burdock to start throwing flour at ghosts.”

“I want you to save lives, Colonel,” Kemp said firmly. “Because he will kill. He told me as much. He sees it as his right. He is planning a Reign of Terror. His first act will be to come back here and kill me. Then, he will move on to bigger targets. Believe me, a little public bewilderment is better than a string of inexplicable murders.”

Adye stood in silence for a long time. The pale dawn light was filtering into the room, making the shadows retreat. He looked out the window at the quiet, respectable street, at the town of Burdock sleeping peacefully, unaware of the nightmare that had been unleashed within its borders.

“Right,” he said, his voice hard as flint. “We’ll do it. I’ll get every available man. We’ll establish a cordon around this part of town. I want roadblocks set up. No one in or out without being searched. We’ll get the dogs from the barracks. And I’ll have men with bags of flour and powdered chalk stationed on every corner.” He turned to Kemp. “I will need you. You know him. You know how his mind works. Where would he go?”

Kemp thought of Griffin’s frantic, brilliant mind, his arrogance, his needs. “He’s cold, hungry, and enraged. He needs shelter and food first. But his pride is his driving force. He won’t just hide. He’ll want to prove me wrong. He’ll want to demonstrate his power. He won’t run far. He’ll do something to create terror. Something public. Something to show us all that he is in control.”

A sudden thought struck Kemp, cold and sharp. “His books,” he said. “His research notes. He told me he left them behind in Iping. He will want them back. They are the source of his power, the proof of his genius. He will feel incomplete without them.”

“Iping?” Adye said. “That’s over twenty miles from here. He can’t get there on foot, not in this state.”

“He will find a way,” Kemp insisted. “He will steal a horse, or stow away on a train. That is his goal. That must be where he is headed. We must warn them. We must have every road between here and Iping watched.”

Colonel Adye was already moving towards the telephone. “I’ll get a wire to the constabulary there. And I’ll get my men moving here.” He paused, his hand on the receiver, and looked at Kemp. The initial shock had worn off, replaced by a grim resolve. “Kemp, what you have done… betraying him. He will not forgive it.”

“I know,” Kemp said quietly, looking at his own trembling hands. “I have made myself the focal point of his madness.”
