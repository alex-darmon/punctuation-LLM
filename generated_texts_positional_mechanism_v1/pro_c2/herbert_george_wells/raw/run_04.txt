Well, one particularly still afternoon—my seventh in the new preserve—as I was cataloguing floral species in a valley of almost painful beauty, there occurred a most peculiar incident. The graceful children of the sun were the descendants of our own leisured classes; the Eloi, living their simple, beautiful, and altogether useless lives. And these bleached, nocturnal things, these Morlocks, who had once been my fellow men, were the descendants of the worker; grown accustomed to the darkness, and now, I began to suspect, the masters of the Eloi’s world. For it was clear to me that the Overworlders, for all their beauty, were a fragile and decadent race. They possessed no industry, no real curiosity, and I had seen a strange fear in their eyes. Did they fear the dark? Or did they fear what came from the dark? The Morlocks, on the other hand, had retained some portion of the old human vigour. It was they who must be tending the great engines whose faint throbbing I could feel beneath my feet; it was they who must be weaving the fabrics and preparing the food for the creatures of the Upper World. And in return? What did they receive in return?

A chilling thought, a grotesque and scarcely credible suspicion, began to take shape in my mind. These beautiful people of the surface were so frail, so childlike. They were fatted cattle, living in a fool’s paradise. And the Morlocks, their subterranean brethren, were not their servants, but their keepers. I remembered the joints of meat I had seen in the great hall where I had dined. I had taken them for some unknown animal. Now a more dreadful interpretation suggested itself. What if the meat was not from some beast of the field, but from the Eloi themselves? The idea was so utterly monstrous, so contrary to any conception of humanity, that I thrust it from me. And yet, it returned, again and again, with a dreadful, insistent logic. The relationship was not one of master and servant, nor of two separate but equal species. It was the relationship of the farmer to his livestock.

I shuddered, and looked again at the two pretty Eloi, still playing their thoughtless game in the sunlight. They were so beautiful, so graceful; and so utterly doomed. They were like the delicate porcelain figures of a bygone age, exquisite and entirely helpless. Their laughter echoed in the ruined landscape, a sound of perfect innocence, and it was the most heartbreaking sound I had ever heard. I had to know the truth. I had to understand the full nature of this terrible symbiosis. My fear of the well, of the darkness and the unknown things that dwelt within it, was still very great. But my intellectual curiosity, and a new and terrible pity for the fate of the Eloi, was now greater. I turned from the sportive couple and faced the black aperture once more. My mind was made up. I would descend. I would venture into the world of the Morlocks and tear the heart from their mystery.

I went back to the great house, seeking Weena. I found her with a dozen or so other Eloi, seated in a circle upon the floor of the great hall, placidly eating fruit. They seemed to have forgotten my existence entirely. She looked up at my approach, a faint flicker of recognition in her large, soft eyes. I sat by her and, using the simple words of their tongue that I had learned, I tried again to question her about the wells. I pointed in the direction of the ruin, and made a gesture of descent, a downward spiral with my finger. “Morlock?” I asked, using the name that was now forming in my mind. “Down? Morlock?” A look of absolute terror came upon her face, and upon the faces of all those who heard me. It was a terror so profound, so abject, that it was quite unlike any emotion I had yet seen in them. It was the instinctive dread of a sheep at the scent of a wolf. Weena began to cry, making soft, whimpering sounds, and the others huddled together, casting fearful glances towards the shadows of the hall.

My last doubt was swept away. The Morlocks were a source of profound, ancestral terror. My grotesque theory was, in all its essentials, true. The Eloi were their creation, their playthings, and their food. I tried to comfort Weena, stroking her soft hair, but she shrank from my touch, her eyes fixed on the darkness beyond the sunlit entrance. I had spoken the name of the bogeyman, the monster that haunted their racial memory. It was clear I would learn no more from them; their fear was too great to allow for any rational communication on the subject. I must rely upon my own resources.

I spent the remainder of that day preparing for my descent. My chief difficulty was the lack of any adequate light. My matches were a finite and precious resource, and I knew that in the absolute blackness of the Underworld, to be left without a flame would mean certain death. I searched the ruins near the great house, hoping to find some remnant of the old world’s technology; a lamp, a torch, anything that might serve my purpose. I found nothing but decay. The metals had rusted to dust, the plastics had crumbled, and anything of a more complex nature had long since been looted or destroyed. I began to despair, but then my eyes fell upon the great bronze doors of what I took to be a museum or library. They were green with verdigris, but solid and impassable. Yet near them, I had seen a heap of broken fragments, and among these, a quantity of some yellowish, waxy substance. I examined it. It was crumbly and soft. I took a piece and held it to the flame of a match. It sputtered, and then caught, burning with a slow, smoky, but surprisingly steady light. It was camphor, or something very like it. A great store of it must have been housed in the building, and had been preserved, by some chemical miracle, through the long ages.

I waited until the sun was low in the sky, casting long shadows across the land. I felt a pang of genuine affection for this fragile creature, and a renewed determination to understand, and perhaps even to alter, the dreadful conditions of her life. I left her standing in the twilight, a small, forlorn figure, and made my way back to the ruined heap of masonry that hid the shaft.

The air was cooler now, and a purple haze was gathering in the valleys. The world of the Eloi was preparing for its brief night of slumber and fear. I reached the well. The circular opening was a pool of profound blackness against the grey stone. I took a piece of the camphor, moulded it around the end of my metal rod, and with a hand that trembled slightly, I struck a match and lit my makeshift torch. It flared up, casting a flickering, uncertain yellow light, and filling the air with its pungent, aromatic smoke. I took a deep breath, like a diver preparing to plunge into a cold sea, and swung my legs over the edge.

My feet found the first of the metal rungs. They were cold and damp to the touch, and coated with a thin layer of slime. I held my torch aloft and peered down into the darkness. The shaft descended vertically, a perfect cylinder bored deep into the earth. The light of my torch only penetrated a short way, revealing the curve of the wall and the endless succession of hand and foot holds. Below that, there was only an impenetrable blackness. It seemed a monstrous and foolhardy thing to be doing, to be voluntarily leaving the light of the sun and the sweet air of the surface for this dark and terrible abyss. What monsters, I wondered, might be stirring in the depths, roused by the unfamiliar scent of my camphor torch?

Then I thought of the serene and beautiful Eloi, living their lives in blissful ignorance of the price of their comfort. I thought of the quiet terror that filled their nights. I thought of humanity, split and degraded into this monstrous parody of its former self. And I knew that I could not turn back. The truth, however terrible, was down there. I took a firmer grip on my rod, planted my feet securely on the rungs, and began to climb down into the earth. The circle of twilight sky above me shrank with every foot I descended. The air grew colder and more humid. The only sound was the scuffing of my boots on the metal rungs and the faint, rhythmic pulse of the unseen machines, which grew steadily louder as I went deeper. My torch cast gigantic, dancing shadows on the curved walls. I felt terribly alone, a single point of consciousness invading a vast and hostile kingdom. I descended for what seemed a very long time, my arms and legs aching with the strain. The shaft appeared to be bottomless. I paused to rest, clinging to the ladder, and looked up. The opening of the well was now a tiny disc of pale light, a distant star in a black firmament. There was no turning back. I looked down again, into the utter dark, and continued my descent.

At last, after a descent of perhaps two or three hundred feet, I saw a light below me. It was not the steady glow of a lamp, but a faint, intermittent blue flickering. And with the light came a sound, a clanking and clattering that I could now distinguish from the general hum of the machinery. I extinguished my torch, for I did not wish to announce my presence, and continued downward in the darkness, guiding myself by touch. The last few rungs were slippery with a strange oil. My feet touched level ground. I had reached the bottom of the shaft.

It was the smell of the Morlock. The blue light I had seen now resolved itself into flashes from some great piece of machinery that stood in the centre of a colossal cavern. They were the Morlocks.

There were dozens of them, perhaps hundreds. They were just as I had seen the first one: dull white, with large, reddish, light-sensitive eyes. They were naked, but for a sort of grimy harness some of them wore. They swarmed over the great machine, tending to its needs with a frantic, insect-like energy. They were smaller than the Eloi, and their bodies were stooped, their heads held low. They chattered to one another in a strange, guttural tongue. They were the masters of the machine, the engineers of this subterranean world. And as I watched them, my first theory about the division of labour seemed confirmed. Here was Industry, banished from the sunlit world.

But there was another part of the cavern, away from the glare of the machine. Here, the darkness was deeper, but I could hear a different sound, a low murmuring, and the tearing of flesh. I fumbled in my pocket for my matches. I had to see. Shielding the flame with my hand, I struck a match. The sudden flare of light was blinding. The Morlocks near me shrieked and recoiled, shielding their great, sensitive eyes. In that brief moment of illumination, I saw what they were doing. A long, low table of white metal stood in the shadows. And upon it was laid the dismembered body of an Eloi. Several Morlocks were gathered around it, using crude knives to cut the flesh from the bones. My stomach turned, and the match fell from my trembling fingers. My most horrible suspicion was confirmed in all its unutterable ghastliness. The delicate Eloi were not just the cattle of the Morlocks; they were their carefully prepared delicacy.

A wave of nausea and fury swept over me. These creatures, these foul cannibals, had once been men. I felt a surge of primitive, homicidal rage. I gripped my metal rod, and for a moment I was on the verge of rushing forward, of smashing and destroying. But the folly of such an action was immediately apparent. I was one against a multitude. To reveal myself would be to share the fate of the Eloi on the table. I forced myself to be calm, to be the scientific observer once more. I had to understand the whole of it. I retreated back towards the wall, my mind reeling. The relationship was now horribly clear. The Morlocks bred and tended the Eloi, providing them with food, clothing, and shelter in their sunlit paradise. They were the ranchers. I crouched in the darkness, trying to process the full implications of my discovery. Humanity had not just diverged; it had devoured itself. The refinement of the one class had led to a decadent helplessness; the brutalisation of the other had led to a literal and predatory hunger. And as I watched the Morlocks at their grisly feast, another, even more disturbing thought occurred to me. The Eloi feared the Morlocks, yes, but their fear was the passive, witless fear of sheep. They made no attempt to fight back, to escape, to alter their fate. Generations of ease had bred the spirit right out of them. But did they not, on some level, know? Did they not walk willingly to the slaughter? I could not say. The mystery of their consciousness was as profound as the darkness that now surrounded me. I knew I had to escape this ghastly place, to return to the surface. But I also knew that I would be back. My investigation had only just begun. I had to find my Time Machine, and for that, I suspected, I would have to deal with the Morlocks. I was a stranger in a world where man was both shepherd and wolf, and I was caught, horribly, between the two.

They were the mechanics, the engineers, the tenders of the future. And the food of the gods, it seemed, was the price of their labour. I flattened myself into a recess behind a great, vibrating pillar as a group of them scurried past, their large, red eyes glowing dimly in the gloom. They carried tools of some strange, polished metal, and they chattered to one another in their horrible, liquid tongue. They paid me no mind; their senses, so acute in the dark, were evidently focused upon their tasks. I was, for the moment, merely a part of the shadow.

My desperate need for escape warred with a lingering, horrified curiosity. What was the purpose of all this industry? The Eloi had no need of it. Their simple robes, their fruit, their crumbling palaces—none of this required such a formidable and complex machinery. Then the answer struck me with the force of a physical blow. The machines were not for the Eloi. They were for the Morlocks. This entire underworld was a self-contained civilisation. The ventilation shafts which I had mistaken for a service to the upper world were for them; the food they ate was bred for them; the labour they performed was for their own sustenance. The Eloi were not their masters; they were their crop. The Overworld was nothing more than a sunlit pasture, a free-range farm for the production of meat. The serene beauty, the flowers, the amorous play—it was all the ghastly artistry of the rancher.

A wave of nausea and vertigo swept over me. I had to get to the surface, into the clean air. I could not breathe this atmosphere of decay and cannibalism a moment longer. Waiting until the passage was clear, I darted from my hiding place and ran, stumbling and reckless, in the direction I believed the well shaft to be. I had no clear memory of the path, only a frantic instinct guiding me away from the central chamber. I rounded a corner and my foot struck something soft and yielding. A Morlock, crouched in the darkness, let out a sharp, bird-like cry of alarm. In an instant, I was sprawling, my hands scraping against the metal floor. The creature’s clammy fingers closed around my ankle. Its grip was astonishingly strong. I cried out, a strangled gasp of pure terror, and kicked out with my other foot. I felt a sickening, soft crunch, and the grip relaxed. The Morlock shrieked, a high, piercing sound of pain that was immediately answered by other cries from the darkness all around me.

There was no more time for stealth. I scrambled to my feet and ran for my life. The darkness behind me was alive with the patter of bare feet, the sound multiplying until it seemed a whole army was at my heels. I fumbled in my pocket for my box of matches. My fingers, clumsy with fear, found it and struck a light. The sudden blaze illuminated a long corridor before me, and at its end, a circle of faint grey light: the well shaft. It also illuminated the pack of Morlocks behind me, a tumbling, horrid wave of white bodies and glaring eyes, all recoiling from the flame. I did not wait. I hurled the burning match at them and turned and fled towards the opening. They howled in rage and pain as the tiny fire fell amongst them.

I reached the shaft, my lungs burning, my heart a frantic drum against my ribs. The metal rungs of the ladder felt blessedly solid beneath my hands and feet. I began to climb, not daring to look down, expecting at any moment to feel their claws on my legs. The sounds from below were a confused medley of shrieks and chattering, but they were not, I realised, sounds of pursuit. They were afraid of the open air, of the light that trickled down from above. The well shaft was, for them, a barrier. I climbed faster, my relief giving me a renewed surge of strength. Up and up I went, into the growing light, until my head emerged from the opening into the brilliant, glorious sunshine of the upper world. I hauled my aching body out onto the grass and lay there, gasping, my face pressed against the warm earth. The sky had never seemed so blue, the air so sweet. I had been down in that foul darkness for only a few hours, but it felt as if I had returned from the land of the dead.

For a long time I simply lay there, shuddering, every muscle protesting. The contrast was too profound. Down there, the darkness, the thunder of machines, the reek of blood, and the unspeakable horror of the cannibal feast. Up here, the gentle warmth of the sun, the scent of flowers, the drone of insects, and the musical laughter of the Eloi. I sat up and looked about me. The landscape was as placid and beautiful as ever. A short distance away, a group of Eloi were bathing in the river, their lithe bodies flashing in the sunlight. They were like figures from some forgotten pastoral poem. But the poem was a lie. I saw them now not as the inheritors of the earth, but as beautiful, witless sheep, playing in the meadow while the butcher sharpened his knives in the cellar. Every graceful movement, every peal of laughter, was underscored by the terrible knowledge I now possessed. Their serenity was the serenity of the doomed.

My first thought was for Weena. I felt a desperate, paternal fear for her. Had I left her for too long? Was she safe? I got to my feet, my body stiff and bruised, and began to search for her. I called her name, my voice hoarse. I found her not far from the great palace, asleep under a flowering bush, a half-eaten fruit still clutched in her small hand. A pathetic little garland of flowers was tangled in her hair. I knelt beside her, my heart aching with a strange and terrible pity. She was so small, so fragile, so utterly defenceless. She was my friend, the only creature in this alien world who had shown me any affection. And she was food. The thought was a physical sickness. I wanted to wake her, to shake her, to try and impress upon her the awful peril she was in. But what was the use? How could I explain the nature of the Morlocks to a mind that had no concept of fear, or danger, or death? To her, the darkness was simply a thing to be avoided, like a sudden chill in the air. The wells were merely curious, ugly holes. Her world was circumscribed by sunlight and fruit and sleep and play.

She stirred and opened her eyes, and upon seeing me, she smiled a smile of pure, uncomplicated joy. She sat up and threw her little arms around my neck, chattering happily, pointing to the fruit and offering it to me. I could not bear it. I gently detached her arms and stood up, my mind in a turmoil. I looked from her innocent face to the crumbling, magnificent ruins that dotted the landscape. A new and even more dreadful line of speculation had opened up in my mind. How had it come to this? My theory of the Capitalist and the Labourer seemed now a crude and simplistic starting point. This was not merely the result of a social divide; it was the result of a perversion of biological law. Nature, red in tooth and claw, had been banished from this world, only to re-emerge from underground in a more terrible form.

I considered the process. The wealthy, refined class, having secured the world for their pleasure, would have eliminated all dangers. Predators, diseases, hardships of every kind would have been conquered. In their walled gardens and beautiful palaces, they would have cultivated ease and gentleness. But in doing so, they would have bred out the very qualities that ensure survival: courage, cunning, strength, and fear itself. They would become, as I saw them now, perfect, beautiful, and utterly helpless. Meanwhile, the workers, banished underground, would have faced the opposite pressures. In the darkness and confinement, strength and ruthlessness would be virtues. Faced with dwindling resources, deprived of the variety of the surface, what would they eat? Perhaps at first they subsisted on fungi, on roots, on vermin bred in the tunnels. But the memory of the surface, of the soft, slow creatures who lived there, would remain.

Perhaps it began as an act of desperation. A single, starving Morlock dragging a lone Eloi down into the dark. Then, the discovery that these creatures were not only easy to catch, but also, perhaps, nourishing. The taboo, ancient and powerful, against eating one's own kind, would have been the last barrier to fall. But in a world where the two species had diverged so completely, were they still one's own kind? To the Morlock, the Eloi would have seemed as alien as a deer or a sheep does to us. And so, the farm was established. The Eloi, with their dwindling intelligence and lack of fear, would have been easy to manage. Any who showed signs of vigour or suspicion would have been the first to be taken, the first to be culled from the herd. It was a ghastly, inexorable logic, a process of artificial selection that had, over thousands of generations, perfected each species for its role: the Eloi for the table, the Morlock for the kitchen.

And my Time Machine. Where was my Time Machine in this monstrous economy? The Eloi would not have touched it. They had no curiosity, no mechanical aptitude. It must have been the Morlocks. They, with their interest in machinery, would have been irresistibly drawn to it. I pictured them examining it, their pale fingers probing its levers and dials, their great eyes glowing as they puzzled over its quartz rods. They would have seen it as a prize, a strange and wonderful new engine. And they would have done the only thing they knew to do with such a prize: they would have taken it below. It was down there. Down in that black, foetid abyss, with the cannibals.

My despair gave way to a cold, hard anger. I was not just an observer anymore. I was a participant in this dreadful drama. I had a possession to recover, a means of escape to secure. More than that, I felt a flicker of some older, deeper instinct. I could not save this world. I could not reverse the course of eighty thousand years of devolution. But I could not simply turn my back and flee, leaving this pastoral horror to continue unchallenged. And I could not, I would not, leave Weena to her fate. I looked at her as she plaited blades of grass, and a resolve hardened within me. I would go back down. But I would not go back as a frightened, stumbling trespasser. I would go back armed. I would go back bearing the one thing the Morlocks feared, the one weapon against which they had no defence. I would go back with fire. I would become, for a time, a being of the upper world, a god of sunlight and flame, and I would bring my own kind of justice to the creatures of the dark. My quest for knowledge was over; my war for survival had just begun.

I had seen no sign of animal life, save for the Eloi and the Morlocks themselves. My first act, upon this grim resolution, was to seek a weapon. My fists, which I had instinctively clenched upon first seeing the Morlock, were a civilised man’s poor resort against a primal horror. I left Weena playing by the river, her placid incomprehension a strange comfort, and made my way back into the colossal ruins where I had first encountered the creature of the dark. I moved now with purpose, my eyes scanning the debris not for architectural clues or relics of a forgotten art, but for something that I could wield. I needed a club, a mace; something to give weight and reach to my anger. After some searching amongst shattered cornices and the colossal vertebrae of a fallen arch, I found it: a metal bar, perhaps a lever from some mighty but forgotten machine. It was about a yard in length, solid and heavy, but not too heavy to swing. Its surface was coated in a greenish verdigris, but the iron beneath was sound. I scraped it against a block of granite, and a line of bright metal shone forth. It felt good in my hand. It felt solid and real in a world that had become a waking nightmare.

My second task was to secure a more reliable form of fire than my dwindling supply of matches. The brief flare of a match was enough to startle, but not to repel. I needed a torch, a constant and controllable flame that would serve as both shield and beacon in the underworld. In a sheltered corner of the ruins, I gathered a store of dry moss and splinters of a wood that seemed resinous, for it was strangely preserved. I took a sleeve from my coat, which was already in a lamentable state, and tore it into strips, wrapping them tightly around one end of my iron bar and binding them with tough, trailing creepers. I soaked this makeshift torch head with the driest, most combustible dust I could find. It was a crude device, but my hopes, and indeed my life, now rested upon its efficacy.

With these preparations made, a new and difficult problem presented itself: the question of Weena. I could not, in all conscience, take her with me into the pit. The very thought of her frail form in that suffocating darkness, surrounded by those pallid hunters, was intolerable. Yet to leave her alone on the surface was almost as unthinkable. She was as helpless as a kitten, and with the sun westering, the time of the Morlocks’ ascendancy was approaching. For a time I was at a loss. I tried to explain to her, using gestures and the few simple words of her language I had mastered, that I must go away for a time but would return. She only looked at me with her large, trusting eyes, and clutched my hand, evidently fearing I would leave her. Her simple affection, which had been a source of pleasure, now became a source of acute distress.

Finally, an idea occurred to me. There was a high hill, some two or three miles distant, from which I had seen a great green structure, a ruin of a different sort, made of some malachite-like stone and with many windows. I had called it in my mind the Palace of Green Porcelain. It seemed a more solid and defensible place than these crumbling ruins. And from the hilltop, the land lay open and clear, with fewer of the dark thickets and heaps of masonry that might conceal a well shaft or a lurking Morlock. I decided I would carry Weena there, to a spot where she might be safer, and where I might find her again upon my return.

I took her by the hand and, with my iron bar in my other, I led her away from the river valley and up the long, gentle slope. She came willingly enough, chattering her sweet, meaningless words, and pointing out bright flowers for me to pick for her. The journey took what remained of the afternoon. As we climbed, the air grew cooler, and the character of the landscape changed. The vegetation was sparser, the ground stonier. The wells were less frequent here. At last we reached a small plateau near the summit, a place open to the sky and winds, commanding a view over the whole green and golden land. I found a hollow, sheltered by a group of weather-worn, sarsen-like stones, and there I made Weena sit. I gave her the bunch of flowers I had collected for her, and she began to arrange them with rapt concentration. I tried once more, with signs, to convey that she must wait for me. She smiled and nodded, though I felt with a sinking heart that she had not understood the gravity of my purpose, only that I was playing some new and interesting game. Leaving her there, a small, bright spot of colour in the vast, lonely landscape, was one of the hardest things I have ever had to do. I turned my back on her and strode quickly down the hill, not daring to look back, my heart a cold lump in my chest.

The sun was touching the horizon when I reached the well with the broken pillar. The world was awash in the melancholy light of evening; long, purple shadows stretched from every tree and ruin. A profound silence had fallen, the chirping of the Eloi hushed, as if the world were holding its breath. It was the time of the changing of the guard. I paused at the edge of the shaft, my crude torch in one hand, my iron club in the other. I took out my box of matches. I had perhaps a dozen remaining. I struck one. Its small flame sputtered, a tiny point of human defiance against the encroaching dark. I touched it to the head of my torch. For a moment it only smouldered, giving off a thick, acrid smoke. I blew on it gently, my heart hammering against my ribs. Then, with a soft whoosh, the resinous wood caught, and a great, leaping, yellow-red flame sprang into life, casting a wild, dancing circle of light around me and pushing back the gloom. The heat on my face was cheering. The shadows writhed and fled before it.

Gripping the iron bar an inch or two below the flames, I slung my club through my belt and began my descent. The metal rungs were cold and damp to the touch. Down I went, into the throat of the world. The circle of daylight above me shrank, first to a disk, then to a star of pale turquoise, and then it was gone. I was alone with my torch in a vertical tube of darkness. The air grew thick and warm, and a low, persistent humming, the sound I had heard before, grew louder, vibrating through the very rungs I held. It was the pulse of this nether world, the sound of its great, hidden heart. My torch burned magnificently, its flames roaring in the draft, sending sparks cascading into the abyss below me. The darkness was not empty; it was filled with this sound, and with a faint, unpleasant odour, like that of unwashed bodies and raw meat.

After a descent that seemed to last an age, my foot met not a rung, but solid ground. I was in a vast, cavernous space. The shaft did not open onto a floor, but onto a narrow ledge that ran along the wall of a subterranean hall of unimaginable size. The roof was lost in impenetrable blackness far above, but the space was not entirely dark. A faint, uniform, phosphorescent glow emanated from the walls, a sort of leprous luminescence that served to reveal the scale of the place without truly illuminating it. And the noise! The humming was now a deafening, rhythmic clang and thud of immense machinery. Across the cavern, which must have been half a mile wide, I could dimly perceive great, dark shapes moving: pistons bigger than houses, flywheels that spun in a blur, walking beams that rose and fell with ponderous, irresistible force. And tending these machines, swarming over them like pale ants, were the Morlocks.

I crouched on the ledge, holding my torch low so that its light would not betray me. From this vantage point, I saw the true nature of their labour. They were not merely maintaining the equipment; they were operating a gigantic industrial complex. Great conveyors carried lumps of some dark material into roaring furnaces, whose hot breath I could feel even from my perch. Other machines seemed to be weaving textiles; I saw looms of a fantastic size, though what they wove in this darkness I could not imagine. It was a vision of Hell, but a purposeful, industrialised Hell. My theory had been correct; the comfort and ease of the Eloi was paid for by the ceaseless, sunless toil of the Morlocks.

I began to move along the ledge, seeking a way down to the floor of the cavern, my gaze constantly scanning for any sign of my Time Machine. The Morlocks I saw were all intent on their tasks. They moved with a hurried, nervous energy, their large eyes, even in this dim light, seeming to blink and shy away from the distant glare of the furnaces. They communicated with one another in a series of low, guttural clicks and chirps. As I watched them, my anger warred with a kind of pity. They were foul and repellent, yes, but they were also slaves to this vast, clanking world they had built. How many generations had they lived like this, born in darkness, toiling in darkness, and dying in darkness?

My ledge came to an end at a spiral staircase, clearly of the same make as the ladder in the shaft. I descended, my torch held aloft. As I neared the floor, the smell grew stronger, and another, more sinister sound reached me beneath the din of the engines: a wet, tearing noise, accompanied by sharp cracks. I rounded a pillar of rock and stopped, my blood running cold. I had found their dining hall. It was a cleared space, set away from the main machinery, lit by the same faint phosphorescence. In the centre was a large, stone table, dark and stained. And upon it… I cannot bring myself to write it down in plain terms. Let me say only that I saw the remnants of a meal, and scattered amongst the debris on the floor were a few wilted, familiar-looking flowers. The sight of those blossoms, so emblematic of the sunlit world above, in this subterranean abattoir, was the most obscene thing I had yet witnessed. I saw no Eloi, living or dead, but the evidence was irrefutable. My revulsion was so intense that I gagged, and had to lean against the rock, fighting the urge to be sick.

The Morlocks in this area were not labourers; they were butchers and cooks. They were smaller, slighter creatures than the workers, and they scattered with terrified squeals as my torchlight fell upon them. One, slower than the rest, was caught in the full glare. It shielded its great, red-grey eyes with a pale, five-fingered hand and hissed at me, baring a set of sharp, needle-like teeth. The resemblance to a human being, so horribly distorted, was a thing of pure nightmare.

I had seen enough. The need to find my machine and escape this ghastly world was now a burning imperative. I pushed past the horrid banqueting table and plunged deeper into the network of tunnels that led away from the great hall of machines. The air here was cooler, the noise of the industry fainter. I passed storerooms, filled with bales of the cloth I had seen being woven, and what looked like dormitories, vast caves filled with nests of filthy rags where the Morlocks slept in heaps. And in one of these passages, I found it.

It was in a side cavern, smaller than the others, and it was alone. My Time Machine. It stood on the stony ground, its nickel and ivory and brass strangely incongruous in this primitive darkness. It was not damaged, but the Morlocks had clearly been examining it. A panel on the side was removed, and several of the smaller quartz rods from the main frame were laid out neatly on a piece of cloth on the ground. And the main control lever, the smaller of the two that initiated the temporal transit, was gone. They had taken it. Perhaps they saw it as the key to the whole engine; perhaps it was merely a shiny trinket. Without it, I was as trapped as they were.

A new wave of despair washed over me. To have come so far, to have faced so much, only to be thwarted by a missing lever. I searched the cavern frantically. It was not there. The Morlocks must have carried it off to some place of safekeeping. I stood there in the dark, my torch flame sinking lower, and I felt the last of my strength, my civilised resolve, begin to fail me. I was trapped at the bottom of the world, in a land of cannibals, with my only hope of escape dismantled.

My first impulse, born of that despair, was one of primitive violence. I would find a piece of metal, a heavy stone, and I would hunt the foul things down one by one until I found the one that held my key. I would smash and batter my way to freedom. But the madness of this notion was immediately apparent. I was one man, weakened and unnerved, against a whole subterranean nation. They would overwhelm me in an instant; my bones would soon join those of the Eloi upon their dreadful tables. My fury collapsed into a shivering weakness. The torch, which I had propped in a crevice of the rock, sputtered, and the shadows in the cavern leaped and danced. Its impending failure sent a fresh stab of fear through me. To be left in that absolute blackness, with the knowledge of what crept and crawled there, was a fate too hideous to contemplate.

It was then that an idea, a very different kind of idea, began to take shape in my mind, born not of rage but of that very scientific curiosity that had led me to this ghastly epoch. The Morlocks were not mindless brutes. Their actions bespoke a certain cunning, a mechanical aptitude. They had not simply smashed my machine; they had investigated it, dismantled it with a degree of care, laying out the quartz rods as a watchmaker might lay out the components of a timepiece. They had identified the lever, rightly or wrongly, as a thing of significance. This was not the work of beasts; it was the work of a devolved, but not entirely extinguished, intelligence. And if they possessed intelligence, then perhaps, just perhaps, they were capable of reason. Not the reason of a philosopher, to be sure, but the low, calculating reason of a hungry animal that knows when to fight and when to bargain.

What could I offer them? What treasure did I possess that they might covet more than the curious metal stick they had taken from my engine? The answer was in my hand, or rather, in my pocket. My matches. Fire was a terror to them, but it was also a fascination. I had seen it in the way the Overworlders had been drawn to the little flames I made for their amusement. And I had seen it in the way the Morlocks, even as they fled from my torch, could not help but stare back at its compelling glow. They lived in a world of clanking machinery and profound darkness, lit only, I supposed, by the faint phosphorescence I had glimpsed and perhaps the glow of forges. A portable, controllable source of light and heat; that would be a thing of immense power in their society. It might be a weapon, a tool, or a symbol of authority. It was my only coin.

My despair had not vanished, but it was now overshadowed by a desperate, calculating hope. I had to initiate a parley. I took a steadying breath, the foul air catching in my throat. I first tended to my torch, feeding it a fresh piece of the oily rag I had used before, ensuring it would last me a while longer. Then, I took out my box of safety matches. I counted them. There were seventeen left. Seventeen chances to win back my future. I returned to the main passage, leaving the cavern of my crippled machine behind me, and walked back towards the great hall of the looms. My heart hammered against my ribs, not with the panicked terror of before, but with the cold, focused fear of a man taking a deliberate and colossal risk.

I stopped at the entrance to the vast chamber. The noise was immense, a deafening chorus of clicks and clatters from the weaving machines, punctuated by the hisses and low calls of the Morlocks attending them. I could see them in the gloom, scores of them, pale figures moving with a disturbing, insect-like purpose. I dared not enter. Instead, I chose my ground carefully, in the mouth of the tunnel, with a clear line of retreat behind me. Then, I began. I struck a match.

The tiny flare of light was shockingly bright in the immense darkness. A wave of hissing and chittering arose from the chamber, and the nearest Morlocks recoiled, their great eyes reflecting the flame. I did not move. I held the match aloft, letting it burn down almost to my fingers before I dropped it. The sudden return to gloom was almost as startling as the light had been. I waited. A low murmur went through the assembled creatures. They were disturbed, but also, I sensed, intrigued. I struck a second match.

This time, their reaction was different. There was still fear, but it was mixed with a palpable curiosity. A few of the bolder ones, on the far side of the chamber, crept a little closer, their heads cocked in that peculiar, downward-tilted way. I was a strange new phenomenon, a bringer of miniature suns. I let the second match burn out and then, deliberately, I lit a third, but this time I did not simply hold it. I used it to light a small, dry fragment of the cloth I had picked up. It flared with a brighter, more sustained yellow flame. Now the hissing stopped entirely. The clatter of the machines seemed to falter, as if the operators were distracted from their endless tasks. I had their full attention.

I laid the burning cloth upon the stone floor and gestured towards it with an open hand, an archaic pantomime of offering. I then pointed to myself, and then made a gesture of seeking, looking around me as if for something lost. It was a crude and perhaps foolish performance, but it was all I could devise. The Morlocks remained where they were, a silent congress of pallid faces and shining eyes, watching me from the edge of the light. Minutes passed. The flame on the cloth died down to a glowing ember and then went out. The tension was unbearable. Had I failed? Had they simply interpreted my actions as an eccentric threat?

Then, from the deepest shadows at the back of the hall, a new figure emerged. It was different from the others. It was older, I think; its skin seemed looser, its movements less skittish and more deliberate. It was no larger than the rest, but it carried itself with an air of authority, and the other Morlocks shuffled back to make way for it. It paused at the edge of the torchlight, its great, red-rimmed eyes fixing upon me with an unnerving, analytical stare. It was not merely seeing me; it was assessing me. Here, I felt, was the intelligence I had gambled on finding.

Slowly, it advanced. It did not run or scuttle, but walked with a strange, shambling gait, its long arms held low. It stopped a dozen feet from me and made a low, guttural sound. It was not a hiss or a squeal, but something more complex, a sound that might, after a million years of degradation, still be a vestige of speech. I felt a profound shudder of revulsion, but I stood my ground. This was the crucial moment.

I took out the box of matches again. I held it up for the creature to see. I shook it, so it could hear the rattle of the sticks inside. Then, with my other hand, I mimed the action of a lever, a short, sharp pull downwards. I pointed from the box, to myself, and then to the imaginary lever. I repeated the gesture twice more, a desperate piece of theatre in that cyclopean, stinking cavern.

The lead Morlock watched my every move. Its head tilted, and for a moment I thought it would screech and call the pack to attack me. But it did not. It emitted another of its low, guttural queries and took a step closer. It pointed a long, pale finger at the matchbox. Then, it pointed back into the darkness from which it had come. A negotiation. My mind reeled at the implication. Here, at the very end of the world, in this pit of horrors, the ancient art of the bargain was being reborn between the two sundered children of Mankind.

I nodded slowly, my throat too dry to make a sound. I held the matchbox out on my open palm. The creature stared at it, then at me. It then turned and, with a sharp bark, gestured to another, smaller Morlock that had been lurking in its shadow. It chittered something to this attendant and then pointed down a side tunnel. The smaller Morlock gave me a terrified, wide-eyed glance and then scurried off into the blackness.

The wait that followed was one of the most agonising periods of my life. I stood there, face to face with this chieftain, or foreman, or whatever he was, in a silent standoff. The torchlight flickered, my only shield. The great weaving machines had fallen completely silent now. The entire population of this dreadful workshop was watching the drama unfold. What if it was a trick? What if the other creature was returning with a host of its brethren to overwhelm me from behind? I kept glancing over my shoulder, but the tunnel of my retreat remained empty.

After what seemed an age, I heard a faint sound from the side passage: the scuttling of bare feet on stone. The small Morlock returned. And in its hand, it clutched a piece of metal that gleamed dully in the torchlight. It was my lever.

The small creature scurried to its master's side and handed over the prize. The lead Morlock took it. It did not look at the lever, but kept its luminous eyes fixed on me. It held the lever up, then pointed again, unequivocally, at the box of matches in my hand. The exchange. I took a slow step forward. The creature did the same. The stench of it was abominable, a sickly, musky odour of unwashed flesh and raw meat. I could see the fine, flaxen hairs on its stooped back, the dirt ingrained in its pale skin, the sharp, carnivorous set of its teeth. Overcoming a wave of nausea, I extended my hand with the box. It extended its, holding the lever.

Our fingers almost touched. The skin of its hand was cool and soft, like a toad's belly. I snatched the lever and it snatched the box of matches in the same instant. The transaction was complete. It immediately clutched the box to its chest, as a child might a new toy, and gave me one last, long, unreadable look. Then, with a series of sharp barks, it turned and shambled back into the darkness, its horde of followers melting away behind it. The clatter of the looms began again, hesitant at first, and then rising once more to its deafening, relentless rhythm.

I was alone again. I looked at the lever in my hand. The solid, familiar feel of the machined brass and ivory was the most reassuring sensation I had ever known. I had done it. I had faced the darkness and, for a price, I had won. I turned and fled back down the tunnel, not stopping until I was back in the small cavern with my Time Machine.

My hands trembled so violently I could barely work. I first set about restoring the quartz rods to their proper places, my fingers fumbling with the clamps. The Morlocks had loosened everything, but broken nothing. Their curiosity had been methodical. At last, I came to the lever. It slid back into its socket with a satisfying click. I was whole again. My escape was possible.

Even as I worked, a new fear grew in me. I had given them the secret of fire. What would they do with it? Would they burn the Overworld? Would they use it to gain mastery over their environment, to begin a new, terrible ascent? Or would they, in their ignorance, simply burn themselves and their world to ashes? I had given a box of matches to a child in a powder magazine. The thought was a chilling one, but my own survival was the paramount concern. I had to leave. Now.

I finished my work and scrambled into the saddle. My hand went to the newly restored lever. Just as my fingers closed around it, I heard a sound from the tunnel mouth. A scuttling, and the flash of great, luminous eyes. They had followed me. Or perhaps my bargain had not been sanctioned by all of them. A group of four or five Morlocks stood there, their expressions, if such a term could be applied, a mixture of curiosity and a new, predatory confidence. One of them held up a stick, at the end of which a piece of rag was tied. It was trying to make a torch. And another held a stone, tapping it against the rock wall with a clear, menacing intent. They were learning. They understood that I was leaving, and they understood, perhaps, that I was a source of knowledge they could not afford to lose.

They began to advance into the cavern. I had no time to think. I did not wait to see what they would do. With a cry that was half-triumph and half-terror, I thrust the lever forward.

There was the familiar lurch, the sickening sense of displacement. But it was different this time. The cavern did not simply melt away. Instead, a furious, desperate struggle seemed to ensue. The Morlocks had leaped upon the machine at the very instant of my departure. I felt their weight, their soft, detestable hands clawing at me, at the frame of the machine. The whole engine shuddered and groaned, and for one dreadful second I thought they would drag me back, or that the strain would shatter the quartz rods.

Then, the process took hold. The pull of the future, or the past, whichever way I was going, became irresistible. I saw one of the Morlocks, its face a mask of screaming confusion, lose its grip and fly backwards into the wall of the cavern, which was already becoming a streaky blur. Another clung to the rail beside me, its great eyes wide with a terror that superseded its predatory instinct. Its knuckles were white. Its whole body seemed to stretch, to elongate, as the machine and I were pulled violently out of its moment in time. Its grip failed. I had a final, nightmarish glimpse of its pale, human-spider form, arrested in the space I had just vacated, before it was lost to me forever.

The machine was free. The familiar greyish indistinctness closed in, the flickering alternation of day and night accelerating to a continuous vibration. The sounds of the Underworld were gone, replaced by the low, steady hum of my own velocity. I was moving through time again. I pushed the lever further, driving myself on, desperate to put as many years as possible between myself and that awful, cannibalistic future. I did not care where I landed; I only knew that I had to get away. I had seen the fate of Man, the grim, logical conclusion of our own societal divisions, and I could not bear the thought of it. The graceful, childlike Eloi and the grim, industrious Morlocks; they were our children, the two halves of our nature sent to their appalling dooms. And I, for a moment, had stood between them, a ghost from the past who had traded the fire of the gods for a simple brass lever, and in so doing, had perhaps sealed their fates for good. The thought was a burden I would carry for all the days I had left.

The machine pitched like a stricken beast, throwing me against the brass rail. I clung to it, my knuckles white, as the very fabric of existence seemed to tear and re-form around me. Slowly, shakily, I rose to my feet. The air that met my lungs was thick, heavy, and humid, carrying a strange, coppery tang and the overwhelming scent of damp earth and decay. It was not the fresh, sweet air of the Eloi’s world, but a ponderous atmosphere that seemed to press upon the skin. I looked out from the frame of my machine. I had come to rest in what appeared to be a vast, shallow valley, though the contours were soft and indistinct. The sun, a pale and swollen orb, hung low in a sky the colour of sulphur, its light diffused and wan, as if seen through a cataract. There were no trees. There were no grasses. The entire landscape, as far as the eye could see, was covered in a prodigious growth of what I could only describe as fungus. Great grey and purple stalks, some as thick as a man’s body, rose from the ground, branching into bulbous, sponge-like caps. The ground itself was not soil but a matted, rubbery carpet of mycelium, pulsing with faint, circulatory patterns of a darker hue. The silence was not, I now realised, an absence of sound, but an absence of familiar sound. There was no birdsong, no insect-hum. Instead, a low, almost subliminal thrumming seemed to emanate from the ground beneath my feet, a deep and resonant vibration that I felt more in my bones than heard with my ears.

My machine had settled on a relatively clear patch of this fungal mat, crushing a section of it. A thick, milky sap, phosphorescent and pale, oozed from the wounded flesh of the world. I stepped out, my feet sinking slightly into the spongy surface. The sheer alienness of the scene was overwhelming. In the world of 802,701, despite its strangeness, I had recognised the descendants of Man, the ruins of our architecture, the ghost of our world. Here, there was nothing. This was a planet that had forgotten humanity entirely. It was a world that had been reclaimed not by the jungle or the ice, but by a different kingdom of life altogether. I thought of the endless, silent wars fought in my own time in the soil beneath my feet; the struggle between plant root and fungal network. It appeared that in this distant future, the fungi had won. And they had won completely.

I turned my attention to the Time Machine. My heart sank. The stopping lever was indeed immovable, fused into its setting. The cracked quartz rod was dark and inert. The damage was severe, perhaps irreparable. To be stranded was a possibility I had never truly allowed myself to contemplate. Now, it was a stark and terrifying reality. I was a castaway on an island in time, an island thirty million years wide. My first instinct was to despair, but the scientific habit of mind is a difficult one to break. I was here. I was alive. And before me lay the greatest biological mystery I had ever encountered. What manner of ecosystem was this? What had happened to the animal kingdom? What fed upon these colossal growths, and what, in turn, fed upon them?

I began a cautious exploration, my nerves stretched taut. I walked for what must have been a mile, the landscape unchanging in its grotesque uniformity. The air was still and breathless. The only movement was the slow, almost imperceptible pulsation in the ground-mat and the faint, rhythmic luminescence that now seemed to be glowing within the caps of the larger stalks. The light waxed and waned in slow, complex patterns, like a vast and silent conversation. The thought was absurd, and yet I could not shake it. Could it be that this entire world was not a collection of individual organisms, but a single, contiguous entity? A planet-spanning creature of which these stalks were merely the fruiting bodies? My mind, accustomed to categorising the world into flora and fauna, struggled for purchase. This was something other.

I came upon what I at first took for a river, a broad channel of darker, glistening material cutting through the grey plain. But as I drew closer, I saw that it was not water. It was a stream of the same phosphorescent, milky sap I had seen bleeding from the wound my machine had made, but here it flowed with purpose, a sluggish, thick current moving through a defined channel. And along its banks, there was activity. Small, semi-translucent globes, the size of a man’s head, were budding from the mycelial mat. They swelled and pulsed with the internal light, and as I watched, one of them detached itself and rolled slowly into the sluggish river, where it was borne away by the current. It was a form of propagation, I surmised. Spores on a scale I could scarcely imagine.

As I stood, mesmerised by this silent, orderly process, a new sensation intruded. It was not a sound, nor a sight. It was a pressure, a focused attention. The thrumming in the ground beneath my feet seemed to concentrate, to localise directly under me. The rhythmic pulsing of the lights in the fungal stalks around me altered. The slow, languid patterns became faster, more agitated, and they seemed to be oriented towards me. I felt a profound sense of being watched, examined, scrutinised, not by a pair of eyes, but by the entire landscape. It was an inchoate, voiceless query, a wave of pure information pressing in upon my mind. It was not hostile, not yet; it was profoundly, overwhelmingly curious. This vast, silent intelligence had become aware of me.

I was an anomaly. A foreign body. In a world that was a single, networked consciousness, I was a point of radical individuality, a rogue thought. The pressure in my mind intensified. It was not a language of words, but of raw concepts; questions of chemistry, of structure, of origin. My own thoughts felt clumsy and loud in the face of this silent, sophisticated interrogation. I felt like a barbarian shouting in a library. I stumbled back, away from the river of sap, my heart hammering against my ribs. The fear I felt was of a different order to the terror the Morlocks had inspired. That was a primal, physical dread. This was an existential horror, the fear of a single soul on the verge of being absorbed into a boundless, alien mind.

My retreat seemed to trigger a change in its response. The curiosity was replaced by something else, something I could only interpret as analysis, and then, decision. The ground before me began to swell. The rubbery mat heaved upwards, forming a wall, a soft, pliable barrier to block my path back to the Time Machine. It rose without a sound, a living wave of fungus. I turned and ran, my feet slipping on the slick surface, my breath catching in the thick, heavy air. The world itself was rising to stop me. Other walls were rising to my left and right, not aggressively, but with a calm, inexorable certainty, funnelling me away from my machine, guiding me towards a part of the valley where the fungal stalks grew in a dense, dark forest.

The communication, if it could be called that, came again, clearer this time. It was an impulse not to harm, but to understand. To understand, it seemed, by assimilation. By dissection. By absorption. I was a puzzle it wished to solve by taking it apart. I was a rogue cell in a body that had decided upon its excision. I ran with the desperation of a man who is being hunted not by a predator, but by the very ground he runs upon. The air grew thicker with spores, making me cough and gasp. The light from the fungal caps pulsed in a dizzying, hypnotic rhythm, trying to confuse my senses.

I remembered my matches. I had used them to amuse the Eloi, to frighten the Morlocks. They were a remnant of my own age, a symbol of man’s mastery over fire. Here, in this damp, saturated world, would they even strike? Fumbling in my pocket, my fingers closed around the small, precious box. I pulled it out, and with a trembling hand, I struck a match against its side. It failed. I tried again. A tiny, sputtering flame burst into life, a pinprick of harsh, yellow light in this world of cool, biological luminescence.

The effect was instantaneous and shocking. The thrumming ceased. The pressure on my mind vanished. The walls of fungus before me seemed to recoil, to shrink back from the tiny flame as if from a blow. The very air around my hand felt suddenly thin and clear. For this world, this creature, fire was something utterly alien. It was a process of rapid, chaotic oxidation that it had never encountered, a reaction wholly outside its slow, deliberate chemistry of life. In my hand, I held a concept it could not understand, and which it feared.

Emboldened, I struck another match, and then another, holding the small torch before me as I ran. The path before me cleared. The living walls sank back into the ground, the agitated lights calmed. I had found a weapon. I did not stop running until the familiar, incongruous shape of my Time Machine was in sight. It stood where I had left it, a thing of brass and ivory and quartz, a relic of an age of metal and fire and individual minds, marooned in a world of flesh and water and collective thought.

I clambered back into the driver’s seat, my body shaking with exhaustion and reaction. The silence from the world-creature was absolute. It was waiting. Watching. It had learned of my existence, and now it had learned of my defence. But I knew this was only a temporary reprieve. My matches were finite. My energy was finite. But the creature, the world itself, had all the time there was. It could simply wait for my little flame to die out. I looked at the broken lever, the cracked rod. My problem was no longer simply to escape this time, but to escape this place. I had to repair the machine. I had to pit my single, frantic human intellect, the product of a billion years of frantic, competitive evolution, against a slow, thoughtful, planetary mind that had never known a rival. And as I opened the panel to the machine’s inner workings, my hands still trembling, I did not know which of us was the more advanced, nor which, in the end, would be proven the more fit to survive. The sulphur-coloured sun cast my long, lonely shadow across the pulsing, living ground, and I began my work.

And then it tried a new tactic. It was not a pressure, not a wordless command, but a soft, seductive murmur that bloomed in the quiet corridors of my mind. I reached the ruins without further incident, though the sense of being gently and probingly examined never left me. They were even more vast and desolate than I remembered. Great blocks of masonry lay tumbled about like a giant’s playthings, their surfaces veiled by the ubiquitous, pulsing growth. Here and there, however, were stones the fungus had not yet conquered, their hard, crystalline faces exposed to the sulphur-coloured sun. I found what I was looking for: several flat, thick pieces of basalt, which I could arrange into a small, enclosed hearth. The labour was immense. The stones were heavy, and the simple act of lifting and carrying them left me gasping for breath, my muscles screaming in protest. All the while, the world watched. I felt no direct interference, but there was a quality to the light, a rhythm in the air, that seemed to sap my strength and sow seeds of doubt in my mind.

Fuel was the next problem. The living fungus was too damp, too saturated with the planet’s vital fluids, to burn. But as I searched among the ruins, I came upon a sheltered overhang where a section of the fungal carpet had died. Perhaps it had been cut off from the planet’s collective life, or had been scorched by some ancient cataclysm. Whatever the reason, it had dried into a brittle, cork-like substance, light and porous. I broke off a piece and put a match to it. It did not merely burn; it flared into life with a startling, almost explosive intensity, giving off a sickly green flame and a plume of thick, acrid smoke. The heat was ferocious. It was not the clean, honest fire of wood, but the volatile, chemical combustion of a strange biology. It was perfect.

I spent the next several hours in a fever of activity, a Prometheus in reverse, bringing a forgotten and unwelcome fire back to a world that had evolved beyond it. I built my small furnace, a crude but effective kiln of basalt slabs, leaving a channel at the bottom to allow air to feed the flame. I gathered a great pile of the dried fungus. Then, with everything in readiness, I returned to the Time Machine to retrieve the precious, broken rod. My heart was a leaden weight in my chest. The next few moments would determine everything.

I placed the two halves of the quartz rod upon a flat piece of stone I would use as an anvil, and pushed it carefully into the heart of my kiln. Then, taking a deep breath, I lit the pile of fungus. The effect was immediate. A great whoosh of green and violet flame erupted from the furnace, the heat so intense that I had to shield my face. The air shimmered, and the stones of the kiln began to glow a dull red. The light from the surrounding world-creature, which had been a steady, observant pulse, suddenly began to race. The colours shifted from cool blues and greens to agitated reds and oranges. And then, the assault began anew.

It was no longer seductive. It was a desperate, frantic, full-frontal attack upon my senses and my sanity. The vision of the garden was gone, replaced by a whirlwind of images torn from the deepest parts of my soul. I saw my laboratory, my books and instruments scattered and broken. I heard the voices of my friends, of Filby and the Medical Man, not as they were, but as cruel parodies, their faces twisted in mocking laughter at my absurd ambition. Their words echoed in my head, not as sound, but as pure, injected thought: *Give up. It is hopeless. You are a fool. You are alone. Come back to us. This is madness.*

I gritted my teeth, the smoke and heat stinging my eyes. I used the metal bar I had brought to poke and stir the fire, to drive the temperature higher still. The quartz within the furnace was beginning to glow, a faint cherry-red that seemed to mock the inferno around it. The psychic bombardment intensified. The creature was throwing everything it had at me, all the fear and doubt and loneliness I carried within me. But even in its perfection, there was a flaw. The Weena I had known was a creature of sunlight and simple joys, but she was also a creature of fear, of an instinctive dread of the dark and the unknown. “You will not have me!” I roared, to the fire, to the silent, living world. “You will not use her against me!”

With a cry that was half sob, half battle-cry, I thrust my head and shoulders into the blinding heat, shielding my eyes with one arm. The pulsing lights of the fungus went dark. I had done it.

I pulled the rod from the furnace and dropped it onto the cool basalt, then staggered back, collapsing to my knees, my body shaking with a violent ague. I was drained, not merely of physical strength, but of some deeper, essential energy. The battle had been fought not in the world of matter, but in the landscape of the mind, and I had won. But at what cost?

Slowly, as the furnace fire died down to a sullen green glow, the world came back to life. The fungal caps began to pulse again, but tentatively, weakly. The light was a dim, bruised purple. The silence remained. The mental connection was gone. Whether the creature had severed it, or I had broken it, I did not know. I had hurt it. I had driven a spike of alien, violent energy into its slow, contemplative existence, and it had recoiled in pain and shock.

I waited until the quartz had cooled enough to touch, then picked it up. It was whole. A milky, scarred seam ran through its center, a testament to its ordeal, but it was solid. I did not know if the repair would hold, if the internal structure was too compromised for the delicate work of temporal travel. But it was my only hope.

I gathered my things and stumbled back towards the Time Machine, leaving the dying embers of my forge like a wound on the living skin of the planet. Dust, the fine grey powder of ages, lay thick upon everything; it carpeted the floor in a soft, soundless layer and draped the exhibits in ghostly shrouds. Our footsteps made no sound. It was a gallery of Palaeontology, preserved with a strange and terrible perfection. Weena, however, took no interest in these marvels of a former world. The bones only frightened her. She pointed with a shaking finger at the hollow eye sockets of the Megatherium and hid her face in my coat. I felt a surge of confidence, of power. I was no longer a blind man stumbling in the gloom. I now possessed a portable fire.

Then, we came to it. A hall of machinery. It was vaster than all the others, a cavern of steel and brass. Another was fused with rust into a single, solid piece. Whatever explosives they might have used had long since decomposed into inert powder. Humanity had forgotten warfare so completely that it had not even bothered to preserve its tools.

It was about a yard long, and thickened at one end into a heavy knob. It was not a finely balanced weapon, but it was sturdier and better shaped for a bludgeon than my simple iron bar. I set my lump of camphor down on a metal casing, where its light cast a steady glow. I jammed the end of my old bar into the joint of the new lever and heaved with all my might. The metal groaned in protest. I put my whole weight into it, my feet slipping on the dusty floor. There was a sharp crack, and the lever broke free from its housing.

I picked it up. It was heavy, but balanced enough. It felt good in my hand. It felt powerful. I had my weapon. I had my fire. It was time to leave. I had pushed my luck far enough in this mausoleum of the past. The sun would be setting in a few hours, and I had no intention of being caught in this vast, dark place when the Morlocks came out to hunt.

I turned back to retrieve my camphor torch, and as I did so, my blood ran cold.

Weena was no longer where I had left her. For a moment I felt a surge of blind panic. “Weena!” I called out, my voice swallowed by the immense space.

The others fell back for a moment, chittering with agitation, their ranks disturbed by the sudden violence.

A hand, soft and fleshy, with claw-like nails, grabbed at my leg. I kicked out viciously and it released its grip.

They scrambled over their fallen comrade, their big eyes glowing with a dreadful, luminous intensity in the light of my torch. They were not strong, individually, but there were so many of them. I felt their damp, soft bodies brushing against me. Their proximity, the foul, faint odour of them, filled me with a profound nausea.

We could not win this fight, a war of attrition against a seemingly endless supply of these subterranean horrors. Our only hope was the exit, the daylight.

“This way!” I shouted, though Weena could not understand.

They were like ghouls, their bleached faces turning away from the light, but their hands still reached for us from the shadows. We fought our way out of the hall of machines and into the gallery of minerals. The tight confines of the corridor worked to my advantage, funnelling our attackers. They could not swarm us from all sides here. I kept them at bay with the fire, while constantly looking over my shoulder to ensure the way was clear. I fumbled in my pocket for the jar, an almost impossible task while holding a mace and a shrieking Eloi. My fingers closed around it.

At that moment, a Morlock leaped upon my back. Its weight bore me down to one knee. I felt its damp chin on my shoulder, its foul breath in my ear. Its long fingers scrabbled for my throat. Weena shrieked and beat at it with her tiny fists. With a roar of rage and disgust, I threw myself backwards, slamming my attacker against the massive leg-bone of the giant Saurian. It released its grip with a gurgling cry. I scrambled up, swinging the mace blindly.

The daylight! We had to reach the daylight!

Holding Weena tight against my side, I made a final, desperate run for the entrance. The Morlocks surged after us, their chittering reaching a crescendo. They seemed to know their time was short, that their prey was about to escape into the hated glare of the sun. Hands clutched at my coat, tearing the fabric. I did not dare to look back. I fixed my eyes on that oblong of brilliant, beautiful light, the symbol of my own world, of reason, of safety.

Ten yards. Five yards. We burst out of the archway and into the blinding sun. I stumbled and fell to my knees on the hot stone of the steps, blinking in the sudden, painful glare. The change from the deep gloom to the full heat of the afternoon was staggering.

I turned, my weapon at the ready. The Morlocks had stopped at the threshold. They crowded in the darkness of the entrance, a squirming mass of pale bodies and blinking eyes, hating the light that had saved us. For a moment, we stared at each other, the pursued and the pursuers. Then, one by one, they melted back into the shadows of the museum.

I remained on my knees for a long time, gasping for breath, the heavy mace on the step beside me. The camphor in my hand had burned out completely. Weena was sobbing quietly, her face buried in my shoulder. I looked up at the sun, a blazing, indifferent god in the blue sky. We were safe, for now. But the Palace of Green Porcelain was no longer a repository of ancient wonders to me. It was a lair. A tomb. And I knew, with a dreadful certainty, that I would have to go back. Not for knowledge, not for tools, but for my Time Machine. They had it. I was sure of it now. They had dragged it down into their dark, subterranean world, and the entrance to that world, I now suspected, lay somewhere in the depths of that green and silent palace. I had found my weapons, but in doing so, I had merely confirmed the nature of the war I had to fight. And as the sun began its slow descent towards the west, I gathered my small, trusting companion close, and began to scan the landscape for a place to withstand the siege of the coming night.
