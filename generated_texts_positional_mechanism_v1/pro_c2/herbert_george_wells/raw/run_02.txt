I cannot explain it. It may be that the planet itself was younger, or that its core possessed some geothermal ferocity long lost to our own Earth. It is a common assumption that a world, once cooled, remains placid and predictable in its geology. But those who study the grand, slow violence of celestial mechanics, such as the theories of the great Lyell, understand that planetary life is a series of epochs, not a steady state. As a world’s inner pressures shift, its surface may buckle and break in new and startling ways; and it may be that this world was in the throes of such a tectonic youth. Whatever the cause, the fact is that the ground beneath my feet was alive with a faint, resonant hum.

Well, one particularly vibrant afternoon—my tenth cycle, I believe—as I was cataloguing the flora in a valley of shimmering, crystalline grasses, there occurred a most peculiar event. Weaving through these strange, glassy stalks, which chimed faintly with every step, I found a deep chasm, its far side lost in the purple haze of the atmosphere. By contrast with the jewelled brightness of the valley, it seemed at first an abyss of absolute black. I approached its edge cautiously, for the shift from the bright world to that dark void made shimmering motes dance before my eyes. Suddenly I stopped, transfixed. A single point of soft, blue light, pulsing with a slow and steady rhythm, was glowing in the depths.

The old explorer’s impulse to classify and understand surged within me. I secured my harness to a rocky outcrop and leaned over the precipice. I was too far to get a clear look. Then the thought of the complete alienness of this world, and my utter solitude, gave me a moment’s pause. And then I remembered my mission’s primary directive: to observe and record. Overcoming my hesitation to some degree, I unspooled a length of cable and began my descent. I will confess that my breathing was shallow and quick. I extended a hand downwards, into the cold air rising from below. At once the light blinked out, and a low, mournful tone echoed up from the chasm. I froze with my heart in my throat, and saw a ripple spread across the darkness; not a ripple of water, but of shadow itself. It climbed the far wall of the chasm, resolved itself into a definite shape, and in a moment was clinging to the rock face opposite me, camouflaged almost perfectly against the stone.

My impression of it is, naturally, fleeting; but I know it was a deep obsidian black, and had a faceted, insect-like shell; also that it possessed several long, delicate limbs that unfolded like a fan. But, as I say, its change was too swift for me to see with clarity. I cannot even say whether it was a single being, or a colony of smaller things acting as one. After a moment’s paralysis I continued my descent into the chasm. I could not find it at first; but, after my eyes adjusted to the profound gloom, I saw one of those strange, porous rock formations I had noted earlier, honeycombed with perfectly circular tunnels. A sudden thought struck me. Could this entity have retreated into the rock itself? I activated my helmet’s lamp, and, peering inside, I saw a complex, moving lattice, pulsing with that same soft, blue light which regarded my intrusion with a silent, unified glow. It made me recoil. It was so like a living circuit! It was flowing deeper into the warren of tunnels, and now I saw for the first time a network of silvery filaments connecting each pulsing node. Then my lamp flickered as a strange energy field interfered with it, and when the beam stabilised the network had gone dark.

I do not know how long I hung there, staring into that geological hive. It was not for some time that I could bring myself to accept that the thing I had seen was life. But, gradually, the truth began to form in my mind: that Life on this world had not evolved into distinct flora and fauna, but had merged into a single, symbiotic existence: that the crystalline grasses on the surface were not the world’s dominant life form, but that this lithic, sentient, subterranean network, which had flashed before me, was the true inheritor of the planet.

I thought of the resonant hum of the ground, and of my theory of geothermal activity. I began to suspect its real source. And what, I wondered, was this silent intelligence doing in my tidy biological classifications? How was it related to the passive, chiming beauty of the surface world? And what purpose was hidden in those depths? I hung there upon my cable telling myself that, at any rate, there was no sign of aggression, and that I must explore further for the solution to my questions. And yet I was utterly terrified to proceed! As I wavered, a tremor ran through the rock face to which I clung, and a soft, chiming melody rose from the valley above.

The grasses seemed to sway in a pattern, their crystalline structures resonating with the sound from below. Apparently it was some form of communication; for when I directed my lamp back into the tunnel, a faint, blue pulse answered from within the darkness. But the light was different now, inquisitive, and it did not retreat. I tried to analyse the phenomenon with my instruments, and I failed. So presently I ascended, meaning to go back to the lander, and review my data. But my mind was in a state of upheaval; my assumptions and hypotheses were shattering and reforming into a new schema. I had now a clue to the import of the humming, to the porous rocks, to the mystery of the chimes; to say nothing of a hint at the meaning of the planet’s strange geology and the absence of any animal life! And very vaguely there came a suggestion towards the solution of the great biological question that had vexed me.

Here was the new perspective. Plainly, this second form of life was geological. There were three circumstances in particular which made me think that its rare appearances were but minor expressions of a vast, planet-spanning consciousness. In the first place, there was the networked structure I had witnessed, common in many terrestrial fungi and root systems, but on a scale I could scarcely imagine. Then, those pulsing lights, with their capacity for unified action, are a common feature of bioluminescent organisms—witness our own deep-sea creatures. And last of all, that evident connection to the surface flora, that call and response between the dark and the light—all reinforced the theory of a singular, composite entity.

Beneath my feet, then, the very crust of the planet must be threaded with life, and these tunnels were its nervous system. The presence of the resonant grasses across the plains—everywhere, in fact, except in the deepest chasms—showed how universal was its surface expression. What so natural, then, as to assume that it was this subterranean mind that cultivated the crystal fields that fed it light and energy? The notion was so staggering that I at once accepted it, and went on to speculate on the _how_ of this planetary sentience. I dare say you can imagine the shape of my theory; though, for myself, I soon felt it was but a crude approximation of the truth.

At first, proceeding from the biological models of our own world, it seemed clear as day to me that the gradual fusion of the ancient, separate domains of plant, animal, and fungus was the key to the whole system. No doubt it will seem absurd enough to you—and biologically heretical!—and yet even now there are terrestrial phenomena that point that way. There is a tendency for organisms to form ever more complex symbiotic relationships; there are the lichens of our arctic regions, for instance, there are coral reefs, there are our own gut biomes, and they are ever more critical to our understanding of life. Evidently, I thought, this tendency had progressed until the very distinction between organism and environment had dissolved. I mean that life had gone deeper into the planet’s own substance, becoming one with the rock and soil, till, in the end—! Even now, does not a vast forest act in some ways as a single entity, sharing resources through its roots, practically indistinguishable from the ecosystem it creates?

Again, the solitary nature of complex animals—due, no doubt, to the high metabolic cost of individuality and the pressures of competition—is already being challenged by the efficiency of collective existence. About our world, for instance, perhaps the most successful creatures are the social insects. And this same efficiency—which is due to the division of labour and the subsuming of the individual to the whole—leads towards a loss of that very self-awareness we hold so dear.

What, then, was the purpose of this strange, beautiful, and ultimately vacant race of the sunlight? At first I had assumed them the masters, the inheritors, the final product of civilisation. Now I saw them in a new and more terrible light. They were not the gardeners; they were the prize blooms. They were not the shepherds; they were the cosseted flock. I saw with a sudden, chilling clarity the logic of this planetary symbiosis. The subterranean world toiled, not for wages or from coercion as we would understand it, but from the simple, overwhelming biological imperative that drives the root to seek water and the leaf to seek light. It laboured to maintain the surface, to cultivate the crystal fields, and to sustain these delicate, useless creatures. The beautiful people, in their turn, provided… what? I could not yet be certain. Perhaps their simple, joyous lives generated some psychic energy, some subtle warmth that the vast, cool mind below craved. Or perhaps, and the thought made me shudder, they were merely a harvest.

This new theory, vast and unsettling as it was, seemed to fit the facts more completely than my last. It accounted for the perfect, unmanaged beauty of the world, for the indolent bliss of the upperworlders, and for the fleeting, purposeful horror of the thing in the dark. My mind reeled with the immensity of it all. I had stumbled upon an Earth that had resolved the chaotic struggle of evolution into a single, terrifyingly perfect organism. The planet itself had become the ultimate individual, and all that lived upon and within it were but its cells, its organs, its thoughts made manifest.

Yet for all my theorising, one thing remained: I must know. I must have proof. The mystery of the well, of the shaft leading down into the planet’s living heart, was no longer a mere curiosity; it was the central question of this entire future world. I looked again at the dark aperture, half-obscured by the fallen pillar. The faint, cool air that breathed from it seemed to call to me, a silent invitation into the profoundest of secrets. I felt a dread that was deeper than any simple fear of beasts or darkness. It was a philosophical terror, a fear of losing my own defined self in the face of a consciousness so vast and so alien.

And withal I was absolutely afraid to go! I, a man of science, a creature of reason and empirical fact, hesitated on the brink of the greatest discovery of all time. I sat there, my hand upon the stone, my mind a battlefield of curiosity and horror. As I did so, another pair of the beautiful surface-dwellers came into the shadowed ruin. They were, as ever, engaged in some light-hearted chase, their laughter like the chiming of small, perfect bells. The female, her hair a cascade of gold, ran from the male, who playfully tossed petals of a scarlet flower at her.

They stopped short when they saw me, their mirth instantly extinguished. They looked from my grim face to the well, and a look of genuine panic, of deep instinctual revulsion, crossed their features. They made little cooing sounds of distress, not as one reprimands a social error, but as a bird might cry at a hand reaching for its nest. The male took the female’s arm and gently tried to pull her away from the sight. I pointed again to the well, attempting their soft, liquid language, trying to form a question. The effect was instantaneous and profound. They both flinched as if I had struck them, their faces paling. They would not look at the opening. They turned their backs to it, their distress so palpable it was almost a physical force in the air.

I abandoned the attempt. It was clear that to them, this aperture was not merely ignored; it was a source of primal dread, a thing so fundamentally wrong that their simple minds could not even process its existence. They were creatures of the sun, and the very idea of the dark below was a violation of their nature. I took out my box of matches, as I had done before, and struck one. The flare of it immediately broke their trance. Their fear was forgotten, replaced by that innocent, childlike fascination. They drew near, their eyes wide, as I lit another, and another. I saw then the impassable gulf between us. I was a creature of questions; they were creatures of sensation. I sought the _why_ of the darkness, while they fled from it, only to be captivated by a momentary, meaningless light.

Presently they tired of my game and, with a final, nervous glance towards the well, they drifted away, their amorous sport resumed as if their moment of terror had never happened. I was alone again with the silence and the secret. Their reaction had only strengthened my resolve. Come what may, I told myself, I would not be a mere butterfly, flitting ignorantly over a world I did not comprehend. I would be a man; I would know. I would descend that shaft.

My heart hammered against my ribs as I made my preparations, which were simple enough. I had the box of matches, my only source of light, which I secured in an inner pocket of my coat. I peered over the edge once more. The metal rungs I had seen before formed a ladder descending into the absolute black. They seemed old, yet not corroded, and were spaced for a creature of roughly my own size. Gripping the topmost rungs, which were cool and strangely smooth to the touch, I swung my legs over the side and began to go down.

The air grew cooler almost at once. The brilliant sunlight of the upper world was cut off, and I was enveloped in a blackness more profound than any night I had ever known. For the first dozen yards, the circular opening above was a blinding disc of blue and white, but it shrank rapidly, and soon was but a distant, pale star. The only sounds were the scuff of my boots against the wall of the shaft and my own ragged breathing, which seemed unnervously loud in the oppressive silence. The rungs were firm under my hands and feet. They did not feel like worked metal; they had a peculiar texture, as if they had been extruded from the very substance of the wall, an organic part of the structure.

I descended for what felt like a great time. I paused occasionally, my muscles aching, and struck a match. Its brief, flickering life would illuminate a few yards of the cylindrical shaft, showing me the endless repetition of the ladder and the strange, almost polished texture of the walls. There was no sign of life, no sound save my own. Then, as I went deeper still, I began to be aware of a new sensation. It was not a sound, not at first, but a vibration. It came up through the soles of my feet and the palms of my hands, a deep, steady, sub-audible hum. It was the pulse of the world.

As I continued my descent, the hum grew in intensity until it was a low, resonant note that seemed to fill my entire body. And the darkness, I realised, was no longer absolute. Faint, ghostly lines of phosphorescence began to appear in the walls of the shaft, weaving in intricate, web-like patterns. They pulsed with a slow, rhythmic light, in perfect time with the hum that vibrated through me. The light was a cold, blue-green, like the bioluminescence of the deepest sea. It was as if I were descending into the vascular system of some colossal creature, and these were its glowing veins.

I do not know how far I went. Hundreds of feet, certainly; perhaps thousands. The air changed. It grew warmer again, and thick, heavy with a faint, loamy smell, like a forest floor after a great rain, but with an undercurrent that was metallic and strange. I paused, clinging to the ladder, and looked down. Far below, the darkness gave way to a soft, diffuse glow. I was nearing the source of the light, the heart of the humming. My fear was now almost entirely replaced by a breathless, scientific eagerness. I quickened my pace.

Suddenly, something white flashed in the darkness beside me. I cried out in alarm, my hand slipping on a rung. It was one of the ape-like creatures, the bleached thing I had seen in the ruins. It was clambering down the ladder past me with a swift, unnerving agility, its large, dark eyes fixing me for a moment with a look of utter indifference before it continued its descent. It made no sound. It was not a predator hunting me, nor a frightened creature fleeing. It was merely a cell, a corpuscle, moving through an artery on some errand I could not fathom. It soon vanished into the glow below.

My journey ended abruptly. The ladder stopped, and my feet touched a solid surface. I stood at the mouth of the shaft, which opened into the roof of a cavern so vast that my mind struggled to grasp its scale. I was struck dumb with wonder. I stood on a gallery of sorts, a balcony of the same dark material as the shaft, which overlooked a scene of the most breathtaking and alien beauty.

The cavern was not a place of dead stone, but a place that lived. The floor, far below me, was a forest of crystalline structures that rose like great trees towards the roof. They were not transparent, but milky and opalescent, and from within them came the soft, blue-green light that illuminated the entire immense space. Connecting these crystal-trees were thick, rope-like cables of the same luminous mycelia I had seen in the shaft walls, forming a network that criss-crossed the cavern in every direction. And the air, from wall to wall, was filled with the deep, organ-like hum I had felt on my descent. This was the source. This was the engine room of the world.

And everywhere, moving through this glowing forest, were the white creatures. There were thousands of them. From my vantage point, I could see them clearly in the diffuse light. They moved with an unthinking, unified purpose across the cavern floor and along the glowing cables. They were tending the crystal-trees. Some seemed to be pruning them with their hands, breaking off small, duller fragments which they carried away into darker side-tunnels. Others were gathered at the bases of the largest crystals, stroking them, their movements slow and methodical, like farmers tending some strange crop. They were workers. They were the ganglia of this planetary brain, the mitochondria of this terrestrial cell.

I watched them for a long time, mesmerised. I saw now the perfect, terrible logic of it all. The shafts, the wells, were not merely for ventilation. They were light-wells. They funnelled the sun’s energy down from the surface, and these crystalline structures, this fungal-mineral forest, converted that light into the energy that powered the entire system. The white creatures, the Morlock-things, were the gardeners, the technicians, the mindless custodians of the machinery. And the fruit of their labour, the purpose of this whole vast enterprise? I could not be sure, but I suspected it was some subtle nectar or energy that was conveyed upwards to nourish the beautiful, useless people of the sunlight, completing the circuit of this perfectly balanced, perfectly inhuman world.

I had solved the economic problem. It was not a matter of capital and labour; it was a matter of photosynthesis and metabolism. But in my moment of intellectual triumph, a cold, practical fear reasserted itself. Where was my Time Machine?

I had last seen it in the house of the bronze gates, the great green structure that I now suspected was a primary processing centre, or perhaps a temple, in this bizarre world. I began to walk along the gallery, which seemed to circle the entire cavern. I was searching for some sign, some clue to the fate of my only hope of escape. The white creatures below paid me no mind. I was as insignificant to them as a dust mote.

I walked for what must have been a mile, the same incredible vista always to my side. Then, in a recess of the cavern wall, I saw it. I stopped, my heart leaping with a sudden, desperate hope. It was the familiar outline of my machine, the glint of quartz and nickel and ivory, unmistakable even in the strange, blue light. It was not damaged. It was simply… there.

But as I drew closer, my hope turned to a new and more profound horror. The machine was not merely sitting on the rock. Thick, luminous tendrils of the living mycelium grew from the floor and walls of the recess and had wrapped themselves around the framework. They were not crushing it; they were integrating it. They snaked around the saddle, through the intricate brasswork, and into the very housing of the quartz rods. The entire machine was caught in a glowing, pulsating web. The hum of the cavern seemed to be concentrated here, and the metal of my machine was vibrating in sympathy with it. I reached out a trembling hand and touched the time lever. It was warm, and it was no longer bare metal. It was coated in a thin, living, organic film.

The truth dawned on me with the force of a physical blow. The machine had not been stolen by these creatures out of malice or curiosity. It had been brought here, to the central brain of the world, to be studied. To be assimilated. The planetary intelligence had encountered a piece of technology utterly alien to its experience, a device that operated on principles beyond its biological frame of reference, and it was doing the only thing it knew how to do: it was incorporating it. It was connecting it to its neural network, feeling it, tasting it, learning it. It was digesting my invention.

I stood frozen before it, all my theories, all my intellectual pride, draining away to leave only a cold, stark terror. My machine, my singular, brilliant creation, my only bridge back to my own time, to humanity, was becoming an organ in the body of this new and silent god. I had feared the loss of my life to the fangs of some degenerate beast, but this was a threat infinitely more terrible. This was the loss of my escape, the absorption of my knowledge, the slow, patient, and inexorable consumption of my last and only hope. I was not just a castaway in the future; I was a specimen, and my greatest tool was being dissected by an intelligence as vast and as unfeeling as a geological age.

My mind, which had moments before been a whirl of speculation and triumphant discovery, was now a void of cold, hard fact. The fact was this: I was trapped, and the very instrument of my freedom was being unmade, or rather remade, into something else. The first shock gave way to a surge of frantic energy. I could not simply stand and watch this monstrous digestion take place. I flung myself at the machine, my hands scrabbling for a purchase on the smooth, alien growths that ensnared it. I seized a thick, luminous cord that had wound itself about the saddle-post and pulled with all my strength.

The tendril was surprisingly supple, yet it possessed the unyielding tenacity of woven steel. It was warm to the touch, and I felt a faint, rhythmic pulse within it, a slow and steady beat that was the lifeblood of this subterranean world. It did not yield; indeed, it seemed to cling the tighter, and a low thrumming vibration passed from it into my hands and up my arms. The light of the cavern, which had been a constant, dim blue, seemed to intensify, shifting in hue towards a deeper violet. The great hum that filled the air changed its pitch, rising to a note of questioning, of inquiry.

And then I perceived a new and more dreadful thing. Below me, on the vast floor of the cavern, the activity of the white Morlocks had ceased. In their thousands they had stopped their inscrutable labours. A universal stillness fell upon them, and in that silence, a thousand pairs of large, greyish-red eyes turned upwards. They were all looking at me. It was not the curious gaze of an animal, nor the hostile glare of an enemy. It was a single, coordinated act of attention, as if a million scattered cells of a single body had suddenly focused upon a foreign particle. They were the white corpuscles of this planetary organism, and I was the invading microbe.

A cold sweat broke out upon my brow. I let go of the tendril as if it were a hot coal. The creatures below began to move. Not with the scuttling haste I had witnessed in the upper world, but with a slow, deliberate purpose. From all across the great cavern floor, streams of them began to converge upon the base of the colossal stalagmite upon which the recess was perched. They moved in silent droves, their pallid bodies a flowing river of white in the violet gloom. They were coming for me.

My fear was no longer an intellectual horror; it was the raw, primal terror of the cornered animal. I looked wildly about the recess. There was no other escape. My back was to the cold stone; before me was my captured machine, and beyond it, the terrifying drop to a floor that was now swarming with the planet’s silent antibodies. My heart hammered against my ribs. I thought of Weena, a fragile flower in a world that had become a charnel house. I thought of my own sunlit time, of my friends, of the familiar, chaotic, and beautiful world I had left. Was this to be the end? To be torn apart by these degenerate vermin, or to become part of the very substance of this monstrous, thinking fungus?

In my desperation, my eyes fell again upon the time lever. It shone with a soft, internal light, the organic film that coated it pulsing in time with the cavern’s hum. An idea, born of utter hopelessness, flashed into my mind. The machine was being assimilated. It was being connected, understood. What would happen if I were to engage it? What would happen to a mind, however vast, if it were suddenly subjected to the inconceivable paradox of temporal displacement? Could I, perhaps, use its own process of absorption against it? It might destroy the machine. It might destroy me. It might cast me adrift into the uncharted abysses of Eternity. But it was a chance, and it was the only one I possessed.

I did not hesitate. The first of the Morlocks were beginning to appear on the gallery, their large eyes glowing with reflected light as they clambered up from below. I reached out and laid my hand firmly upon the time lever.

The moment my flesh made contact with the living sheath, my consciousness was torn from me. It was not a loss of sense, but an expansion of it so vast and so terrible that my own identity, my own human mind, became a grain of sand in an infinite desert. I was the cavern. I was the mycelium. I felt the slow, patient, geological time of its growth, the aeons spent in darkness, the slow branching and spreading through the crust of the earth. I felt the sun as a fading memory, a myth of a former age. I felt the network of consciousness, the thoughts that were not thoughts as I knew them, but slow, silent, chemical comprehensions.

And I felt the Morlocks. I felt them not as individuals, but as my own hands and feet, my own sensory organs. My theory of the Capitalist and the Labourer was a child’s drawing of a mighty engine. This was no mere social division; it was a speciation so profound as to be a new form of life altogether. The indolent Eloi were not masters; they were cattle, a carefully cultivated crop grown on the surface for a purpose I could not yet fathom, their fleeting lives a form of sustenance. The Morlocks were not slaves; they were the integrated, unthinking, and utterly contented cells of the true inheritor of the Earth. Theirs was not a life of misery; their individuality had been shed millennia ago, traded for a place in the vast, silent, and immortal mind of the world-organism. The horror of it was not in their suffering, but in their complete and utter lack of it. They were at peace, a peace I found more profoundly terrifying than any torment.

In that fleeting instant of connection, I also felt the organism’s awareness of me. It perceived my mind, a frantic, ephemeral spark of isolated consciousness. It perceived my thoughts as a chaotic, painful noise. It perceived my machine not as a mechanism of metal and quartz, but as a wound in the fabric of reality, a place where time did not behave as it should. It was not studying my machine out of curiosity; it was attempting to heal this wound, to absorb the paradox, to make the impossible part of its own seamless existence.

The sheer alien pressure of that vast, silent mind was enough to shatter a human intellect. I felt my own thoughts, my memories, my very name beginning to dissolve, to be drawn out and smoothed into the silent, oceanic consciousness. With a final, desperate spasm of my own will, a shriek of my soul against this utter annihilation, I pulled back.

I stumbled away from the machine, my connection severed. I was myself again, but a self that had been horribly scoured. The cavern returned to being a space outside of me, the hum a noise in my ears. I was gasping, my body drenched in a cold sweat, the phantoms of a billion years of silent, vegetable thought echoing in my skull. The Morlocks were now only a few yards away along the gallery, their approach still unnervingly slow and methodical. They were not rushing to attack; they were simply closing a gap, re-establishing order.

But I now knew the truth. My former theory was a pathetic oversimplification. The gulf between the Eloi and the Morlock was not the result of a mere social divergence. It was a planned, deliberate, and ancient symbiosis, or rather, a form of planetary agriculture. Humanity had not split. It had been sundered. One branch had been cultivated into beautiful, witless livestock; the other had been assimilated into the operating system of a global intelligence that had long ago supplanted the individual. My own age’s anxieties about Capital and Labour were the faint, early tremors of an earthquake that had, in the fullness of time, swallowed the world.

This new knowledge did not bring despair, but a strange, cold clarity. I could not fight this thing. I could not reason with it. I could not hope to escape it by stealth. To this entity, I was a discordant note in a symphony that had played for a million years. My only hope was to become a note so jarring, so utterly dissonant, that the symphony itself would break.

I turned back to the Time Machine. The Morlocks paused, their heads cocked as if sensing my intent through the rock beneath my feet. I now understood that to destroy the machine was to destroy myself. It was my only bridge, and it was burning. But if I could not pull it from the fire, perhaps I could use the fire itself.

My plan was no longer merely to engage the lever. That would simply invite that terrible, mind-dissolving communion again. No, I had to force the machine into a state that the mycelium could not comprehend, could not process. I had to initiate a temporal jump, however small. If the machine were to move, even an inch, into the future or the past, while physically bound to this cavern, what would happen? Would the tendrils be sheared away by the irresistible force of temporal motion? Or would the machine, the cavern, and I myself be torn apart by the resulting paradox? It was a gamble of the most terrifying kind, but inaction was a certainty of absorption and oblivion.

With the silent, white figures closing in, I climbed into the saddle. It was strange to be there again, ensnared as it was. The ivory and nickel felt alien under the living film. I placed my hands upon the controls, my fingers finding the smaller levers that governed the direction and velocity of travel. My intent was not to travel far; a mere second, a fraction of a second, would be enough to test the theory. I would attempt a micro-jump forward.

The Morlocks were upon the recess now. Their soft, cold hands reached for me. They did not claw or strike; their touch was questing, a simple physical expression of the great mind’s will to subsume me. I felt their fingers on my boots, on my coat. Their large, luminous eyes, devoid of any emotion I could recognise, stared at me with the placid intensity of a microscope.

There was no more time for thought. With a prayer to a God I had long since abandoned in my worship of science, I thrust the main lever forward.

The effect was not what I had anticipated. There was not the familiar lurch and blurring of the world. Instead, the instant I moved the lever, a colossal shriek of pure energy erupted from the machine. It was not a sound, but a wrenching of the senses, a silent scream of tortured physics. The violet light of the cavern flared into an incandescent, blinding white. The great hum rose in pitch until it was beyond hearing, a pressure that seemed to squeeze my very bones.

The mycelial tendrils, which had been wrapped so securely about the frame, flashed into brilliant light, like the filaments of a shattered lamp. They did not snap or break; they sublimated. They turned to glowing, incandescent vapour in an instant. The Morlocks who were touching me and the machine were likewise consumed. They did not cry out. They simply vanished in a flash of white fire, their substance converted into the raw energy of the paradox I had unleashed.

The machine was free.

But the cataclysm was not over. A wave of force, of displaced reality, exploded outwards from my position. The great stalagmite upon which I was perched groaned, and vast cracks appeared along its length. The gallery itself buckled and tore. From the cavern floor below, a sound finally arose; a chorus of chittering, terrified squeals from the thousands of remaining Morlocks as their world, their god, their very consciousness, was assaulted by a force it could not comprehend.

I did not wait to see the result. The machine was free of its bonds, and it was active. The familiar, giddy sensation of temporal movement was beginning to take hold, the world outside starting to flicker. The walls of the cavern seemed to writhe and flow. I had the presence of mind to pull the lever further, to throw myself headlong into the stream of time, away from the epicentre of the destruction I had caused. My only thought was to escape, to put as many years as possible between myself and that terrible, silent world.

I was frozen by the sheer scale of my impotence. My fists, which had once seemed such potent weapons against the clumsy Morlocks, were now just bundles of flesh, absurdly inadequate.

Then, from the shadows of the ruins, they came. I had called them the new Morlocks in my mind, but that name was insufficient. The Morlocks had been apes, brutes. These were different. They were taller, more slender, their movements economical and precise. Their skin was not the bleached white of their forebears, but a mottled grey, like polished stone. It was not skin at all, I realised with a shudder; it was a thin, segmented carapace, a chitinous armour that gleamed in the pulsing light of the central device. They were a fusion of the insect and the man, the progeny of the spider-like thing I had seen in the well and the intellect I had inadvertently granted them. Their heads were large and domed, and they possessed not two eyes, but a cluster of smaller, black lenses that glittered with cold, analytical intelligence. They were the Artificers.

They paid me no mind, though I was crouched in plain sight among a tumble of masonry. Their attention was entirely on their work. Two of them carried strange implements, rods that glowed with a faint internal energy. They moved through the circle of Eloi, making delicate adjustments to unseen apparatuses at the base of the tripod. They were not drooling predators; they were technicians. This was not a hunt; it was a harvest. And what were they harvesting? I did not believe it was the Eloi themselves. Their numbers seemed undiminished. No, they were harvesting the _product_ of the Eloi. They were draining the psychic energy that the Eloi, amplified by the tripod, were generating. They were feeding not on flesh, but on the very essence of thought and emotion, on the distilled spirit of what had once been human consciousness. The ultimate form of cannibalism.

My scientific curiosity warred with my horror. The pieces of the Time Machine, my beautiful instrument of ivory and quartz and brass; they had not just been broken. They had been studied. Its principles, the manipulation of energies beyond the common ken, had been mastered. These Artificers had taken the simple, elegant physics of my device and perverted it into this obscene technology of the soul. The hum grew louder, the light from the tripod more intense. The swaying of the Eloi became more frantic. A faint, silvery mist began to rise from the circle, shimmering in the air, coalescing around the apex of the machine. It was the energy, made visible. A river of stolen spirit flowing into the collectors of the Underworld.

It was more than I could bear. All caution, all reason, deserted me. I thought only of Weena, a gentle soul being rendered down for fuel. I gave a great cry, a wordless shout of rage and despair, and I burst from my hiding place. I ran towards the circle, my only thought to shatter the apparatus, to break the circuit.

The reaction was instantaneous. One of the Artificers turned its composite eyes towards me. There was no surprise, no fear. There was only analysis. It raised its glowing rod, not as a club, but as a director of force. I did not feel a blow. I felt a sudden, complete cessation of my own will. My legs locked; my arms fell limp to my sides. The very air around me seemed to turn to thick glass, holding me fast. I was twenty yards from the circle, a statue of impotent fury. The humming intensified to a piercing shriek, and the silver mist flooded into the machine. The Artificer that held me regarded me with its multiple black lenses, and though it had no mouth, no features to read, I felt an overwhelming sense of its thought. It was not anger. It was not malice. It was, I believe, curiosity. I was an anomaly, an unexpected variable, and it was studying me.

Then, for the first time, one of them communicated. It was not a sound, but a voice that bloomed inside my own skull, a cold, precise series of concepts. `The catalyst. The prime mover. Unforeseen.` My mind reeled. It was inside my head. It was reading me as easily as I might read a book. My terror was a fresh, fascinating flavour for it. My guilt, a complex and intriguing spice. `Your emotional output is inefficient,` the thought-voice continued, dispassionate and clinical. `Erratic. The lesser species is superior in its purity. A single, sustained note. You are a cacophony.`

I struggled against the invisible bonds, a fly in amber. My mind, which I had always considered my fortress, had been breached, its contents inventoried by a thief. `You accelerated the schedule,` the voice explained, as a master might lecture a dim-witted pupil. `The symbiosis was inefficient. `The Great Work?` I screamed in the confines of my own mind. `What work? What are you doing?`

The Artificer gestured with its free hand towards the sky, where the first stars were beginning to appear. The thought it projected was not made of words, but of pure, staggering concepts. I saw galaxies, nebulae, the vast, cold emptiness of space. I saw a slow, inexorable dimming, the heat death of the universe, the final, pathetic whisper of entropy. And I saw their ambition. They were not content to rule a dying Earth. They meant to escape it. Not in body, but in essence. They were building a vessel, not of metal and rivets, but of consolidated consciousness. They were harvesting the psychic energy of the Eloi, the last dregs of human vitality, to power an escape from reality itself, to become a collective, immortal mind capable of surviving the end of all things. The Time Machine had not just taught them engineering; it had taught them cosmology. It had shown them their ultimate doom, and they had resolved to un-write it.

The scale of it was beyond madness. It was a divinity forged from parasitism. They were not merely monsters; they were aspiring gods, and the Eloi were their sacrament. And I, in my arrogant meddling, had been their prophet. My arrival had been the miracle that showed them the way.

`You are… an historical artifact,` the thought-voice concluded. `Your purpose is served.` The Artificer turned its attention back to the harvest. The pressure holding me vanished. I collapsed to the ground, gasping, my limbs trembling uncontrollably. The shriek from the tripod subsided back into a hum, and the silver mist faded. The technicians gathered their implements. The circle of Eloi slowed their swaying, their eyes slowly regaining a dim flicker of awareness. The great work of the evening was done.

As the Artificers retreated back into the black shadows of the ruins, one of them paused. It carried a small, intricate cage of the same strange metal, and inside it, a tiny, white, ape-like creature huddled in fear. It was one of the old Morlocks. I saw its pale fur, its large, terrified eyes. They had not exterminated their ancestors. They had preserved them. As lab specimens? As a control group in their grand experiment? I lay on the damp grass, shivering, a broken thing. The Eloi began to disperse, wandering aimlessly, with the slow, graceful movements of sleep-walkers. Weena drifted past me, her eyes vacant. I whispered her name. She did not hear me. She did not see me. The part of her that had known me, that had shown affection, had been harvested, burned as a single drop of fuel in an engine that would carry her murderers beyond the end of time.

I was utterly alone. I was no longer an explorer or a scientist. I was a ghost, haunting a world of my own making. My theories, my clever deductions about Capital and Labour, about the bifurcation of the human race, were the pathetic scribbles of a child trying to comprehend a whirlwind. The truth was grander, more terrible, and infinitely more strange. Man had not simply split into two. One branch had been cultivated into a power source, and the other had evolved to become its engineer, aspiring to an insane godhood. There was no balance, no simple economy of predator and prey. There was only a factory, and I had supplied the blueprint.

I looked at the sky, at the cold, indifferent stars. The Artificers were down below, in their vast, humming workshops, working on their Great Work. The Eloi were their flock. And I? I was the shepherd who had led the flock to the abattoir of the soul. My quest was no longer for the Time Machine, no longer for escape. That was a luxury I had forfeited. My purpose now was to bear witness. To understand the final act of the human tragedy. And I suspected, with a certainty that was a cold weight in my gut, that the final act had not yet been written, and that its author had one last, terrible role for me to play.

The person I had known was gone, as utterly as if she had been consumed by fire. Perhaps she had been.

My terror of the Artificers was immense, a primal fear that resonated in my very bones, yet it was matched by a consuming, desperate curiosity. I had to know more. My thoughts returned again and again to the well-like shafts. I had once peered down them in fear of the Morlocks; now I looked at them with a different kind of dread, a dread of the intellect, of the soul. They were no longer crude tunnels into a charnel house; they were the stairways to the engine room of this entire world. I chose a shaft some distance from the main gathering places of the Eloi, one half-hidden in a thicket of strange, blood-red foliage. The sun beat down, and the air was thick and still. The silence of the world above seemed a fragile crust over a vast, humming secret below.

Taking a steadying breath, a prayer to a God I had long ceased to believe in, I gripped the metal rungs I had seen before and began my descent. The air grew cooler at once, and carried with it a peculiar, sterile scent, like ozone and cold metal. It was nothing like the faint, meaty odour I remembered from my first descent. The light of the upper world faded quickly to a small, blue circle above me. I was alone in the vertical dark, the sounds of my own breathing and the scraping of my boots against the wall my only companions.

After what seemed an age, my feet touched a level surface. I was in a place of profound blackness. I fumbled for my matches, my hands trembling so violently I feared I would drop the box. The first match flared, a tiny, defiant sun in the immense dark, and I gasped.

This was no cave. I stood at the entrance to a vast, cavernous space, a hall so enormous that the feeble light of my match could not begin to find its walls or ceiling. The floor was a polished, seamless material, cool to the touch. The air hummed with a low, resonant frequency that I felt more than heard, a vibration that seemed to course through the floor and up into my very skeleton. The entire world below was a single, vast, intricate machine.

My match burned my fingers, and I dropped it, plunging myself back into darkness. The hum seemed to grow louder in the absence of light. I lit another. This time, I saw movement. Far in the distance, a soft, internal luminescence pulsed along what looked like conduits or tracks, racing through the darkness in complex, geometric patterns. It was as if the nervous system of some titanic beast had been laid bare.

I began to walk, cautiously, into the darkness, lighting match after match. I soon realised that I did not need them. The floor itself began to emit a soft, phosphorescent glow, a trail of light that appeared a few feet before me and faded a few feet behind, guiding me forward. I was being led. The thought sent a fresh wave of fear through me, but I suppressed it. I was a willing captive; I had come here for answers, and it seemed the machine itself was prepared to offer them.

The path of light led me to a gallery, overlooking the main cavern. And from there, I saw it. I saw the Great Work.

Below me, suspended in the centre of the immense hall by what appeared to be fields of pure force, was a sphere of shimmering, liquid light. It was perhaps a hundred yards in diameter, a miniature sun, burning with an intensity that seemed impossible in this subterranean place. Around it, the Artificers moved. They did not walk; they glided through the air, borne by unseen currents, their slender, elongated forms silhouetted against the central glow. They were not tending machines in any conventional sense; they were weaving with light, manipulating strands of energy that they drew from the glowing sphere and plaited into complex, incandescent tapestries. They worked with a silent, focused intensity that was utterly mesmerising.

And I saw where the power came from. From the ceiling, great crystalline tubes descended, their ends lost in the darkness above. And through these tubes flowed a stream, a river, of faint, silvery mist; the very same mist I had seen drawn from the Eloi. It cascDwned into the central sphere, feeding its impossible fire. The consciousness, the spirit, the very essence of the Eloi, was being piped down here to power this colossal, incomprehensible engine.

I stared, my mind reeling, trying to fit this new, impossible reality into a framework I could understand. This was not industry, not as my age understood it. This was a form of physics that bordered on theology. They were not building a machine; they were constructing a god. Or perhaps, a vehicle to carry them to God.

My gaze was drawn to a smaller, quieter part of the great hall. Here, several Artificers were gathered around a series of cubes, fashioned from a dark, glassy material. Within each cube, held in a state of suspended animation, were creatures. I saw a Morlock, its large eyes closed, its white form curled in on itself. In another cube, I saw a creature that looked remarkably like a great cat, a tiger perhaps. And in a third, to my utter astonishment and horror, I saw the form of one of the Eloi, perfect and still, its hair fanning out in the clear, viscous substance that filled the cube. They were archivists. They were preserving specimens of the world they were preparing to leave behind, a biological record of their own evolution.

My mind, straining under the weight of these revelations, began to spin its own frantic theories. They were not merely content to rule this dying world. They were preparing for a journey. The sun, as I myself had speculated, was doomed. The Earth itself was doomed. These beings, these post-humans, were not waiting for the end. They were building their escape. But an escape to where? Another star? Another dimension?

The thought-voice returned, but this time it was not directed at me. It was a general broadcast, a series of commands or calculations that echoed not in my ears, but directly within my skull. It was a language of pure mathematics, of geometric forms and abstract concepts that blossomed and collapsed in my mind with breathtaking speed. I could not understand it, but I could feel the sheer intellectual power behind it, a power that made the greatest geniuses of my own time seem like fumbling children. I was a caveman, trying to comprehend a symphony.

And then, one of the gliding figures stopped its work. It turned its great, dark head, and I knew, with a certainty that froze the blood in my veins, that it was looking directly at me. I had been noticed.

The guiding light on the floor vanished. The hum of the cavern changed its pitch, rising to a keen, piercing note of inquiry. I felt a pressure building in my mind, a probing, insistent force that sought to pry open the corners of my consciousness. It was not hostile, not in a way I could recognise; it was methodical, curious, the way a biologist might probe a new specimen. It sifted through my memories, my emotions, my very sense of self, with an effortless, terrifying precision.

I saw flashes of my own life projected onto the dark air before me: my laboratory in Richmond, the faces of my friends, the launch of my machine, the verdant world of the Eloi, the terror of the Morlocks, Weena’s face, filled with simple affection. The Artificer was reading me, reading the history of its own ancient world from the living record of my mind.

The probe became more intense. `Anachronism,` the thought-voice stated, a cold, flat declaration inside my head. `A temporal anomaly. Unforeseen.` The pressure increased. `Your origin? Your method of transit?`

I fought back. I do not know how, but some instinct for self-preservation, for mental privacy, asserted itself. I thought of the most complex, the most irrational things I could. I pictured paradoxes: a snake eating its own tail, a man travelling back in time to kill his own grandfather. I threw concepts of poetry, of love, of irrational rage at the intruder; the very human chaos that these logical, crystalline beings had evolved beyond.

The probe recoiled, not in pain, but in what felt like surprise. `Disordered. Illogical. Redundant data loops.` There was a pause, a moment of silent consideration. `The emotional content is… inefficient. But the energy signature is high.`

Energy signature. The thought struck me with the force of a physical blow. They had harvested the gentle, placid emotions of the Eloi. What would they make of the turbulent, violent passions of an older, wilder form of humanity? Was I not just a curiosity to them, but a potential power source? A rich, untapped vein of raw, chaotic feeling?

`A proposal,` the thought-voice continued, the tone now shifting, becoming almost… persuasive. `Your species is extinct. Your time is dust. You are a ghost, a statistical improbability. But your pattern contains a unique resonance. A chaotic potential. It could be of use to the Great Work.`

It was offering me a place. Not as an equal, but as a component. It was inviting me to be assimilated, to have the essence of my being, my memories, my loves and hates, drained from me to add a single, infinitesimal drop of fuel to their cosmic engine. The ultimate purpose. The final act. To become a part of their god-machine.

`Your purpose is served,` it had said before. Now I understood. My purpose was to be consumed.

Panic, pure and animalistic, overwhelmed my scientific curiosity. I turned and fled, plunging back into the absolute darkness, away from the guiding light. I scrabbled and stumbled, my hands outstretched, expecting at any moment to collide with a wall or to feel the mental grip close around me again. But nothing happened. The humming returned to its previous pitch. The pressure in my mind receded.

It had let me go. Why? Had my chaotic mind proven too distasteful? Or was it simply so confident in its power that it saw no need to restrain me? A man does not trouble himself to cage an ant that has wandered into his house; he merely observes its frantic scurrying before it finds its own way out.

My hands found the ladder. I did not hesitate. I climbed, scrambling upward with a desperate strength I did not know I possessed. I climbed out of the cold, humming, logical hell of the future, back toward the warm, irrational, dying world of the sun. As I broke the surface, gasping in the thick, hot air of the twilight, I looked back at the world of the Eloi. The beautiful people were gathering for the harvest, their faces turned toward the ruins with a placid, bovine contentment. The sun was setting, painting the sky in glorious, funereal shades of purple and red. It was the most beautiful, most terrible sight I had ever seen. I had seen the engine, and now I was watching the fuel being led, willingly, to the fire. And I knew, with a fresh and horrifying certainty, that the Artificers were not the end of the story. They were a transition. Man was not the end-product of evolution. Man was the material from which something else, something vast and strange and terrible, was being built. And the universe would not even notice the change.

I spent an hour or more searching the hillsides in the gloom, avoiding the pristine, round well-shafts of the Artificers and looking for something else. At last I found it. This was no silent, humming conduit of pure intellect. This was the service entrance to hell.

The air grew hotter and more foul with every yard I descended, the darkness absolute. The only sound was the scuffing of my boots, the clang of the machinery growing louder, and the heavy beat of my own heart. This was the engine room of the world. Great chains moved on immense, groaning gears. Pistons the size of cottages slammed up and down, and the air thrummed with a power that was raw, chaotic, and brutal. It was the polar opposite of the cold, silent, effortless hum from the Artificers' chamber. This was the grimy, sweating, bleeding muscle that moved the world, while the Artificers were the disembodied brain that gave the orders.

I began to move along the ledge, seeking a way down. Soon I saw Morlocks. They were swarming over the machinery, tending to its needs with a frantic, insect-like energy. I arose stiffly, my limbs a concert of aches and bruises. The dew-soaked grass was cold against my skin, a stark contrast to the memory of the close, fetid air of the underworld. The eastern sky was a wash of pale lilac and rose, and the great, ruined structures about me were beginning to shed their nightly shrouds of black, resolving into definite shapes of grey and silver. The world was unutterably beautiful, and I had never hated it so much. My mind, a raw and flayed thing, recoiled from the tranquil scene. The sun rose, a benediction of gold and rose; yet I saw in its warmth only the heat of a farmer's lamp in a fattening-pen. The birds began their morning chorus in the cypress trees, but their song was to me the thoughtless chirping of poultry in a coop. Everything was tainted, everything was a lie.

My first thought was of Weena. I had left her sleeping amongst her fellows in the great hall of the palace. A frantic urgency seized me. I had to get back to her, to see for myself that she was safe, though what safety meant in this world I could no longer fathom. I set off at a limping run, my body protesting at every step. I found myself starting at the rustle of a leaf, at the skittering of some small, unseen creature in the undergrowth. Every dark shadow, every gaping doorway of a ruin, seemed to hold a pair of luminous eyes. The journey that had before been a matter of pleasant, curious exploration was now a gauntlet of imagined terrors. I was a man who had looked into the cellar of the world, and I could not unsee the things that scuttled there.

I found the great house stirring with the first movements of the day. The beautiful people were emerging into the sunlight, stretching their graceful limbs, their faces untroubled and placid. They were like flowers turning to the sun, and the sight of their innocence filled me with a strange mixture of pity and revulsion. They chattered their soft, liquid language as they began their aimless sports, their perpetual, pointless holiday. I searched their faces for any sign of Weena, my heart a cold knot of dread.

And then I saw her. She was sitting alone on the steps of a high terrace, a small, forlorn figure, plucking the petals from a crimson flower. When she saw me, her face lit with an expression of such pure, uncomplicated joy that it was like a knife in my heart. She ran to me, her little feet barely seeming to touch the marble flags, and threw her arms about my legs, chattering with relief. She had been afraid for me; that much I could understand. She pointed to the dark interior of the palace and then to me, and shook her head with a child’s simple negation. She had feared the dark had claimed me.

She placed a garland of newly-plucked flowers upon my head; a gesture so innocent, so terribly, heartbreakingly blind, that I had to fight back a surge of emotion that threatened to overwhelm me. I took her small hand in mine. Its warmth and trust were the only real things in this damnable paradise. How could I explain to her the nature of the night? How could I warn a creature who had no concept of sustained danger, who lived only for the sweet fruit and the warm sun? I could not. I was her only shield, a stranger from a savage past, and a pathetically inadequate one at that.

For the next two days, I did not let her out of my sight. My mind was a feverish engine, working ceaselessly on the problem of my escape. The Time Machine, my only portal back to the familiar world of sense and struggle, was imprisoned within the White Sphinx. And the Sphinx, I now knew, was a gateway to the realm of the Morlocks. They had dragged my machine inside, no doubt out of a brutish, degenerate curiosity, the last flickering remnant of the mechanical aptitude of their ancestors. My task was clear: I had to get into the Sphinx.

Weena, in her simple way, seemed to understand that my mood had changed. I no longer explored the sunlit ruins with the same intellectual curiosity. I was restless, watchful, and given to long periods of silence. She would sit beside me, her hand in mine, occasionally placing a flower in my buttonhole or offering me a strange, sweet fruit, her large, gentle eyes full of a dim, questioning concern. I tried to teach her. I took the little metal box of my matches, which I guarded now as a most precious treasure, and tried to impress upon her the nature of fire. I would strike one, and she would clap her hands in delight at the sudden, bright flame. But when I tried to make her understand its power, its use as a weapon against the dark, her attention would wander. It was a pretty game, and nothing more. The concept of a tool, of a means to an end beyond immediate amusement, was utterly lost to her.

Her fear of the dark, however, was a language we both understood. As dusk fell each day, she would cling to me with a desperate tenacity, her body trembling. Her people would gather in the great halls to sleep in vast, huddled masses, seeking comfort in numbers. They feared the night, but as a child fears a nightmare, without understanding its substance. They did not know that the monster under the bed was real, and that it was hungry.

On the third day after my escape from the well, I resolved to examine the Sphinx more closely. With Weena trotting trustfully beside me, I walked across the overgrown lawn towards the grim, brooding edifice. It sat upon a pedestal of some greenish-white metal, its carved face weathered by countless centuries into an expression of blank, inscrutable mystery. Its sightless eyes seemed to look past me, into the depths of time itself. The great bronze doors set into the pedestal were shut fast. I ran my hands over their surface. They were seamless, without handle or visible lock. I pushed against them with all my strength, but they did not yield so much as a hair's breadth. They were as immovable as the foundations of the world.

I circled the pedestal, searching for any other point of entry, any crack or lever or secret panel. There was nothing. The metal was smooth and unbroken on all sides. Weena grew distressed by my frantic activity, pulling at my coat and murmuring her soft, plaintive sounds. She pointed away from the Sphinx, towards the sunlit gardens, her meaning clear. This was a place of ill omen, a place to be avoided. She was right, of course, but for all the wrong reasons.

Defeated for the moment, I sat on the grass and looked at the Sphinx, my mind racing. The Morlocks had taken my machine in. Therefore, they must have a way of opening the doors. It would not be a method requiring complex thought; their intellect was surely a degraded thing. It would be something simple, something mechanical. And if they could open them, so could I. But how? My eyes roved over the landscape, seeking a clue. I saw the familiar well-openings, the ventilating shafts that dotted the hillside. They were the chimneys and back-doors to the Morlock world. The main entrance, the grand portal, had to be the Sphinx itself.

An idea began to form in my mind, an idea that filled me with a mixture of excitement and dread. I could not solve this mystery by daylight. The creatures of the Underworld were nocturnal. If I wished to learn their secrets, I must watch them in their own time. I would have to keep a vigil through the night.

The prospect was a terrifying one. To willingly stay out in the open, in the darkness that even the Eloi feared instinctively, felt like a form of madness. My every nerve screamed in protest. The memory of the cloying darkness below, of the soft, cold hands, was still hideously fresh. But the alternative, to remain trapped in this gilded cage, this beautiful abattoir, was infinitely worse. I had to risk it.

That evening, as the sun began its descent, I prepared myself. I gathered a small pile of dry sticks and leaves, a pitifully small pyre. My matches were my only true defence, and I had a dwindling supply. I chose my observation point with care: a thicket of dense, rhododendron-like shrubs on a slope that overlooked the White Sphinx, some hundred yards distant. It offered good concealment, yet a clear view.

As the last rays of sunlight vanished, Weena's terror became acute. She whimpered and tried to pull me back towards the great house where her people were gathering for their communal sleep. It was a struggle to persuade her to stay with me. I held her close, murmuring what I hoped were reassuring sounds, and pointed to my little pile of wood and the matchbox in my hand. I tried to convey the idea of a great fire, a protective light, but I doubt she understood. She stayed only because her fear of being left alone with the encroaching darkness was, for the moment, greater than her fear of this strange, exposed place.

The moon was not yet risen. The world was plunged into a profound, velvety blackness, broken only by the brilliant glitter of the stars. The air grew cold. Silence fell, a deep, listening silence that seemed to magnify every tiny sound: the sigh of the night wind, the distant cry of some nocturnal bird. Weena was a trembling weight in my arms. I waited.

I do not know how many hours passed. My senses were stretched to an almost painful degree of alertness. At last, I saw it. From the direction of one of the well-shafts nearer the river, a flicker of movement. Then another. Pale, hunched shapes, moving with a queer, shambling gait. The Morlocks. They did not swarm out in a ravenous horde, as I might have expected, but emerged in a queerly business-like fashion, in twos and threes. They kept to the shadows, their large eyes glowing with a faint, reflected starlight. My blood ran cold, but I forced myself to remain still, to watch.

They were not, it seemed, on a hunt. At least, not yet. Some of them scurried towards the fruit trees, clambering up the trunks with an ape-like agility. I realised with a jolt that they were gathering food. For whom? For themselves, or for the Eloi? Was this part of their husbandry? Tending to their livestock? The thought was grotesque. Others moved about the ruins, their purpose unclear. They were like a crew of ghoulish workmen, performing the nightly maintenance of a world that was not their own.

Then, a group of four Morlocks detached themselves from the others and shambled directly towards the White Sphinx. My heart hammered against my ribs. This was it. I strained my eyes in the darkness, praying for the moon to rise.

The creatures reached the metal pedestal. For a moment, they simply stood there, their heads turning as if listening. Then one of them bent down. I could not see exactly what it was doing, but I heard a faint scraping sound, followed by a low, grinding noise. It was the sound of heavy, well-oiled machinery. Slowly, ponderously, one of the great bronze doors began to swing inwards, revealing a rectangle of absolute blackness.

The Morlocks slipped inside, one after the other, and the door began to swing shut behind them, with that same, heavy, grinding sound. But before it closed completely, one of the creatures seemed to do something to the mechanism from within, for the door remained ajar by a foot or so. A faint, pale luminescence, like that of phosphorescence, seeped from the gap.

My mind seized upon the new facts with a desperate excitement. The mechanism was not on the door, but at the base of the pedestal! A lever, a hidden treadle, something set into the foundation itself. And they had left the door open! Why, I could not guess. Perhaps for ventilation. Perhaps in readiness for some later task. It did not matter. A path was open to me.

The moon began to rise, bathing the landscape in its serene, silver light. In its glow, the Sphinx looked more sinister than ever, a mausoleum with its door invitingly, terribly, ajar. The moment of decision was upon me. I had to go now, whilst the opportunity presented itself.

And then I looked down at Weena, who had fallen into a fitful, whimpering sleep in my arms. I could not take her with me. To bring her into that black, Morlock-haunted labyrinth would be to deliver her directly to her fate. My mission was one of stealth and, if necessary, of brutal combat. She would be a liability, and I, a hopelessly compromised protector. I had to leave her behind.

The thought was an agony. To abandon this one, small, affectionate creature who trusted me completely felt like the greatest betrayal I had ever committed. But what was the alternative? To stay and be devoured with her? To fail in my attempt to retrieve the Time Machine and doom us both? My reason, cold and hard, told me there was no other way. My only hope of saving her, in any real sense, was to first save myself; to return to my own time and perhaps, I thought with a wild, desperate fancy, to return with the means to alter this dreadful destiny. It was a fool's hope, but it was all I had.

I gently disengaged her arms from my neck. She stirred, her eyes opening. In the moonlight, they were dark pools of confusion and fear. I tried to explain, using gestures. I pointed to myself, then to the Sphinx, then made a motion of returning. I pointed to the great house, urging her to go there, to her own people. She only shook her head and clung to me more tightly.

I will admit that my resolution wavered. The thought of her waking alone in the dark, surrounded by the prowling horrors I now knew to be real, was almost more than I could bear. Then, my hand touched the matchbox in my pocket. I had a sudden, desperate inspiration. It was a cruel kindness, but it was the only one I could offer.

I took the box and showed it to her. I struck a match. The sudden flare of light captivated her, as it always did. I struck another, and another. Then I pressed the box into her small hands, closing her fingers around it. It was the most valuable thing I possessed, save the Time Machine itself. It was fire. It was light. It was a power she could not comprehend, but its novelty, I hoped, might distract her, might comfort her.

I kissed her forehead. Her skin was cool. Then, before I could lose my nerve, I pushed her gently away from me and stood up. I did not look back. I could hear her begin to cry, a thin, pitiful sound in the vast silence of the night. Every step I took towards the Sphinx was a torment. But I did not falter. My scientific curiosity had been replaced by a single, burning purpose. I would have my machine back. I would escape this graveyard of a world. And with every footfall on the moonlit grass, the sound of Weena’s weeping grew fainter, until at last it was swallowed by the silence, and I was utterly, terribly alone.

The White Sphinx loomed before me, a silent, silver ghost in the moonlight. Each step I took was a conscious act of will, a victory over the screaming impulse to turn back, to find Weena, to snatch back the fool’s gift I had given her. The matchbox! My one shield against the dark, my Promethean fire, given away for a tearful, temporary comfort. What had I been thinking? My mind, which I had always prided as a cool and logical instrument, had betrayed me utterly, undone by the simple pity for a creature no more sophisticated than a favourite pet. The irony was a bitter acid in my soul. I, who had unravelled the grand, terrible secret of this world’s dual inheritance, had been undone by a sentiment worthy of the most witless Eloi. And now I was to face the children of the night, the masters of the machine, with nothing but my bare hands and the fading courage of a man who knows he has sealed his own doom.

The silence was no longer a simple absence of sound. It had become a presence in itself, a listening, waiting entity. The gentle sigh of the night breeze through the strange, stiff foliage felt like the world’s last exhalation. I strained my ears, but heard nothing; not the patter of Morlock feet, nor the distant cry of Weena. I was in a vacuum, a pocket of dead time between the past I had forsaken and the future I was about to invade. The great statue of the Sphinx, its chipped face impassive, its stone flanks gleaming, seemed to mock my plight. It held my only hope of escape, locked within its bronze belly, and the price of entry was a journey into the earth’s blackest bowels, a journey for which I had, in a moment of supreme folly, extinguished my only light.

I reached the pedestal. The bronze panels were smooth and cold to the touch, seamless to my probing fingers. There was no handle, no seam, no obvious means of ingress. I remembered how they had slid apart before, to admit the Morlocks who had stolen my machine. I was forced into a strategy of ignoble patience. I found a spot in the deep shadow cast by one of the Sphinx’s paws, a niche between the pedestal and the crumbling stonework of the terrace. There I crouched, a second-rate hunter waiting for his prey to show him the way into their own lair. The minutes stretched into an eternity. The moon, my solitary companion, began its slow descent towards the western hills, its light shifting and elongating the shadows. My fear, which had been a sharp and urgent thing, cooled into a kind of grim, fatalistic resolve. But beneath it, a frantic calculation was taking place. Without matches, what could I do? I searched my pockets, a mad, repetitive gesture. A pocket-knife with a broken blade. A length of string. My watch, which had long since stopped. Useless trifles. My fists were my only weapon. My intellect, my supposed advantage, seemed a poor, insubstantial tool against the physical reality of the coming struggle.

Then I heard it. A faint, grating sound, like stone on stone. It came from the base of the pedestal. I peered from my hiding place, my heart a frantic drum against my ribs. One of the great bronze plates was sliding sideways, revealing a yawning rectangle of absolute blackness. The sound had not been the panel itself, but rather the clumsy movement of some creature just within. For a moment, nothing emerged. The dark opening was like a mouth, exhaling a faint, foul odour, a sickly-sweet scent of raw meat and unventilated machinery. Then I saw them. Two Morlocks, their pale, slug-like forms silhouetted for a bare instant against the moonlit grass before they scurried away into the ruins. They moved with that same fumbling haste I had observed before, their large eyes doubtless finding the moonlight an intolerable glare.

This was my chance. I did not hesitate. Before the panel could slide shut, I darted from my hiding place, my soft-soled shoes making no sound on the turf. I took a deep breath of the cool night air, a final farewell to the upper world, and plunged into the darkness. The bronze door slid shut behind me with a soft, final boom that echoed in the enclosed space. The blackness was instantaneous and absolute. It was a physical thing, pressing on my eyeballs, filling my lungs. The foul air was thick and warm, and the silence was replaced by a new sound; a deep, rhythmic throbbing that seemed to come from the very heart of the earth. It was the pulse of the underworld, the ceaseless turning of its mighty engines.

I was blind. Utterly and completely blind. I stretched out my hands, and they met a cold, smooth wall. I had a sudden, horrifying vision of being trapped in this antechamber forever, to starve and die in the suffocating dark. Overcoming the rising panic, I began to edge my way along the wall, one hand trailing against its surface. My foot struck something, and I stumbled. It was a step, leading down. I had found the way. Gripping the wall, I began my descent, feeling for each step with my foot before committing my weight. The air grew warmer and more foul with every step. The thrumming of the machinery grew louder, a vast, monotonous symphony of industrial labour.

I do not know how long I descended. Time, in that sensory vacuum, lost all meaning. My only measure was the count of the steps, which I soon lost, and the increasing ache in my legs. My other senses, sharpened by the loss of my sight, began to bring me a strange and terrifying picture of my surroundings. I could hear, beneath the din of the great machines, a faint, continuous pattering and chittering, the sound of the Morlocks themselves. And the smell! It was a complex and nauseating perfume; the odour of oil and hot metal, overlaid with that sweetish, fleshy scent, and a damp, earthy smell like a freshly opened grave. I felt a draft of air on my cheek, and realised I was passing the mouths of side-tunnels. The entire world beneath my feet was a hollow, living thing.

My groping hand, which had been sliding along a smooth, metallic surface, suddenly found a different texture. It was rougher, almost organic, and yielding. I snatched my hand back with a gasp. Then, driven by a morbid curiosity that overrode my disgust, I reached out again. It was a curtain of some sort, made of a thick, leathery fabric. I pushed it aside. A faint, ghostly light trickled into the passage from beyond. After the absolute blackness, even this dim phosphorescence was a dazzling relief. I stepped through the curtain and found myself on a narrow gallery overlooking a vast cavern.

And what a cavern! My mind reeled. It was here that the great dynamos I had heard were housed. Huge, incomprehensible machines whirred and pounded, their colossal pistons rising and falling in a tireless rhythm. But it was not the machinery that held my attention. It was the light. The cavern was lit by a strange, cold, greenish luminescence that seemed to emanate from the very rock of the ceiling, where thick veins of some phosphorescent mineral pulsed with a faint, sickly glow. And in this dead light, I saw the Morlocks.

There were hundreds of them. They swarmed over the machinery, oiling, adjusting, and tending to its needs with a practiced, ant-like diligence. They were the mechanics, the engineers, the forgotten children of the machine age. But I saw more. In the centre of the cavern was a wide, circular table of polished stone. And upon it were the remains of their last meal. I will not tell you what it was. But I knew then, with a certainty that turned my blood to ice, the full, unspeakable horror of the relationship between the Eloi and the Morlocks. The beautiful, carefree children of the sun were not merely the decadent masters; they were the cattle. My theory of the split between Capital and Labour was not just a clever speculation; it was a living, breathing nightmare, and I was standing in the abattoir.

A wave of nausea and vertigo swept over me. I clung to the railing of the gallery, my knuckles white. The sight of that dreadful feast, combined with the foul air and the ceaseless, pounding din, was almost too much to bear. But then I saw it. In a side alcove, partially hidden by a great, humming condenser, stood my Time Machine.

It was there. Intact. They had dragged it here, to this central hall of their world. A few Morlocks were sniffing around its base, their pale hands stroking the quartz and ivory, their large, unblinking eyes reflecting the green glow. They seemed to regard it with a mixture of fear and curiosity, like primitive men contemplating a fallen meteorite. Perhaps its intricate workmanship, so far beyond their own degraded abilities, resonated with some long-buried ancestral memory of making and building.

A new strength, born of rage and desperation, flooded through me. I would not end up on that stone table. I would not become another meal for these subterranean ghouls. I would have my machine back, and I would return to the clean air and sane world of the nineteenth century. But how? I was one man against a legion, and I was unarmed. My eyes darted around the cavern, searching for a weapon, a tool, anything. My gaze fell upon a heavy metal lever, a long rod of steel used for shifting some great gear, resting in a bracket on the wall of the gallery not ten feet from me. It was heavy, unwieldy, but it was something.

I crept along the gallery, keeping to the deepest shadows at the back. The Morlocks below remained oblivious, absorbed in their tasks and their feasting. The chittering sound they made seemed to rise and fall in a kind of ghastly, communal language. I reached the lever. It was wedged tight in its bracket. I put my shoulder to it and heaved, my muscles screaming in protest. With a grating shriek of metal on metal, it came free. It was almost too heavy to wield effectively, but its sheer weight was a comfort. I was no longer entirely defenceless.

Now for the machine. I had to cross a hundred feet of open floor, in full view of the entire swarm. Stealth was no longer an option. It had to be a charge, a sudden, desperate blitz. I hefted the iron bar, its cold weight familiarising itself to my grip. I took a last look at the dreadful scene, the pounding machines, the ghoulish feast, the pale, swarming figures. This was the ultimate destiny of mankind, a future of cannibalistic symbiosis, of beauty fed by horror. And a great rage, a righteous fury against this whole degraded future, filled my heart.

With a yell that was half-battle cry, half-scream of terror, I vaulted over the railing of the gallery. I landed heavily on the cavern floor, my legs jarring from the ten-foot drop. The chittering stopped. A thousand pairs of luminous eyes turned towards me. For a moment, there was a dead silence, broken only by the thrum of the dynamos. It was as if I had dropped into a nest of spiders. Then, with a collective, hissing shriek, they came for me.

I did not wait for them. I ran, my heavy boots pounding on the stone, the iron bar held before me like a lance. The first Morlock to reach me was a large, bold one. It leaped at my throat, its sharp teeth bared. I swung the bar in a great arc, and felt a sickening crunch as it connected with the creature’s skull. It fell without a sound. But there were more, dozens more, swarming at my legs, their clammy hands clawing at me, trying to drag me down. I kicked and stamped and swung the bar, clearing a path before me by sheer, brute force. They were weak, individually, their bones brittle from generations in the dark. But their numbers were overwhelming.

I fought my way forward, foot by bloody foot, towards the alcove. The Time Machine was my beacon, my sanctuary. The air was filled with the shrieks of the wounded and the enraged hissing of the pack. They were all around me, a writhing, pulling mass of pale flesh and glaring eyes. I felt teeth sink into my arm, and I roared in pain and fury, smashing my attacker against the wall. I was a primeval man again, a beast fighting for its life against other beasts in the depths of a cave. All my civilised veneer was stripped away, leaving only the raw, savage instinct to survive.

I was ten feet from the machine. Five feet. I could almost touch it. But they were dragging me down. My legs were tangled in a mass of writhing bodies. My strength was failing. The iron bar, now slick with gore, was torn from my grasp. Clammy hands were at my throat, my face. The sickly-sweet stench of them was suffocating me. This was the end. I was going to be torn apart and devoured in the guts of the earth. My last thought was of Weena, and the sunlight, and the green world I had lost forever.

Then I saw a flicker. A flicker of orange light, near the great stone table. A small flame, dancing and growing. For a hallucinatory second, I thought my mind had broken, that I was seeing the ghost of my lost matches. But the flame grew, licking at the greasy remnants on the table, spreading to the leathery hangings on the wall. Fire! Someone had started a fire!

The Morlocks around me recoiled, their hisses turning to shrieks of panic. They had the dark-dweller’s instinctive, absolute terror of flame. The press of bodies around me loosened. Who could have done it? Who else was down here? Then I remembered the matchbox, pressed into a small, trusting hand. Weena. Could she have followed me? Could she have found her way down one of the shafts, guided by some incredible, loving instinct? The thought was so wild, so impossible, it gave me a new surge of adrenaline.

I did not wait to find out. I scrambled to my feet, shaking off the last of the clawing hands, and lunged for the Time Machine. I threw myself into the saddle. My hands found the controls, the familiar feel of the ivory and brass. The quartz rod was still in place. The smaller lever, the one that initiated the temporal journey, was under my right hand. The fire was spreading now, casting huge, dancing shadows across the cavern. The Morlocks were in a complete rout, a screaming, panicked mob fleeing from the light and the heat. Some were trampling each other to get to the dark tunnels; others were simply running in circles, blinded and terrified.

A few, braver or more desperate than the rest, saw me on the machine and made one last, frantic rush. As I fumbled with the lever, a Morlock leaped onto the frame, its face close to mine, its great, red eyes filled with a baleful, hopeless rage. I struck it with my fist, a solid blow to the jaw that sent it reeling backward into the growing conflagration. Then my fingers closed on the starting lever. I thrust it forward.

There was the familiar lurch, the sense of falling and rising at once. The cavern, the machines, the fire, the screaming Morlocks—all of it dissolved into a blur of streaking colours. The deep thrumming of the dynamos was replaced by the high-pitched hum of my own machine. The greenish phosphorescence gave way to a rushing grey indistinctness. I was moving. I was free. I was hurtling through time, away from the dreadful graveyard of a world that had once been the future of Humanity. My clothes were torn, my body bruised and bleeding, but I was alive, and I was on my way home. And as the ghostly shapes of the ages flickered past me, my last, waking thought was not of my escape, but of a tiny, golden-haired girl, and the terrible, beautiful fire she had made in the dark.

The journey back was a delirium of pain and regret. The very act of temporal displacement, which on my first voyage had been a source of dizzying exhilaration, was now an agony. Each flicker of day and night that whipped past the crystal frame of my machine was a lash against my conscience. My body, bruised and broken as it was, registered these blows less keenly than my spirit. I was a man fleeing a battlefield, leaving his most loyal soldier to perish. No; that was not right. Weena was no soldier. She was a child, a flower, a creature of sunlight I had dragged into my own darkness, and for that I had abandoned her.

The ghostly procession of the ages, which had so fascinated me before, was now a torment. I saw the sun, a vast and bloated crimson orb, hang dying in a purple sky. I saw the earth, barren and silent, save for the whisper of a ceaseless wind across red deserts and frozen seas. I saw monstrous crab-like things scuttling along the shores of a dying world, the final inheritors of our long and tragic enterprise. And through it all, through the eons of decay and the final, frigid twilight of the planet, I saw only her face, small and trusting, framed by the glare of the fire she had made for me. A fire of love. A fire of doom.

I do not know how long I travelled. My indicators were a blur, the delicate needles trembling with the machine’s exertions. The throbbing grew uneven, a sign, I feared, of some deep-seated strain within its complex mechanism. I was approaching my own time; I knew it by a subtle change in the quality of the flickering light, a certain grimy familiarity to the shades of grey and brown that streaked past. I could almost smell the damp coal smoke of Richmond, feel the comforting chill of an English evening. Home. Safety. My laboratory, my books, my friends gathered around a fire of a much more civilised sort, eager for my story.

And then I knew, with a certainty that was like a physical blow, that I could not do it.

To return now would be a betrayal far greater than the first. To sit in a plush armchair and recount the tale of the Eloi and the Morlocks, to speak of Weena’s devotion as a mere anecdote in a traveller’s tale; it was an act of such monstrous egotism that the thought of it made me physically sick. What was my life, my comfort, my scientific reputation, compared to the truth of her existence? I had seen the two poles of humanity’s future, the tender and decadent flower and the bitter, toiling root. I had thought myself a mere observer, a historian of things to come. But my presence there had changed the equation. I had given Weena a name. I had taught her affection, and she, in turn, had taught me a loyalty that transcended species, that transcended time itself. I owed her a debt.

It began with a sound, a low, rhythmic thumping that seemed to come from the very earth beneath our feet. Then, from a dozen of the well-like shafts in the valley below our hill, great gouts of water erupted, arcing through the air. They had, it seemed, repurposed some of their ancient machinery, turning their ventilation or drainage systems into a weapon. They were trying to extinguish our fires from a distance.

Panic seized the Eloi. Their god of fire was under attack! Great clouds of steam hissed up from our perimeter blazes as the water struck. Some of the smaller fires were quenched at once. I ran among my terrified charges, shouting to them to protect the main bonfire, to use the stored wood to rekindle any that went out. My voice, and the sight of Weena calmly helping to drag dry logs towards a sputtering fire, seemed to steady them.

But this was only the prelude. Under the cover of the confusion and the steam, the Morlocks advanced. They did not come from one direction, but from all sides, a coordinated assault. They were no longer a mere mob. They moved in disciplined groups, using the shadows and the ruined outer walls for cover. And they had a new tactic. Some of them carried wet, heavy-looking cloths or hides. As they neared the fires, they would fling these onto the flames, smothering them with a dreadful efficiency.

The battle that ensued was far more terrible than the first. We were fighting on our own ground, yes, but the Morlocks were fighting with a plan. It was a contest of fire against water, of light against a thinking, disciplined darkness. I was in the thick of it, my iron bar a poor substitute for the torch that had been so effective before. Here, in close quarters, the Morlocks’ superior strength was a terrifying advantage. I saw them seize Eloi and, ignoring the flailing blows of their small fists, simply drag them, screaming, away into the night.

Yet my little people did not break. They had lost their innocence, but they had found a desperate, collective will to survive. They swarmed the Morlocks who broke through our lines, jabbing with sharpened sticks I had fashioned, throwing stones, even biting and scratching with a feral intensity I had never imagined they possessed. They used the fire intelligently, snatching burning brands from the central pyre and using them to drive back the creatures carrying the wet cloths.

For what felt like hours the chaos raged. The courtyard was a vision of hell. The air was filled with steam, smoke, the shrieks of the fighting, and the nauseating smell of wet, singed fur. I fought with the strength of a madman, my mind a cold knot of calculation in the heart of the frenzy. I directed the Eloi, pointing out breaches in our defence, organising sorties to rescue those who were captured. I felt a blow to my head and staggered, the world swimming in a red haze, but I forced myself to my feet. I could not fall. If I fell, all was lost.

Then, as suddenly as it began, it was over. A high-pitched piping sound echoed from the valley, a sound I had not heard before, and the Morlocks disengaged. They did not flee in a rout as before. They withdrew, carrying their dead and wounded, melting back into the shadows with the same discipline with which they had attacked. It was an orderly retreat. They had tested our defences, found them formidable but not impregnable. They had taken their losses, but they had inflicted them as well. They would be back.

I stood leaning on my iron bar, gasping for breath, my whole body a symphony of pain. The courtyard was a wreck. More of the Eloi were gone. Perhaps a dozen. The survivors huddled around the great fire, which now burned low. They were not triumphant. They were exhausted, terrified, and bereaved. The hard triumph of the first victory had been replaced by the grim, bitter reality of a war of attrition. They had learned that victory does not always feel like winning.

Weena came to me, her face streaked with soot and tears. She pointed a trembling finger towards the darkness where the Morlocks had vanished. Her lips formed a single word, a word I had taught her. "Again?" she asked, her voice a tiny, desolate whisper.

"Yes," I said, my own voice heavy. "Again."

It was in that moment of shared despair that my resolve hardened into a new and terrible shape. This could not go on. I could not preside over the slow, bloody annihilation of this fragile race. A defensive war was a losing war. They could not win by holding a fort. The only way to win, the only way to secure any kind of future for them, and for me to ever see my own time again, was to take the war to the enemy.

I had to go down.

I had to descend into their world, into the black heart of their subterranean empire. I had to find their central nexus, the seat of their power, the location of their machinery. And I had to find my Time Machine. I would not go for reconnaissance this time. I would go to destroy. I would take their most precious weapon, fire, and I would turn their ventilated world into a furnace. It was a monstrous thought, a genocidal thought. But as I looked at the weeping, terrified face of Weena, and at the cowering remnants of her people, it seemed the only thought possible. It was them, or the Morlocks. I had forced the choice, and now I had to see it through.

I gathered the Eloi who were still capable of understanding me. I gathered Weena to my side. I began to speak, using the simple words and grand gestures they knew. I pointed to the bonfire. I pointed to the wells in the earth. I made a sweeping, downward gesture, and then a great, explosive one. I was trying to explain the inexplicable, to rally them for one final, desperate gamble: an invasion of hell itself. They watched me, their large, luminous eyes filled with a mixture of terror and a dawning, terrible hope. They were my army, my children, my sin. And I would lead them down into the dark.
