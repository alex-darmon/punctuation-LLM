“You will have to feel. Do not be squeamish. You are a man of science, are you not? Think of it as a problem in tactile perception.” A faint disturbance in the air, a shifting of dust motes in the lamplight, was the only sign of movement. “My jacket and shirt are gone. Burnt away. Start with the shoulder. It is the worst.”

Edevane reached out a trembling hand into the empty space. His fingers met warm, firm flesh. It was a profound shock, this contact with the unseen. He flinched back.

“Steady,” the Voice hissed. “Do you want me to bleed all over your precious books? Get on with it.”

Gritting his teeth, Edevane dipped a wad of cotton in the warm, carbolic-laced water. He reached out again, his fingers exploring the invisible contours of a shoulder and arm. He could feel the heat of the skin, the wiry tension of muscle beneath. Then his fingers found the wound. It was a broad patch of raw, blistered flesh, slick and ragged. The texture was horrifying. Edevane had to fight the urge to gag. He began to clean the area, his touch as gentle as he could manage. The invisible body beneath his hands winced and stiffened, a sharp, guttural sound of pain escaping from the air itself.

“Careful, you clumsy oaf,” Davies snarled.

“I am doing my best!” Edevane retorted, a spark of anger cutting through his fear. “This is not exactly a conventional procedure!”

He worked in silence for several minutes, cleaning the burns on the shoulder, arm, and down the left side of a solid, muscular torso. The sheer physicality of it was bewildering. He was acutely aware of the man’s breathing, the tightening of his unseen stomach muscles against the pain, the very heat of his life force radiating into the air. When he was done cleaning, he began to apply the bandages, wrapping the linen strips around a body he could only perceive by touch. It was like blindfolding himself and attempting sculpture. He wrapped the shoulder, then the arm, then wound a broad strip around the man’s chest.

“Tighter,” the Voice instructed, its tone strained. “It will help to hold me together.”

When he had finished, Edevane sat back on his heels, exhausted. A collection of freshly wrapped, pristine white bandages now hung in the air before him, rising and falling with the rhythm of breathing. It was a sight more grotesque than any blood or gore.

“Better,” the Voice said. A disembodied sigh of relief. “Much better. You are more useful than you look, Edevane.” The bandages shifted as the invisible man presumably got to his feet. “Now. Get me a drink. The good whiskey. I have earned it. And get one for yourself. You look as though you are about to faint.”

Edevane rose and went to the cabinet where he kept a decanter of Scotch. His hands still shook as he poured two glasses. He handed one into the empty air. It was taken from him by an unseen, steady hand. He watched, mesmerised, as the glass tilted and the amber liquid within it vanished.

“Ah,” the Voice said, with satisfaction. “That is the stuff.”

Edevane drained his own glass in two desperate swallows, the alcohol burning a welcome fire in his chest. He turned to face the centre of the room, where the new bandages hovered. “Now,” he said, his voice finding a new firmness. “You will tell me everything. I have earned that, I think. I am your accomplice. I have lied to the police. I have dressed your wounds. I will not be a blind servant in my own house. What is this… this madness? How have you done it?”

There was a low chuckle. The armchair creaked again. “So the worm turns. Very well, Edevane. A fair request. You, of all people, should appreciate it. It is not magic. It is physics. Pure, beautiful, terrible physics.”

“The machine,” Edevane said, gesturing towards the humming contraption.

“The Harmoniser,” the Voice corrected him. “The heart of the matter. You remember my paper on quantum resonance fields? The one the Royal Society dismissed as ‘imaginative speculation’?”

“I remember it,” Edevane said. “It was theoretical to the point of fantasy. You postulated that by manipulating the resonance of cellular matter, you could alter its refractive index.”

“Precisely! They called me a dreamer. A charlatan! But I did it, Edevane. I built it! It is not about bleaching the body’s pigments, as that fool in the penny dreadfuls imagined. That is a child’s idea. This… this is about persuading light to ignore you. To pass directly through you as if you were a vacuum. The Harmoniser generates a specific resonance field. It tunes the very substance of the body, every cell, every molecule, to a state of perfect transparency.”

Edevane stared at the machine, at the pulsing violet light. “But the energy required… the stability… it would be impossible to maintain.”

“Difficult, not impossible,” said Davies. “That is its function. It does not create the state; it maintains it. It is a tuning fork for my entire being. Without its constant, stabilising hum, the resonance would collapse. The results would be… catastrophic.”

“Catastrophic how?”

“A rather messy and instantaneous return to visibility, I should imagine. Possibly in a thousand screaming pieces. I have not been inclined to test the theory. Which is why the destruction of my laboratory tonight was such a damnable inconvenience. This,” the Voice said, and the air seemed to shimmer around the humming machine, “is the only other one in existence. My backup. My salvation. And it is here. In your house.”

Edevane felt a cold dread that had nothing to do with the man and everything to do with the machine. He was tethered to this creature not just by fear, but by this humming brass engine. His safety was tied to its continued function. “And this is what you call ‘great things’?” Edevane asked, his voice dripping with scorn. “Hiding in my house, dependent on a machine to keep from flying apart?”

“That,” the Voice said, and its tone lost all its scientific ardour and took on a chilling, zealous quality, “is merely the beginning. This is not a parlour trick, Edevane. It is a new state of existence. A higher state. Man has been a slave to his body, to his visibility, for all of time. We are seen, therefore we are judged, constrained, caged. But an invisible man… an invisible man is a god.”

Edevane took a step back. “A god? You are a fugitive with burns on your back.”

“A temporary setback! Think, man! Use your imagination! Why stop at one? With your help, with your resources, we can refine the process. Simplify it. Create others. An Invisible College, Edevane. A secret fraternity of men who can walk through walls, who can stand in the secret councils of the world, unseen and unheard. We could command politics, finance, war! We could topple kings and build empires from the shadows. The world will be our laboratory, and humanity our subject. No more grubbing for grants, no more begging for recognition from fools like the Royal Society. We will have true power!”

The sheer, unadulterated megalomania of it stole Edevane’s breath. This was not the voice of a scientist. It was the voice of a madman, a tyrant in waiting. “That is monstrous,” he whispered. “You are talking about the end of freedom. The end of privacy. It is a fantasy of absolute tyranny.”

“It is the next stage of evolution!” the Voice boomed, and the whiskey glass was slammed down on a nearby table with a loud crack. “And you will help me. You have no choice. You think your little lie to Colonel Adye was the end of it? I have been in this town for a week, Edevane. I have been in your house before. I have read your letters. I have seen your research notes.”

Edevane felt his blood run cold.

“I know about your… financial difficulties,” the Voice continued, its tone a silky, venomous whisper. “I know about the rejection of your grant application from Cambridge, the one you told no one about. I know you fear your career is stalling, that you are becoming a provincial nobody. I can give you everything you want, Edevane. Resources. Recognition. A share in the future. Or… I can destroy you. A few whispered words in the right ears. A few strategically misplaced documents. Your reputation would be in tatters. Who would they believe? A respected, if struggling, professor, or the disembodied voice of a madman? It would be so easy to drive you insane.”

The trap was complete. It was not a cage of steel bars, but one of circumstance, of fear and ambition. Davies had seen the cracks in his life and was now wedging them open with terrifying precision.

Suddenly, a new sound intruded. The steady, low hum of the Harmoniser faltered. It dropped in pitch, rising again in a wavering, sickly groan. The violet light in the tubes flickered, turning a muddy orange.

“What was that?” the Voice shrieked, a new note of pure panic in it. “The generator! The generator you old fool, it’s unstable!”

In the air, the pristine white bandages seemed to flicker, to shimmer at the edges. A pained, strangled cry came from the armchair. “Do something! Edevane, now!”

Edevane was galvanised. His own survival was linked to that hum. He rushed past the desk to the corner of the room where the small petrol generator chugged away, powering the machine. The engine was sputtering, running rough. He had been so distracted he had not noticed its irregular beat. He knelt, his scientific mind taking over, pushing aside the terror. The fuel line. It must be a blockage in the fuel line.

His fingers, stained with invisible blood and carbolic, fumbled with the copper pipe. He blew through it sharply. A small clot of debris shot out the other end. He reconnected it, adjusted the choke, and cranked the engine again. It sputtered once, twice, then caught, settling back into its steady, rhythmic chugging.

In the centre of the room, the Harmoniser’s hum returned to its pure, low frequency. The light in the tubes brightened again to a clean, pulsing violet.

The sound of ragged breathing came from the armchair. “Close,” the Voice rasped. “God, that was… close.”

Edevane stood up, his hands grimy with oil. He had done it. He had saved him. He had chosen to maintain the monster’s existence rather than risk seeing what would happen if he did not. The choice, if it had ever been his, was gone. He was no longer just an accomplice; he was a mechanic. He was a keeper.

The bandages in the chair were still. “You see, Edevane?” the Voice said, its confidence slowly returning, infused with a chilling smugness. “You see how much we need each other? You are indispensable.”

Edevane looked from the pulsing, arcane machine to the empty chair where a bandaged nothing sat. He looked at his own trembling, filthy hands. He had wanted to be a man who discovered the secrets of the universe. Instead, he had become the janitor for a man who wanted to conquer it. The hum of the machine filled the silence, a steady, monotonous drone that sounded like the end of his world.

“You want whiskey?” said Edevane, his voice a dry croak. “I thought Kemp offered that.”

“Do not speak that name,” the Voice hissed, and the bandages twitched with a sudden, violent energy. “That weak-willed, sentimental fool. He had his chance to be part of the new world, and he chose the old one. He chose morality. Imagine! A man of science choosing morality over progress.” A sound that was almost a chuckle, but without any mirth, came from the armchair. “There will be no more Kemps, Edevane. Only men like you. Sensible men. Now, the whiskey.”

Edevane moved like a man in a dream, or rather, a nightmare from which he could not wake. He retrieved a glass and a decanter from a small sideboard. His hand trembled so much that the neck of the decanter chattered against the rim of the glass, a frantic, tiny percussion against the steady thrum of the Harmoniser. He filled it nearly to the brim.

“Where are you?” he asked, his eyes scanning the empty space above the chair.

“Here, you idiot,” the Voice said, with a weary sort of impatience. A hand, or the suggestion of a hand, materialised for a fraction of a second as a faint shimmer in the air, then was gone. It was enough. Edevane extended the glass. It was plucked from his fingers with a startling firmness. He saw the tumbler suspend itself in the air, tilt, and then heard a long, satisfying gulp.

“Ah. Better,” said the Voice. “Much better. It is a peculiar sort of chill, this state. It seeps into the bones. Not a cold of the air, but a cold of the atoms. As if every particle of me is a little too far from its neighbour.” The glass was set down with a solid clink on the table beside the chair. “Now, about my plans.”

Edevane remained standing, a statue of apprehension. “Plans?”

“Of course, plans! Did you think I went through… all this,” the voice was thick with a self-pity that was somehow more menacing than rage, “for a mere parlor trick? To frighten rustics in an inn? I am not Griffin. His was a chemist’s mind, brilliant but linear. He saw a key and thought only of what door it might unlock. I see the key, and I see how to remake the entire house, the street, the city! Invisibility is not a state of being, Edevane. It is the ultimate form of access.”

He spoke now with a growing fervour, the weariness gone, replaced by the zeal of a prophet. The dressing gown, Edevane noted with a sick fascination, seemed to expand, to take up more space, as if puffed up with the ego of its wearer.

“Think of it. The secrets whispered in Parliament. The strategies laid out in the boardrooms of the great banks. The confessions murmured in the most private chambers. All of it, all of it, will be mine. Not to listen to, you understand. But to use. To leverage. A word here, a stolen document there. A nudge to this stock, a ruinous rumour for that politician. I will not be a king, Edevane. Kings are targets; they sit on thrones and wait for the assassin’s knife. I will be the nervous system of the nation. The unseen hand that guides the seen. I will be the ghost in the machine of empire.”

Edevane swallowed. The sheer, breathtaking arrogance of it was almost as stunning as the science that made it possible. “And me?” he said, the words feeling small and pathetic in the face of such megalomania. “What is my part in this… this nervous system?”

“You?” said the Voice, and the empty space where a head should be seemed to turn towards him. “You are the Cerebellum. You regulate. You maintain. This machine,” a bandaged arm gestured towards the humming Harmoniser, “is my only vulnerability. It is the anchor that keeps me from… drifting. You will tend to it. You will improve it. You will make it silent, more powerful, perhaps even portable. You will be my mechanic, my engineer, my one and only confidant. Your reward will be a world remade to our specifications. A world of order. A world of true, rational control.”

The word “confidant” hung in the air, a grotesque mockery of friendship. Edevane looked at the machine, its violet tubes pulsing like some infernal organ. He had built it from theory, a curiosity of wave mechanics and biological resonance. He had never imagined this. He had been so proud of its intricate wiring, its precise calibration. Now he saw it as a monstrous thing, a leash and a lifeline all in one.

“It cannot be done,” Edevane said, a flicker of his old scientific self asserting itself. “The energy requirements… the instability… to make it portable is a fantasy.”

“Then make it reality!” the Voice snapped. “Griffin worked with pestles and mortars in a grubby little room! We have this! We have your brain, Edevane! Do not tell me what cannot be done. I have done the impossible! Your task is merely the monumentally difficult.”

A frantic desire to argue, to point out the flaws in the physics, the sheer impracticality, rose in Edevane, but it was quelled by a new, more immediate sound. A sharp, percussive rapping from downstairs.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

The sound was utterly alien in the sealed world of the laboratory. Both of them, the seen and the unseen, froze. The humming of the Harmoniser suddenly seemed deafening.

“Who is that?” the Voice whispered, all the grandiosity instantly gone, replaced by the sharp paranoia of a cornered animal. “You told me you were secluded here! You told me no one ever comes!”

“No one does,” Edevane whispered back, his heart hammering against his ribs. “Hardly ever. The postman… a delivery…”

Knock. Knock. KNOCK. Louder this time. More insistent. Followed by a hearty, bellowing voice that carried easily through the floorboards.

“Dr. Edevane! Are you in there, sir? Constable Roberts! Just making my rounds!”

Edevane felt the blood drain from his face. The dressing gown in the chair, which had been perfectly still, now gave a violent, convulsive shudder. A low, guttural curse issued from it, a sound of pure, venomous rage.

“Get rid of him,” the Voice commanded, its whisper a razor’s edge from his ear. He felt a cold spot on his neck, the unnerving proximity of the unseen mouth. “Get rid of him now. And Edevane… be convincing. For both our sakes.”

Edevane’s legs felt like lead. He looked at the door of the laboratory, then at the armchair. He could not leave Davies here. He could not take him. He was paralysed.

“Go!” the Voice hissed, and he felt a sharp, painful prod in his back. It was like being poked with a cold iron bar.

The jolt sent him into motion. He stumbled out of the laboratory, pulling the heavy oak door closed behind him. The sudden dimness of the hallway was a brief relief. He leaned against the wall for a moment, composing himself, trying to slow his breathing. From downstairs, the constable called out again.

“Doctor? Everything alright? Heard some funny noises from up here earlier. Like a motor running.”

“Coming!” Edevane called out, his own voice sounding thin and reedy to his ears. He straightened his coat, ran a hand through his dishevelled hair, and began the long walk down the stairs.

Constable Roberts stood on his doorstep, a large, solid man whose physical presence filled the frame. His helmet was tucked under his arm, and his face, ruddy with the evening chill, was a mask of placid concern.

“Evening, Doctor,” he said, his eyes crinkling at the corners. “Sorry to disturb you. But Mrs. Higgins down the lane, she mentioned hearing a generator start up. Said it sounded like it was coming from your property. Highly unusual, she said. Thought I’d best check everything was in order.”

“A generator? Ah, yes. Yes, constable. My apologies,” Edevane said, forcing a smile that felt like a grimace. “Just a little experiment. Pushing the boundaries of… of electrical accumulation. Nothing to be alarmed about.”

Roberts’s gaze drifted past Edevane, into the dark hallway. “All on your own in this big house, sir? Must get lonely.”

“I prefer the solitude,” Edevane said quickly. “It is conducive to my work.”

“Of course, of course,” the constable said, though his eyes did not lose their inquisitive edge. “It’s just… the noise, you see. And Mrs. Higgins also mentioned a… well, she described it as a scream. Earlier this afternoon. A man’s voice.”

Edevane’s blood ran cold. The generator sputtering. Davies’s cry of panic. “A scream?” he said, feigning puzzlement. “Good heavens, no. Nothing of the sort. Perhaps an animal. A fox, maybe. They make the most dreadful sounds.”

“A fox,” Roberts repeated slowly, his gaze still fixed on the shadows behind Edevane. “Right. A fox. Inside the house?”

“No! No, of course not,” Edevane stammered. “In the garden. The sound… it must have carried. Yes. That’s it.”

He was aware, acutely aware, of a faint creak from the top of the stairs. He did not dare look. He knew, with a certainty that chilled him to the bone, that Davies was there, listening. A silent, invisible predator perched just out of sight. He could feel the weight of that unseen stare.

“Well, as long as you’re sure, sir,” Roberts said, finally pulling his gaze back to Edevane. He seemed to be deliberating. “Perhaps you wouldn’t mind if I just had a quick look around? For your own safety, you understand. A scream is a scream.”

Panic, pure and absolute, seized Edevane. “No!” he said, far too loudly. He moderated his tone, trying to sound merely put-upon. “Constable, I assure you, it is quite unnecessary. I am in the middle of a delicate procedure. The calibrations are extremely sensitive. Any disturbance…”

He trailed off. He could feel the invisible pressure from the top of the stairs, a silent command to succeed. The consequences of failure were unthinkable. He imagined the constable pushing past him, finding the humming machine, the empty chair with its depressed cushion, the disembodied bandages.

To his immense relief, Roberts seemed to take the hint of the eccentric, private scientist. He sighed, a gust of wind carrying the scent of damp wool and pipe tobacco.

“Very well, Doctor. If you say so. But do call if you need anything. Anything at all. These old houses play tricks on the ears.” He put his helmet back on his head, a gesture of finality.

“I will. Thank you, constable. Good evening,” Edevane said, already trying to close the door.

“One more thing, sir,” Roberts said, his hand stopping the door from shutting. Edevane’s heart leaped into his throat. “Your hand, sir. Are you alright?”

Edevane looked down. His right hand, the one resting on the door, was smeared with grease and oil from his frantic work on the generator. And something else. A dark, reddish brown stain near the cuff. Blood. Invisible blood that had become visible.

“Oh, that,” he said, his mind racing. “Just a cut. A slipped wrench. Occupational hazard, you know.” He laughed, a brittle, unconvincing sound. “Clumsy of me.”

Roberts looked from the hand to Edevane’s face, his expression unreadable. For a long moment, the world seemed to hold its breath. Then, the constable nodded slowly. “Take care of that, sir. Can turn nasty if you’re not careful. Good evening, Dr. Edevane.”

He turned and walked down the path, his boots crunching on the gravel. Edevane watched him until his form was swallowed by the darkness, then he closed the door, bolted it, and leaned his forehead against the cool wood, his body shaking with a relief so profound it felt like pain.

He had done it. He had lied, and he had been believed.

He turned, and saw the scarlet dressing gown standing at the top of the stairs. It was perfectly still. The silence from it was more terrifying than any shout. Slowly, Edevane began to climb the stairs, returning to his master.

“That was clumsy,” the Voice said, as soon as he was back in the laboratory. The door clicked shut behind him. The air was thick with menace. “A slipped wrench? You sounded like a boy caught in the orchard.”

“He is gone, isn’t he?” Edevane said, his voice trembling with exhaustion and residual fear. “That is what matters.”

“What matters is the margin of error!” the Voice snarled. The dressing gown strode across the room, the empty sleeves swinging. “The blood on your cuff. The pathetic lie about a fox. You are a terrible liar, Edevane. We must work on that.”

Suddenly, the dressing gown stopped before him. He felt the chill again, the intense, localised cold of the Invisible Man’s body. A bandaged hand rose and seized the front of his coat, twisting the fabric.

“Do you understand what would have happened if he had come in here?” the Voice whispered, the words a venomous spray of air against his cheek. “He would have seen the machine. He would have asked questions. I would have had to kill him. Here. In your laboratory. And then, Edevane, I would have had to kill you, for being so incompetent as to make it necessary. Do you understand?”

Edevane could only nod, his eyes wide with horror, fixed on the empty space within the collar of the robe.

“Good.” The grip released him. Edevane stumbled back. “You have passed your first test. Barely. Now, you will bring me food. A proper meal. The cutlets were an appetiser. And more whiskey. My nerves are quite shattered.”

As Edevane turned to obey, a broken shell of a man, the Voice spoke again, in a tone that had changed completely. It was no longer angry, but contemplative, almost scientific.

“It is a curious thing, Edevane. The fear. While he was there, I felt it too. A primitive, animal terror. The hunter being hunted. My heart, I believe, was beating quite fast. I could feel the vibration of it through my own ribs.”

The bandaged form had moved to the armchair and sat down. The cloth creaked.

“But the blood,” the Voice continued, musingly. “Your little accident with the cuff confirms a theory. My tissues are invisible. My blood, my living blood, is invisible. But once it leaves the living system, once it coagulates… it becomes matter like any other. It becomes visible.” A dry, rasping laugh filled the room. “A fatal flaw. A man who cannot bleed without leaving a trail. I am Achilles, Edevane, and my heel is the whole of my circulatory system. We must be very, very careful.”

The Invisible Man fell silent. Edevane, halfway to the door, paused. He looked back at the pulsing violet machine, at the empty dressing gown slumped in the chair, at the whiskey glass on the table. He was the keeper of the machine. He was the forager of food. He was the liar at the door. And now he knew the monster’s weakness. Not the great, humming engine, but something far more intimate. A simple cut. A drop of blood. It was a terrifying thought, but for the first time since the voice had first spoken his name, it was a thought that contained the barest, most desperate splinter of hope. He went downstairs to ransack the larder, his mind no longer empty with terror, but filled with a terrible and dangerous new calculus.

The larder was a small, cool room, smelling of earth and smoked meats and old stone. Edevane’s hands trembled as he took down a joint of cold roast beef. His mind was not on the food. The calculus, he thought, the terrible calculus. He saw a long carving knife hanging from a magnetic rack. The steel glinted in the gloom. It would be a simple thing. A lunge into the space occupied by the dressing gown. A frantic, blind struggle. A torrent of suddenly visible blood. He imagined the scene with a clarity that sickened him. The red ruin of it all, staining his floor, his hands, his life. And then what? A dead man, visible at last, in his laboratory. Questions. The police. The end of everything. No, that was the act of a brute, a cornered animal. He was a man of science. The calculus was not one of murder, but of control.

He was a prisoner, that much was certain; but a prisoner with a key, or the shadow of one, was a different sort of captive altogether. The Invisible Man needed him. He needed his house, his knowledge, his hands. He needed him to lie, to fetch, to maintain the great, humming engine of his invisibility. And now, Edevane knew, he could be wounded. He could be marked. He could be made traceable. A single drop of blood was a bell that could not be unrung. He took the beef, a loaf of bread, a wedge of hard cheese, and a fresh bottle of whiskey. He felt a strange new calmness settle over him, the cold precision of a man preparing an experiment. He was no longer merely a victim. He was a variable in the equation.

When he returned, the scarlet dressing gown was pacing again, a restless, headless sentinel in the cluttered lab. “Took you long enough,” the Voice snapped. “A man could starve. Did you find anything substantial?”

“Beef. Bread. Cheese,” Edevane said, his own voice sounding distant to him. He set the provisions on the light table he had brought up earlier. “And more whiskey.”

“Good. Good.” The dressing gown ceased its pacing and came to the table. A knife was lifted from the tray, Edevane flinched, but it was merely the bread knife. It sawed through the loaf with vicious energy. A chunk of beef was torn from the joint by an unseen hand. The sounds of eating began again, louder this time, more ravenous. Edevane stood by the door, watching the grotesque pantomime. It was like feeding a ghost, a hungry, demanding, terrifying ghost.

“You know, Edevane,” said the Invisible Man, his mouth full, the words thick and indistinct. “I was a fool. I see that now. My plan was flawless, my science was perfect, but I underestimated the sheer, mulish stupidity of the common man. The landlord, the barman, the villagers… insects. Apes in clothes. They see one thing they cannot understand, and they panic. They want to crush it, to cage it. There is no curiosity, no wonder. Only fear.”

Edevane said nothing. He simply watched the whiskey bottle tilt and pour a generous measure into the glass. The glass then lifted and hovered near the collar of the robe.

“They drove me out,” the Voice continued, its tone growing louder with self-pity and rage. “Hounded me. Threw stones. Fired guns at me! At me! A man who has transcended the very limits of human perception. It would be laughable if it were not so infuriating. I needed a confederate. A partner. I chose… poorly. A tramp. A pathetic, shambling creature I thought I could control. He saw only the money. My notebooks. My salvation. He tried to betray me.” A piece of cheese vanished from the plate. “He is paying for that. But it left me… adrift. Wounded. Hunted. Stumbling through the night, a freak of nature with nowhere to turn. And then I saw the light in your window. Dr. Edevane. I remembered you from the Royal College. Quiet. Ambitious. Always scribbling in the back row. A proper man of science. Not like those other fools. I thought, here. Here is a port in the storm. And I was right, wasn’t I?”

“I… suppose so,” Edevane managed.

“You suppose so!” A sharp, humourless laugh. “Of course I was right. You understand. You must understand. This is the greatest discovery in the history of the world. Greater than gravity. Greater than electricity. We are at the dawn of a new age, Edevane. An age of invisible men.” The dressing gown leaned forward, its empty sleeves resting on the table. “And you are the first disciple.”

It was then that it happened. A change. It was not a loud event, but a subtle, sickening shift in the room’s atmosphere. The steady, deep-violet pulse of the great machine in the corner faltered. The low hum that had become the laboratory’s heartbeat changed pitch, climbing into a strained, high whine. The light flickered, turned a bilious, unhealthy yellow, then sputtered back to a wavering violet.

“What was that?” the Voice snapped, all trace of grandiloquence gone, replaced by a raw, animal alarm.

“I don’t know,” Edevane said, his own heart leaping into his throat.

The machine whined again, louder this time. The violet light died completely, plunging the corner into darkness for a second before returning as a strobing, frantic pulse.

“The machine!” cried the Invisible Man, and his voice was a shriek. He was on his feet in an instant, the chair scraping backwards. “Edevane! The machine! What is it doing?”

“The resonance coils… perhaps a power fluctuation,” Edevane stammered, moving towards the apparatus. The air around it was hot, smelling of ozone.

“Fix it! For God’s sake, man, fix it!” The scarlet robe lunged towards him, and he felt the frantic, cold grip on his arm. The strength was astonishing. “Do you know what will happen if it fails? Do you?”

“Let go of me! I cannot work if you hold me,” Edevane said, his voice sharp with a fear that was suddenly mingled with something else: authority. He was the master of this machine. He pulled his arm free, and for a moment, the Invisible Man simply stood there, a frantic emptiness clad in scarlet.

Edevane peered at the main chronometer dial. The needle was shuddering, bouncing erratically. “The primary regulator is unstable. The feedback loop is overloading.” He reached for a side panel, his fingers fumbling with the latches. “I need to bypass the capacitor bank and reset the sequence manually.”

“Then do it! Hurry, you fool!” The Voice was a desperate whisper, right beside his ear. Edevane could feel the chill of the unseen body, feel the panicked, shallow breaths against his neck. The hunter was terrified. The god was afraid of his own altar.

Edevane pulled the panel open. He was promptly lifted from his feet and thrown against the wall, where he slid to the floor, dazed. Adye, cool as ever, picked up the fallen poker. “He’s by the window! Box him in!” The air filled with the sounds of struggle; grunts, curses, the splintering of wood. Kemp peered over the edge of the desk. He could see nothing but a whirlwind of violence. Adye and the first constable were swinging their pokers at a moving, invisible target, their blows occasionally landing with a sickening thud, but more often meeting empty air. Then, with horrifying speed, the tide turned. The empty suit of clothes suddenly lunged at Adye, and the Colonel went down with a cry, clutching his head. The clothes stood over him for a second, then seemed to simply come apart. The jacket was flung one way, the trousers another. “I am free of your foolish traps!” the Voice roared, now sounding as if it came from near the shattered window. “And now you will learn what it means to cross me!” There was a sound of scrabbling on the outer sill, and then silence. You have unmasked the monster. And now, God help us, he is loose in our town. And he is very, very angry.”

Kemp stared at the seated dressing gown, at the cigar smoke that gave it a phantasmal head. The initial shock was giving way to a riot of other feelings; a terror, certainly, but also a profound and dreadful curiosity. He, Dr. Kemp, a man of rational inquiry, was faced with the living embodiment of a scientific impossibility. It was monstrous, it was terrifying, but it was also… magnificent. “You want me to help you?”

“I demand it,” the Invisible Man corrected him, his tone hardening. “You owe me that. We are men of the same cloth, you and I. “And if I refuse?” Kemp asked, though he knew the question was foolish.

The cigar was placed on the edge of the table, its glowing tip hovering unsupported. The dressing gown shifted. “Then I will begin what I must call, for lack of a better term, a Reign of Terror,” said the Voice, with a calm that was more frightening than any shout. “I will start with you, of course. I will make your life an exquisite misery. I will shatter your work, burn your house, whisper in your ear at night until you go mad. And then I will move on to this town. Kemp felt a cold sweat break out on his forehead. This was not the desperate, wounded man of a few minutes ago. This was something else entirely. A mind brilliant, unbound, and utterly without scruple. “You are insane,” he whispered.

“Am I?” The dressing gown’s sleeve gestured vaguely. “The world has driven me so. But my intellect is intact. I am simply… liberated. From convention. From morality. From visibility. Now, Kemp. The authority in the voice was absolute. Kemp, his will momentarily shattered, found himself nodding. “Yes,” he said. “Alright.”

“Good,” said the Invisible Man. “You are a sensible fellow after all, Kemp.” The dressing gown slumped further into the chair. The cigar glowed a final, defiant red before it was dropped to the floor and crushed by an unseen foot. Silence fell in the room, a silence more profound and terrifying than all the mad talk that had preceded it. Kemp sat frozen, listening to the faint, almost imperceptible sound of a man breathing in the empty chair opposite him. The night had just begun, and he was already a prisoner in his own home.

The next morning dawned grey and oppressive. Kemp had not slept. He had spent the night in a chair downstairs, a heavy poker by his side, starting at every creak of the floorboards, every gust of wind against the window panes. He was a man besieged by an idea. The Invisible Man. The Reign of Terror. His mind, a precise instrument of logic, felt as though it had been bludgeoned. He looked at his own hands, his solid, visible flesh, with a kind of wonder.

He made coffee, his movements stiff and automatic. He found his housekeeper, a stout and imperturbable woman named Mrs. Hallan, in the kitchen. “Mrs. Hallan,” he began, his voice strained. “I have a guest. An old colleague. He has… had an accident. His face is terribly disfigured. He is swathed in bandages and will keep to his room. He must not be disturbed on any account.”

The housekeeper looked at him with placid curiosity. “An accident, sir? Poor soul. Will he be wanting breakfast?”

“I will take it up myself,” said Kemp quickly. “He requires complete privacy. You understand?”

“Perfectly, sir,” she said, and turned back to her duties, a model of incurious discretion. Kemp felt a flicker of relief. The first lie was told. The conspiracy had begun.

He carried a tray upstairs. His heart hammered against his ribs as he approached his own bedroom door. He knocked softly.

“Come in, Kemp,” said the Voice. It was rested, stronger.

Kemp entered. The room was as he had left it, save that the dressing gown was now draped over the back of the chair. There was no sign of his guest, only the depression in the bed where a body had lain. “I have your breakfast.”

“Put it on the table,” said the Voice, seemingly from near the window. “And any news? Have you seen a paper?”

“I have not dared to go out,” Kemp confessed.

“Foolish. We must have information. What is the world saying about my little escapade at Iping? They must be in a panic. Go and fetch a paper. Now.”

The command brooked no argument. Kemp went downstairs, put on his hat and coat, and stepped out into the streets of Burdock. The air was cool, but it felt charged with a strange tension. People spoke in hushed, excited groups. He bought a paper from a boy at the corner, his hand trembling as he exchanged the coins. The headlines were sensational, black and stark.

“THE INVISIBLE MAN! TERROR IN IPING. BIZARRE EVENTS BAFFLE POLICE.”

The story was a garbled mess of eyewitness accounts; of money floating in the air, of a vicarage ransacked, of a ghostly presence that fought off a mob and vanished. It was treated as a mass delusion, a bizarre piece of local folklore come to life. Kemp felt a grim satisfaction. They did not understand. Not yet. He hurried back to the house, the newspaper clutched in his hand like a verdict.

Colonel Adye, the chief constable of the district, had established a temporary headquarters in the Burdock town hall. He was a man of military bearing, with a crisp moustache and tired, intelligent eyes. He looked up as Kemp was shown into his office.

“Dr. Kemp,” he said, his tone wary. “To what do I owe the pleasure? I had thought you a man of seclusion.”

“Colonel Adye,” Kemp began, trying to keep his voice steady. He was acutely aware of the empty space beside him, of the unseen presence that was listening to every word. “I have come about this… this business in Iping. “No,” Kemp said, finding a sliver of defiance. “This is madness. I will not be a party to burglary, to a war with the police. I am a respectable man.”

The mad, seductive logic of it washed over Kemp. Kings. The idea was monstrous, but it took root in the fertile ground of his terror and his awe. He looked around his quiet, book-lined study, the symbol of his ordered, rational life. It already felt like a relic from another existence. He was no longer Dr. Kemp, the detached scientist. The Invisible Man laughed. It was a dry, rustling sound, like dead leaves skittering across pavement. “That’s better, Kemp. Much better. First, we need a map of Port Stowe. And then you must prepare the carriage.”
