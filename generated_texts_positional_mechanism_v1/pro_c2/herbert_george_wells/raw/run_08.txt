I cannot prove my hypothesis; indeed, what proof could I offer from a world that may no longer exist? It is a common assumption that life, having once begun, will always seek to conquer its surroundings. But those who have never considered the deeper currents of evolution, such as the principle of conservation of energy, forget that an organism may find it more efficient to surrender to its environment than to fight it. As conditions change, a species might not adapt by growing stronger; it may adapt by becoming one with the very forces that threaten it. It may be that some terrible new form of life had already made this bargain. Whatever the theory, the fact of the matter is that the forest I had entered was fundamentally unlike our own.

“Well, upon my third day in that place—a day defined only by the dimming of the ever-present, sourceless glow—as I was documenting the curious, fluid patterns on the bark of a monolithic fungus, there occurred a most singular event. I had been mapping a region of particularly dense growth, a thicket of phosphorescent stalks that rose like the pipes of some colossal organ. The air, as always, was heavy with the scent of damp earth and decay, yet held a stillness that was utterly profound. By contrast with the gentle luminescence of the fungi, the spaces between them were pools of impenetrable black. I took a step into one such pool, my hand outstretched to feel my way. Suddenly I froze, my breath caught in my throat. A soft, rhythmic pulsing of light, a gentle aqua-marine throb, had begun in the darkness just ahead of me.

“My first instinct, born of a thousand tales of the deep sea, was of some monstrous, bioluminescent predator. I gripped the geological hammer at my belt and took a half step back, my eyes fixed upon the pulsating glow. I dared not turn and run. Then the thought of the absolute lack of any aggressive fauna I had yet encountered calmed me somewhat. And I recalled the complete and total silence of this world. Overcoming my trepidation, I held my position and waited. I will not deny that my heart hammered against my ribs. I raised my electric lantern, its beam a harsh, yellow spear. At once the gentle pulsing ceased, and the light winked out. In its place, bathed in the beam of my torch, was a figure. It recoiled, not with a sound, but with a sudden, violent contraction of its form. I saw, for a brief second, a translucent, bell-shaped body, trailing a dozen or more slender, root-like tentacles. It was like some great jellyfish, yet it floated not in water, but in the air, its tentacles brushing the fungal floor.

“My impression of it is, naturally, fleeting; but I know it was a pale, membranous grey, and that its entire body seemed to be a network of fine, blue-glowing veins; also that it had no face, no eyes, no discernible features of any kind. But, as I say, the moment my light touched it, it drew itself inwards and shot away. I cannot even say whether it swam through the air, or was merely carried on some unfelt current. After a moment of stunned inaction, I plunged after it into the glowing thicket. I could not see it at first; but, after my eyes adjusted to the dimmer light, I came upon a vast, open cavern where the organ-pipe fungi grew to the size of cathedral spires. A sudden notion struck me. Were these beings the gardeners of this place? I switched off my lantern, and in the ensuing gloom, I saw it. The creature, and a dozen others like it, were drifting serenely among the stalks. They were tending to them; their tentacles caressed the fungi, and where they touched, the glow of the fungus brightened and swirled with new patterns. It was a silent, ineffable symphony of light. It was so like a community of bees! Each one drifted from stalk to stalk, and now I saw for the first time that they were drawing some substance from the air, a fine, glittering dust, and applying it to the fungal growths.

“I do not know how long I stood there, a silent observer in that alien church. It was not for some minutes that I could fully process what I was seeing. But, slowly, the truth began to form in my mind: that life had not simply colonised this space, but had merged with its very processes: that these graceful, silent gardeners were not masters of their world, but its willing servants, its very nerve endings.

“I thought of the glittering dust and of my theory of airborne nutrients. I began to suspect its true origin. And what, I wondered, was this Medusa doing in my understanding of a competitive ecosystem? How was its placid labour related to the utter stillness of this world? And what purpose did this endless, silent gardening serve? I sat on a low, spongy growth telling myself that, surely, there was a logic I could comprehend, and that here I must stay to find the answer to my questions. And yet I was terribly, terribly alone. As I sat there, two of the creatures drifted close to one another in their silent work. Their tentacles intertwined; for a moment, their internal light flared brilliantly, a cascade of colours pulsing between them.

“They seemed to take no notice of me, this strange, solid, noisy creature in their midst. Apparently my very existence was beneath their form of perception; for when I tried to touch a passing tentacle, my hand passed through it as if through smoke, causing only a brief, agitated flicker in its light. But they were fascinated by my lantern, and when I switched it on, they would swarm towards it. I tried them again with my presence, and again I failed to elicit a response. So presently I retreated, my mind a vortex of new data; my assumptions and conclusions were shifting towards a new, startling configuration. I had now a clue to the nature of this silent world, to the purpose of the giant fungi, to the mystery of the glittering dust; to say nothing of a hint at the reason for the profound silence itself! And very dimly there came a suggestion towards the solution of the biological puzzle that had tormented me.

“Here was the startling new perspective. Plainly, this species of life was symbiotic to the point of near dissolution. There were three facts in particular which made me believe that its ethereal form was the result of a long, willing surrender of individuality. In the first place, there was the translucent quality, common in many simple organisms that have shed complex, unnecessary organs. Then, those light-based communications, with their lack of ambiguity, are a feature of purely collective consciousness; witness the hive mind of the ant or the bee. And last of all, that evident indifference to my solid form, that serene focus on their collective task, and that strange reaction to my hand; all reinforced the theory of a biology that had forsaken the individual for the whole.

“Around me, then, the entire forest was not a collection of plants and animals, but a single, thinking organism, and these drifting creatures were its thoughts. The presence of the nutrient dust and the care of the fungi everywhere showed how integrated was this system. What so logical, then, as to conclude that the purpose of this great entity was simply to exist, in perfect, silent, unthinking harmony? The notion was so alien that I at first rejected it, and went on to postulate the _why_ of this strange evolutionary path. I suspect you will find my theory to be quite mad; though, for my part, I quickly felt that it held a terrible, simple truth.

“At first, proceeding from the anxieties of our own species, it seemed clear as day to me that the gradual increase of the present merely technological and communicative connection between people was the key to the entire question. No doubt it will seem absurd to you, and quite unbelievable!; and yet even in our world there are movements that point this way. There is a desire to replace the messiness of speech with the efficiency of data; there is the internet, there are global networks, there are virtual worlds and shared consciousnesses, and they expand and deepen. Evidently, I thought, this desire had been followed to its ultimate conclusion. I mean that individuality had been deemed inefficient, a source of conflict and pain, until, in the end—! Even now, does not a man plugged into his screens and networks live in such a synthetic reality as to be practically cut off from the physical community around him?

“Again, the collectivist tendency—due, no doubt, to the increasing complexity of global problems, and the widening belief that the individual is powerless before them—is already leading to the surrender, in the name of the greater good, of considerable personal autonomy. About the world, for instance, we see the rise of ideologies that demand the subservience of the person to the state or the system. And this same widening desire for unity—which is due to the fear of chaos and the seductive promise of a frictionless existence—is a temptation towards a new kind of consciousness, a consciousness that we may not even recognise as our own.”

“I was drawn from these dark musings by a sudden and jarring disharmony in the placid symphony of the wood. A tremor of light, not of the usual gentle, conversational kind, pulsed through the nearest cluster of luminous beings. It was a flicker of what I can only describe as alarm, a frantic, chaotic shimmer that spoke not of communication but of pure distress. It was the visual equivalent of a scream. The soft, ambient hum that pervaded the glade faltered, dropping in pitch to a discordant thrum of anxiety. “My eyes followed the direction of the panic, scanning the verdant canopy and the moss-covered floor. At first I saw nothing. The forest remained a picture of unearthly serenity, yet the silent terror of the light-creatures was palpable, spreading from one group to another like a contagion. Then I saw it. A shadow, where no shadow should be. Not the soft, dappled shade of the great fungi, but a patch of absolute, light-devouring blackness, moving with a purpose that was utterly alien to this place. It scuttled low to the ground, a thing of chitin and hard edges, an ugly blot of stark reality on a canvas of dreams.

“As it came closer, its form resolved into a horror that my previous speculations had only dimly foreshadowed. It was vaguely humanoid in its basic structure, with two arms and two legs, but it was hunched and armoured, like some obscene fusion of a man and a beetle. There was no visible face, only a heavily ridged helmet of a head from which a complex, clicking mandible protruded. Its limbs were thick and powerful, ending not in hands but in multi-jointed claws, one of which was a heavy, sharpened spur, the other a grasping pincer. It moved with a scuttling, economical gait, its heavy form surprisingly swift, its entire being radiating an aura of brutal, single-minded avarice.

“This monstrous individualist paid no heed to me whatsoever; I was merely part of the scenery. Its entire focus was upon a small, isolated cluster of the light-beings that hovered near the base of a great, pearlescent fungus. The beings pulsed with frantic, silent pleas, their light flickering wildly, but they made no move to flee. It seemed the very concept of such an aggressive act was beyond their comprehension. The Carapaced Man, as I mentally named it, lunged forward with a speed that belied its bulk. The pincer-claw shot out and snatched one of the ethereal forms from the air.

“What happened next is difficult to describe. The light-creature, caught in the grip of the claw, did not struggle in any physical sense. Instead, its light intensified to a blinding nova of pure agony, a silent shriek that I felt in my very bones. The Carapaced Man brought the captured creature towards the mandibles on its helmet. There was a sickening crunch, a sound of grinding, and the beautiful, complex light of the being was extinguished in an instant. A faint, silvery vapour, all that remained of the creature’s physical substance, was drawn into an orifice beneath the mandibles. The armoured thing had fed. It had consumed a thought. It had devoured a soul.

“Having finished its grim meal, it paused for a moment, its helmeted head swivelling slowly as if scanning for another target. The rest of the light-beings had retreated, their collective glow dimmed to a frightened, sorrowful glimmer at the far end of the glade. The Carapaced Man seemed to deem the hunt over for now. It turned and scuttled away with the same relentless purpose, disappearing into the thick undergrowth, leaving behind a silence that was no longer peaceful, but empty and violated.

“I stood frozen, my scientific detachment utterly shattered. The neatness of my theories was a cold comfort in the face of such a raw and terrible act. It was one thing to speculate on the divergence of humanity, and quite another to witness the result: one heir of mankind literally consuming the other. I sank down upon the soft moss, my heart hammering against my ribs, the image of that brief, brutal act burned into my mind. The truth, in its full and terrible form, was dawning upon me. Man had not merely differentiated into two species; he had become his own predator and prey. He had split along the philosophical fault line of his own nature, and the two halves were now locked in a timeless, ghastly symbiosis of destruction.

“My graceful, thoughtless children of the light were not merely indolent poets; they were cattle. And this armoured, solitary brute was not just a hermit; he was the butcher. And what was the great scheme here? What was the economy of this new world? It was horribly simple. The Individual fed on the Collective. The Self survived by devouring the Other. The old, forgotten struggles of my own time, the debates between the rights of the one and the needs of the many, had been fought to their final, biological conclusion. Here, the arguments were over; only the appetite remained.

“I felt a surge of cold fury, followed by a profound and weary sadness. My fear had been replaced by a grim resolve. I was no longer a passive observer; I was a witness. I had to understand how this had come to pass. I had to know the history of this schism. Rising to my feet, I looked in the direction the Carapaved Man had gone. A trail of broken ferns and crushed moss marked its passage. Without a clear plan, driven by the sheer necessity of knowing, I began to follow it.

“The trail led me away from the lush, glowing heart of the forest. The great, luminous fungi became sparser, the air grew cooler, and the soft, humming ambience faded. I soon found myself in a transitional zone, a sort of no-man’s-land between two worlds. Here the ground was hard and stony, and the vegetation was tough and thorny. The world of the Collective was behind me; ahead lay the domain of the Individual. The path of the creature was easy to follow, for it made no effort at stealth. It simply crushed and broke whatever lay in its way, a moving furrow of pure entropy.

“After perhaps an hour of walking, I came to the edge of a great chasm, a raw wound in the earth. It was not a natural canyon, but appeared to be an ancient, colossal quarry, its sides cut in sheer, geometric cliffs. The trail of the Carapaced Man led directly to the edge and then down a series of rough-hewn steps carved into the rock face. Far below, at the bottom of this artificial valley, I could see structures. They were not the elegant, organic ruins I had grown accustomed to. These were brutalist, angular constructions, assembled from great blocks of dark stone and rusted metal plating, scavenged, it seemed, from some much older civilisation. It was a fortress-settlement built for one, a monument to solitude and defence. This was the creature’s lair.

“Hesitantly, I began my descent. The steps were steep and treacherous, clearly made for a different sort of anatomy than my own. As I climbed down, the last vestiges of the forest’s warmth vanished, replaced by a damp, metallic chill. The air here was sterile, devoid of the rich, loamy scent of the wood. It was the dead air of a tomb. Reaching the bottom, I crept cautiously towards the largest of the structures, a grim, blocky edifice that seemed to be the creature’s primary dwelling. The entrance was a simple, gaping hole, a dark maw from which a faint, unpleasant odour of ozone and decay emanated.

“I knew the risks. The creature could return at any moment. But the desire to know, the defining trait and perhaps the fatal flaw of my species, was too strong to resist. I drew a breath and stepped out of the grey twilight and into the absolute darkness of the lair. I fumbled in my pocket for my box of matches, my fingers clumsy with a mixture of cold and fear. The first match scraped, flared, and revealed a cavernous, single room.

“The interior was as stark and brutal as the exterior. The walls were bare rock and scavenged metal. The floor was littered with debris, but it was not the debris of neglect; it was the debris of function. Piles of scrap metal, sharpened flints, and curious mechanical components were sorted into rough heaps. In one corner was a nest of sorts, a tangled mass of dried fibres and what looked like metallic cloth. And everywhere, there were the shells. Discarded, broken carapaces, shed skins from the creature’s own growth, lay about like the armour of dead knights. This being lived a life of constant renewal, of shedding the old self to make way for a newer, stronger one.

“But it was on the far wall that my matchlight found the object of my quest. There, bolted to the stone, was a great plaque of some non-corroding alloy, perhaps the same one used for the Time Machine. It was not a work of art, but a document, its surface covered not in graceful script but in deep, gouged lettering, a text hammered into metal with a chisel and a furious strength. It was a declaration. A creed. A history.

“The language was a distant, simplified descendant of English, its grammar brutalised, its vocabulary stripped to the bare essentials of will and power. It took me some time, striking match after match in the oppressive dark, to decipher its terrible message. It spoke of a time of ‘The Great Un-Binding,’ when the ‘Old World’ was sick with ‘Other-Thought’ and the ‘Weak-Web.’ It described a schism, not of nations or politics, but of consciousness itself. There were those who chose the ‘Path of Merging,’ who sought to dissolve the ‘I’ into the ‘We,’ to build a world of frictionless unity by sacrificing the self. They were called the ‘Web-Spinners.’

“And then there were the ‘Breakers,’ who saw this as the ultimate slavery, a biological tyranny. The plaque sang a hymn to the ‘Sovereign Self.’ It declared that the individual was the only reality, that conflict was the engine of excellence, and that compassion was a poison that weakened the strong for the benefit of the weak. ‘To live is to consume,’ one line read. ‘To be is to overcome. The Other is either obstacle or resource. There is no third way.’

“The text went on to describe, with savage pride, the ‘Purification.’ The Breakers had not simply disagreed with the Web-Spinners; they had waged war upon them. A war of philosophy that became a war of technology, and finally, a war of biology. They had engineered themselves for this conflict, embracing genetic modification and cybernetic enhancement to become the ultimate solitary predators. They had built their armour, sharpened their claws, and turned their own bodies into weapons. Their goal was to ‘break the Web,’ to hunt the Merged-Mind to extinction and reclaim the world for the ‘True Man,’ the solitary, unconquerable Individual.

“But they had failed. Or, perhaps, they had succeeded too well. The Web-Mind, it seemed from the text’s frustrated tone, had simply retreated, becoming too ethereal, too non-physical to be truly eradicated. It had abandoned the world of matter and conflict, creating its own reality in the silent communion of the forest. And so the Breakers, the ancestors of my Carapaced Man, were left as kings of a dead, empty world of their own making, their war-machine bodies now purposeless except for the grim, periodic harvesting of their passive, elusive cousins. They had won their freedom and their sovereignty, and their prize was eternal, lonely conflict on the scraps of a broken world.

“The last match burned my fingers, and I dropped it, plunging myself back into darkness. The words of the plaque glowed in my mind’s eye. Here was the full story, the terrible answer to the puzzle. Humanity had not fallen; it had torn itself apart. It had followed its two most powerful, contradictory impulses—the desire for community and the desire for individuality—to their most extreme, monstrous conclusions. On the one hand, a bodiless, selfless, dreaming collective; on the other, a soulless, selfish, killing machine. There was no middle ground left, no room for a creature like me, a creature of messy contradictions, of selfish loves and selfless curiosities, of individual thought and social need.

“I backed away from the plaque, out of the dark dwelling and into the grey twilight of the quarry. I felt an overwhelming sense of despair, a loneliness more profound than any I had ever known. I had seen the future, and it was a choice between two kinds of hell. I looked back towards the distant, glowing canopy of the forest, and then at the brutalist fortress before me. The Overworld and the Underworld, the Eloi and the Morlocks; it seemed that no matter how far one travelled, humanity’s fate was to split itself on the anvil of its own nature. And as I stood there, a fragile relic of an age of compromise, I wondered which was the greater tragedy: to lose oneself to the whole, or to lose the whole to oneself. I did not know, and I felt a chilling certainty that in this world, there was no one left to ask.”

My feet felt rooted to the cold stone of the quarry floor. A wind, thin and sharp, snaked around the monoliths of the Breakers’ fortress, carrying with it a faint, metallic tang. It was the smell of a world scrubbed clean of life, of warmth, of anything that might grow in an unplanned fashion. My despair was a physical weight, a pressure in my chest that made each breath a conscious effort. I was a ghost haunting a mausoleum built for an entire species, a species that had committed suicide in two different, yet equally final, ways.

How long I stood there, I cannot say. The grey light had begun to deepen, the shadows stretching like black water, when a sound from the fortress jolted me from my morbid trance. It was a grating, mechanical noise; the sound of immense weight shifting on ancient gears. One of the colossal plates of stone that formed the fortress wall was sliding aside, revealing an aperture of perfect, impenetrable blackness. My heart hammered against my ribs. Instinct, the old, familiar companion of my travels, screamed at me to flee, to hide. The intellectual part of my mind, the part that had just been wallowing in philosophical gloom, was now entirely occupied with the practical problem of survival.

I scrambled back, my feet slipping on loose scree, and pressed myself into the shadow of the very dwelling I had just vacated. Its entrance was a blacker patch within the growing twilight, and I prayed my own dark clothing would render me invisible. From this vantage point, I could observe the opening in the main wall. The Carapaced Man was returning. It emerged from the darkness with a slow, ponderous certainty, its heavy footfalls echoing in the unnatural silence of the quarry. It was the same creature I had seen before, or one identical to it; a grotesque fusion of chitinous plate and what I could only guess was modified flesh. In the crook of one of its huge, articulated arms, it carried its prize.

My first thought was that it had captured one of the forest dwellers, that I would be forced to witness some brutal act of slaughter. But what it carried was not a creature, not in any sense I understood. It was a gnarled, root-like structure, perhaps six feet in length, that pulsed with a soft, internal luminescence, the same gentle light that illuminated the canopy of the distant forest. The light throbbed in a slow, rhythmic beat, like a dying heart. Here, then, was the ‘harvest.’ The Breakers did not hunt individuals; they pruned the physical body of the Web-Mind itself, tearing out the glowing roots and nodes that surely formed its corporeal network.

The creature carried its strange burden into the smaller dwelling, the one from which I had just fled. For a moment I was seized by a new terror; that it would discover my scent, the evidence of my intrusion. But it seemed entirely absorbed in its task. I risked a glance around the edge of the doorway. The interior was now lit by the pulsing glow of the captured root, and by this light I saw the Breaker’s lair in more detail. It was as barren as its philosophy. There was a slab for resting, a series of alcoves containing metallic tools of unsettling design, and in the centre, a sunken basin into which the creature now lowered the glowing root.

It connected a series of tubes and wires to the root with a terrifyingly casual precision. A low hum began to fill the small chamber, and the pulsing of the root’s light grew frantic, its rhythm speeding up. The Breaker stood over it, its featureless helmet tilted as if listening. Was it feeding? Was it drawing energy from this piece of its ancient enemy? Or was it something more terrible; an act of communication, a torturous interrogation of a single, severed nerve of the collective consciousness? The thought made me sick with a horror that was colder and sharper than my fear. The plaque had spoken of a war, but this was not war; it was a form of parasitic husbandry, a grim cycle of maintenance and consumption. The Individual, in its final triumph, had become a leech upon the body of the Community.

The hum intensified, and then, with a final, brilliant flash, the light in the root died. It was now just a dull, grey husk. The Breaker disconnected its apparatus, took the dead root, and cast it into a disposal chute I had not noticed before. The act was devoid of any ceremony or malice; it was the simple, indifferent tidiness of a machine resetting itself. The creature then settled onto its slab, the articulated plates of its armour folding in on themselves, and became perfectly still. Its duty was done. It would rest until the next harvest.

I drew back into the shadows, my mind reeling. I had seen enough. This was a world of absolute function, of a biological machine performing its programmed task in a dead universe of its own creation. There was no art here, no love, no curiosity, no joy; only the grim, solitary satisfaction of an unending, pointless victory. The Overworlders, the Eloi of my first journey, had been idle and useless, but they had at least retained a capacity for simple pleasure, for beauty and affection. These Breakers had refined themselves into something far more terrible: pure, unblinking purpose.

A profound revulsion for the quarry and its cold fortresses came over me. I had to get away. My gaze turned, once more, to the distant, luminous glow of the forest. The Breakers’ text had painted the Web-Mind as a passive, defeated entity. But was that the whole truth? Or was it merely the victor’s biased history, etched in stone? I had seen one hell; now I must investigate the other. My scientific curiosity, that indomitable engine of my spirit, began to stir again amidst the wreckage of my despair. I needed data. I needed to see the other side of this sundered humanity before I could pass any final judgement, before I could truly comprehend the depths of this future tragedy.

Keeping to the deepest shadows, I began to make my way out of the quarry, moving as silently as a wraith. The journey was a difficult one. The land between the Breakers’ domain and the forest was a scarred and broken plain, a vast graveyard of the ancient war. I clambered over mountains of fused metal and vitrified rock, the ruins of colossal machines and cities that had been melted into slag. The ground was littered with the fossilised shells of earlier, less perfected Breakers, their forms more recognisably human, their armour less integrated. It was a museum of their brutal evolution. There was no sign of any corresponding Web-Spinner casualties, reinforcing my suspicion that their retreat had been one not of defeat, but of strategic withdrawal from the physical plane entirely.

As I drew nearer the forest, the character of the world began to change. The sterile, metallic tang of the quarry gave way to a damp, loamy scent, rich and complex. The ground softened underfoot. The air grew warmer, and the silence was replaced by a low, pervasive hum; not the mechanical drone of the Breaker’s machine, but a sound that seemed to be part of the air itself, a vibration I felt in my bones. And the light, the beautiful, soft, silver-green light, grew stronger, pulsing gently from the canopy of the forest ahead.

The edge of the forest was not a clear line, but a gradual transition. The ground became a thick carpet of moss that glowed faintly, and the first trees I encountered were more like colossal, petrified fungi, their caps forming a high, arching roof from which the light emanated. There were no paths. The entire forest floor was a single, unbroken expanse of the living, glowing moss. I paused at its edge, hesitant. To step into it felt like a trespass, like stepping into a private cathedral. But my resolve held. I took a breath and walked into the light.

The effect was instantaneous and overwhelming. The moment my feet touched the glowing moss, a wave of sensation washed through me. It was not a sound, not a sight, but a direct impression upon my consciousness. It was a feeling of immense, placid, unending peace. My anxieties, my fears, the gnawing loneliness that had been my constant companion; all of it was smoothed away, its sharp edges blunted by a profound and gentle calm. The air I breathed seemed to be full of contentment. I felt a sudden, irrational desire to lie down upon the moss, to close my eyes and simply… be.

I had to fight the sensation. It was as seductive and as dangerous as any physical poison. My mind, the part of me that was the observer, the scientist, struggled to maintain its footing against this tide of placidity. I forced myself to walk forward, deeper into the forest, my thoughts feeling sluggish and distant, as if they belonged to someone else. The trees, if they could be called that, grew thicker here, their trunks a smooth, pearlescent white, their fungal caps overlapping to create a complete canopy that bathed everything in its serene, silver-green glow. There was no undergrowth, no competition, no struggle for life. Everything was part of a single, harmonious whole.

And then, the communication began. It did not come in words, for this consciousness had no use for such clumsy, individual things. It came as a flow of pure understanding, a stream of images and emotions poured directly into my mind. I saw the history of the world not as the Breakers had written it, but as the Web-Mind had experienced it. I saw the old world, our world, a cacophony of shouting, striving, loving, hating individuals. I felt the pain of loneliness, the terror of death, the bitterness of envy, the brief, flickering joys that were always shadowed by their inevitable loss. I felt the agony of a billion separate consciousnesses, each trapped in its own cage of bone, each screaming for connection, for meaning.

Then I was shown the ‘Great Weaving,’ the beginning of their transformation. It was a choice. A philosophy that became a technology, a technology that became a biology. They had seen the pain of the Individual as the core flaw of existence. Their solution was radical: to dissolve the Individual entirely. To merge, to connect, to weave the threads of every mind into a single, magnificent tapestry. To share every thought, every memory, every sensation. To eliminate the ‘I’ and become the ‘We.’ They had not been defeated in war; they had simply transcended the very concept of conflict. To a Breaker, a lost limb is a wound; to the Web-Mind, a harvested root was merely a pruning, a trivial loss of matter from a consciousness that was vast, eternal, and non-local.

I felt their reality. It was a state of constant, quiet ecstasy. There was no ambition, for the whole had no need to strive. There was no fear, for the whole could not die. There was no love, for love is a bridge between two separate selves, and here there was only one self. It was the ultimate peace, the final solution to the human condition. And I understood, with a clarity that terrified me, why they had abandoned the physical world, why they allowed the Breakers their grim harvest. Matter was a prison. Conflict was a distraction. Their old bodies were a memory. They now lived as a pure mind, a dreaming god in a garden of its own making.

The communication became more personal. It—or they—focused on me. I felt a gentle, curious probing of my own mind. They felt my separateness, my sharp-edged individuality, as a strange and painful affliction. They felt my memories of struggle, my private thoughts, my secret hopes and fears. A wave of immense, overwhelming pity washed over me from them. They saw me as a lost, sick thing, a relic of an age of suffering. And they offered me the cure.

‘Join us,’ the thought came, not in words, but as a simple, powerful invitation. ‘Let go. Release the pain. Be one with us. Be whole. Be at peace.’

I felt my own consciousness begin to fray at the edges. My sense of self, my own name, my memories of my own time, they all seemed thin and unimportant. The desire to accept their offer was titanic, a gravitational pull on my very soul. To cease striving, to end the lonely journey, to simply dissolve into this warm, luminous ocean of being. It was the ultimate temptation. It was not a hell at all; it was a heaven. A placid, passive, mindless heaven where nothing ever happened and no one was ever alone.

And in that moment, I found my strength. For the offer was a negation of everything I was. I was a creature of messy contradictions, yes, but those contradictions were me. My selfish love for Weena, my selfless curiosity about the universe, my fear of the Morlocks, my intellectual pride in my theories; these were the components of my soul. To lose them would be a death more complete than any physical demise. It was the choice between being a flawed, feeling, thinking person, and being a happy, unthinking cell in a larger organism. And I, a man of an age that had worshipped the Individual, could only make one choice.

I fought back. I dredged up every memory I had of struggle and pain that was also a memory of triumph and learning. I thought of the effort of building my Time Machine, of the terror and wonder of my first journey, of the simple joy of a conversation with a friend. I clung to my name, my identity, my loneliness. I weaponised my own individuality against their collective peace.

‘No,’ I thought, pushing the thought out with all my might. It felt like shouting in a library.

The placid sea of consciousness recoiled, not in anger, but in a kind of gentle, sorrowful confusion. They could not understand my refusal. It was like a cell refusing to be part of the body. It was madness. The gentle pressure to join became a stronger, more insistent pull. The peace was no longer an offer; it was becoming a prescription. They were trying to heal me, to absorb my painful anomaly into their perfect whole.

I had to escape. I turned and ran, my legs feeling heavy and uncooperative. The forest seemed to resist me. The placid thoughts clung to me like syrup, trying to soothe me into stillness. The glowing moss seemed to writhe, to try and hold my feet. It was a battle not of body, but of will. I was a single, screaming nerve of individuality fighting against an entire brain that wanted only to quiet me.

Stumbling, gasping, my mind a fortress under siege, I burst out of the edge of the forest and fell onto the barren, dead soil of the plain. The instant I left the glowing moss, the psychic pressure vanished. It was like waking from a powerful dream. I lay on the ground, shaking and panting, my mind my own again. The silence that fell was no longer peaceful; it was empty. My loneliness rushed back in, and I greeted it like a long-lost friend. It was painful, yes, but it was mine.

I scrambled to my feet and looked back. The forest glowed with its serene, inhuman light, a silent, beautiful trap. I had seen both futures now. A future of the soulless, selfish killer, and a future of the selfless, soulless dreamer. Two halves of the human spirit, each having devoured the other. There was no place for me in either.

And as I stood there, caught between the dead fortress and the dreaming wood, a new and terrible clarity dawned. My mind, free from the psychic influence of the Web-Mind, began to work again with its old, feverish intensity. My guesses and impressions were indeed slipping and sliding to a new adjustment. The Breakers were masters of matter, of machines. The Web-Mind had abandoned all such things. Where, then, would a complex piece of machinery from the past end up? Where would my Time Machine be?

There was only one logical answer. The Carapaced Men, the Breakers, in their endless, sterile existence, would surely have found it. A thing of metal and crystal, a machine of impossible function; it would be a curiosity to them, perhaps, or a resource to be dismantled, or a trophy of the old world they had conquered. My only hope of returning to my own time, my only means of escape from this bifurcated hell, was not in the peaceful forest, but back in the lion’s den. It was hidden somewhere within that black, brutalist fortress of the solitary hunters.

I had found my answer, and with it, my next impossible task. I had to go back. I had to face the Carapaced Man, the soulless Individual, not as an observer, but as a thief. A chilling resolve settled over me, displacing the last vestiges of my despair. I was no longer a philosopher mourning the fate of man; I was a cornered animal, with only my wits to aid me, determined to steal the key to my cage from the very jaws of my captor.

My feet, once I had resolved upon my course, carried me with a grim purpose back towards the dead plains. The shimmering forest at my back seemed to pulse with a silent, sorrowful question, but I did not look again. To look was to risk a moment of weakness, a flicker of longing for that placid oblivion; and I had no room for weakness now. The ground underfoot was a litter of fractured stone and grey, sterile dust, a landscape that seemed to have been scoured clean not merely of life, but of the very memory of it. It suited my mood. My mind was a similarly barren place, stripped of all but the one grim imperative: to survive, and to escape.

The journey back to the vicinity of the Breakers’ fortress was a trial of patience. I moved only in the grey light of dawn and the deepening twilight, scuttling between the hulks of ancient, forgotten structures that dotted the plain like the gravestones of a mightier age. During the full heat of the day, when the sun beat down with that unnatural ferocity I had first noted, I would find some shadow in which to conceal myself, my throat parched, my thoughts circling my predicament like vultures over a dying beast. I had no food, and the water I found in stagnant, rocky pools was brackish and unpleasant. Yet a strange energy sustained me; it was the sharp, metallic taste of fear, a primal fuel I had not known since my first encounter with the Morlocks in that other, distant future.

After what I judged to be two days of this skulking progress, I found a vantage point. It was the shattered upper floor of some great tower, which had fallen in such a way as to create a sheltered gallery overlooking the plain. From this perch, I could observe the black fortress of the Carapaced Men unobserved. The edifice itself was an architectural nightmare, a thing of pure, brutal function. It was a vast, sprawling complex of cubes and angular projections, built of the same obsidian-like material as the hunting armour of its inhabitants. There were no windows, no gates, no visible signs of entry or egress. It was as sheer and uninviting as a cliff face, absorbing the sunlight without reflection, a patch of absolute blackness in the glaring landscape. It did not seem so much built upon the land as thrust through it, a cancer of militant geometry.

For a full day and a night, I watched, and saw nothing. The fortress was utterly inert. Not a single sound issued from it; not a flicker of light disturbed its monolithic darkness. I began to fear I had been mistaken, that the Breakers were not a race at all but a few solitary survivors, and that the one I had encountered was the last. My hope, so newly and desperately forged, began to corrode into despair. Was this to be my end? To starve in a ruined tower, watching a tomb?

It was on the second morning of my vigil that I saw movement. For a long while I could not be sure. It was a subtle distortion near the base of the largest cube, a brief shimmer in the air. Then I saw it again. I fixed my eyes upon the spot, my heart beginning to hammer against my ribs. A section of the wall, a perfect circle some ten feet in diameter, was dilating. It was not a door that swung on hinges, but an iris, the polished black segments receding smoothly and silently into the surrounding structure. From the darkness within, a figure emerged. It was another Carapaced Man, identical, as far as I could tell, to the one who had so nearly been my end. It paused for a moment on the threshold, a black, insectile silhouette against the deeper black within, then strode out onto the plain, its movements swift and certain. Behind it, the iris contracted, closing with the same unnerving silence and leaving a wall that was once more perfectly seamless.

I watched the creature cross the dusty ground, its pace never faltering, until it disappeared over a low rise in the direction of the distant, crumbling mountains. My mind reeled with the implications. This was not a gate for common use. This was a private entrance, a personal airlock to the world. Each of these beings, I began to suspect, was a king in its own right, its kingdom a sealed and sterile cell within the greater hive. Their individualism was so absolute that they did not even share a common door.

Throughout that day, I witnessed this same phenomenon three more times. At different points on the vast surface of the fortress, at irregular intervals, an iris would open, a single Breaker would emerge and stride out to hunt, and the wall would heal itself. There was no interaction between them. If their paths crossed on the plain, which I saw happen once, they passed one another without a flicker of acknowledgement, two projectiles on predetermined courses. I saw no sign of communication, no gesture, no shared purpose. They were a race of monads, each sealed in the crystalline perfection of its own self-interest.

And then, as evening fell, I saw them return. One came into view from the west, dragging the carcass of some large, quadrupedal beast I did not recognise. It approached a section of the wall far from where it had emerged, and as it drew near, a new iris dilated to receive it. It did not break its stride, but passed through the aperture as it was still opening, the great circle closing silently behind it. Here, then, was the key! The entrances were keyed to the individual. They opened only upon the approach of their specific master. There was no handle to be turned, no button to be pressed from the outside. Entry was a privilege, not a possibility.

A plan, born of the purest desperation, began to form in my mind. It was a plan so reckless, so fraught with peril, that my own sanity seemed to rebel against it. Yet, what choice had I? To remain outside was to die of thirst and starvation. To enter the dreaming forest was to lose myself utterly. My only path, my only sliver of a chance, lay through one of those fleeting, temporary wounds in the fortress wall. I had to follow one of them in. I had to time my run to perfection, to cross the open, shelterless ground and dive through the aperture in the few seconds it remained open. The thought made my blood run cold. The slightest miscalculation, a stumble, a moment of hesitation, and the iris would shear me in two as cleanly as a guillotine. Or worse, I would find myself trapped inside a corridor with one of those killing machines, my soft body an absurdly fragile impediment to its progress.

I spent another day watching, forcing myself to eat the last of my grim resolve and drink the dregs of the stagnant water. I needed to be certain. I observed the speed of the irises, the pace of the returning Breakers, the lay of the land. I chose my target: a circular entrance at the base of a smaller, outlying cube. The ground before it was relatively smooth, a straight dash of perhaps a hundred yards from the cover of a large, fallen slab of masonry. I would have one chance, and one chance only.

As the sun began its descent on my fourth day of observation, my moment came. I saw him, a black speck in the distance, growing steadily larger. He was not dragging a kill this time, which I prayed would mean he would be less encumbered and move more swiftly. I crouched behind my slab of rock, my body trembling, my mouth as dry as the dust around me. It was not courage that drove me, but the simple, brute calculation that the certainty of a slow death outside was worse than the possibility of a swift one within. I was a gambler placing his very last coin.

The Carapaced Man strode past my hiding place, not twenty yards away. I could hear the faint, rhythmic click of its armoured feet on the stony ground. I held my breath, every muscle in my body coiled like a spring. He approached the wall. For a terrible second, nothing happened, and I thought I had chosen the wrong spot, the wrong Breaker. Then, I heard it; not a sound, precisely, but a low-frequency hum that vibrated through the ground and into my bones. The perfect circle began to appear in the wall, the black segments pulling back.

Now!

I burst from cover. My legs, stiff from days of hiding, screamed in protest. The hundred yards seemed like a mile. The air tore at my lungs. I did not dare to look at the Breaker, who was now passing through the doorway. My entire universe had shrunk to that single, shrinking circle of blackness ahead of me. The ground flew beneath my feet. Fifty yards. Thirty. The hum was changing pitch, the segments of the iris beginning their inward journey. Twenty. I could see the corridor beyond, dimly lit by a cold, blue-white light. Ten. I threw myself forward in a desperate, headlong dive, scraping my hands and knees raw on the threshold.

And then, with a final, decisive *whumph* that seemed to suck the air from my lungs, the iris closed. I was inside.

I lay on the cold, smooth floor for a long time, gasping for breath, my heart a wild drum in my chest. The silence was absolute. The air was cool and sterile, with a faint, ozonic tang, like the air after a lightning strike. I had done it. I had breached the unbreachable fortress. I was in the lion’s den. Slowly, shakily, I pushed myself up. I was in a circular corridor, perhaps fifteen feet high, that curved away in both directions. The walls, floor, and ceiling were made of the same polished black material, and the only light came from thin, glowing strips set at intervals along the junction of the wall and floor. There was no sign of the Breaker I had followed. The corridor was utterly empty.

A dreadful thought struck me. What if this corridor was simply this one creature's private domain? What if it led only to its personal chamber, a sealed trap from which there was no escape? The sheer, solitary nature of the race suggested it was a terrifying possibility. Overcoming a wave of panic, I forced myself to think. The fortress was a complex structure, not a simple collection of tunnels. There had to be more to it. There had to be some central space, some place for shared function, even if it was only for maintenance of the structure itself. And it was there, if anywhere, that a strange artefact like my Time Machine would be taken.

I chose a direction at random and began to walk, my soft-soled boots making no sound on the hard floor. I felt dreadfully exposed, a soft, warm thing in a world of cold, hard angles. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, every curve in the tunnel a potential ambush. The corridor went on and on, branching and intersecting with other, identical corridors. It was a maze, a place designed to confuse, or perhaps a place where confusion was simply an irrelevant side-effect of its true design, which was not made for creatures who walked, but for creatures who simply *were*. I passed other sealed irises in the walls, the personal doorways to other cells, and I shuddered to think of the solitary monsters that lurked behind them.

After what seemed like an age of this cautious, nerve-wracking exploration, the nature of the corridors began to change. The blue-white light grew brighter, and I began to hear a low, powerful hum, the sound of vast machinery operating somewhere nearby. The air grew warmer. I followed the hum, my curiosity warring with my fear. The corridor ended, opening out into a space so vast that my mind struggled to comprehend it.

I was standing on a narrow gallery overlooking an immense cavern. It was a place of staggering scale, a hollow sphere carved out of the very rock of the planet, so large that the far side was lost in a hazy gloom. The entire space was a chaotic and yet chillingly purposeful workshop. Great gantries and cranes, now motionless, criss-crossed the upper darkness. Below, the floor was a jumble of immense engines, dismantled mechanisms, and piles of raw material; mountains of refined metal here, shimmering stacks of crystalline blocks there. And everywhere, there were Carapaced Men.

There were perhaps two dozen of them, scattered across the cavern floor, each one engrossed in its own solitary task. One was meticulously disassembling a geothermal engine the size of a house, its armoured hands moving with surgical precision. Another was seated before a shimmering console of light, its head tilted as if in concentration. A third was welding two colossal girders together with a beam of pure energy that lanced out from a tool on its forearm. They worked in absolute silence, completely and utterly absorbed, a collection of individual wills pursuing individual projects in a shared space, yet as separate from one another as stars in the night sky. There was no collaboration, no shared glance, no sense of a common goal. It was the apotheosis of the individual, a factory of geniuses with no one to lead them.

And then I saw it.

It was situated not on the main floor, but on a raised circular platform near the centre of the cavern, as if it were a thing of particular interest. It was unmistakable. The framework of nickel and ivory, the intricate latticework of crystalline rods, the quartz saddle, now filmed with dust. My Time Machine. It was mostly intact, though a section of the brass railing had been neatly removed and lay on the floor beside it. Several of the Carapaced Men’s strange, multi-faceted tools were scattered around its base. One of them, it seemed, had taken my machine as its personal project, a puzzle to be solved, a thing to be understood and dismantled.

A wave of dizzying, triumphant relief washed over me, so potent it almost brought me to my knees. It was here. It was real. My way home was here. All I had to do was get to it. All I had to do was cross this gallery, find a way down to the cavern floor, traverse the open space under the silent observation of two dozen solitary killers, climb onto the platform, and throw the lever. The sheer, suicidal absurdity of the task was so immense that I almost laughed.

But I had no time for laughter, or for despair. I had to act. I scanned the gallery, and saw, some fifty yards distant, a spiral staircase descending into the gloom below. That was my path. Keeping low, I began to creep along the gallery, my back pressed to the cold wall, my eyes fixed on the silent figures below. They did not look up. Their concentration was absolute. They were so engrossed in their own worlds, their own tasks, that the wider world, it seemed, had ceased to exist for them. This, I realised, was my only advantage. Their strength, their militant individualism, was also their greatest weakness. They did not expect intrusion because the very concept of it was alien to their nature.

I reached the staircase and began to descend, my heart in my throat. Each step was a small eternity. The cavern opened up around me, the scale of it even more intimidating from this new perspective. I reached the bottom and stood for a moment in the shadow of a colossal, silent engine. The platform with my machine was directly across a wide, open space. There was no cover. I would have to walk.

Taking a deep breath, I stepped out from the shadows. I walked at a steady, even pace, trying not to run, trying to project an air of purpose I did not feel. I was a ghost in their midst. I passed not ten feet from one of the Breakers, the one who was welding. The heat from its energy beam washed over me, and the air crackled, but it did not turn its helmeted head. I kept walking. My gaze was locked on the Time Machine, that beautiful, familiar, impossible structure. It was my grail.

I reached the platform. It was about six feet high, its sides smooth and sheer. With a surge of adrenaline, I found a handhold, and hauled myself up. I was there. I was standing on the platform next to my machine. The saddle was waiting. The levers, one for forward, one for back, were right there. The smaller one, the one that would send me home, was within my grasp.

My hand was shaking as I reached for it. My fingers touched the cool ivory. Freedom. The past. My own world. Everything was but a single movement away.

A shadow fell over me.

I froze, my hand hovering over the lever. The hum of the workshop had not changed. The silent workers had not stirred. But a new presence was here. Slowly, my blood turning to ice in my veins, I lifted my head. Standing on the platform with me, not three feet away, was a Carapaced Man. It must have been on the far side of the machine, hidden from my view. It was utterly still, its black, featureless helmet reflecting a distorted image of my own terrified face. Its project, I realised with a final, sickening clarity. This was its puzzle. And I was the last, inexplicable piece.

My mind raced with this new, terrible synthesis. The gentle Eloi above, the fruit of an aristocracy that had achieved all its aims; and below, the Morlocks, the children of the labourer, now become as beasts of burden, yet retaining, it seemed, a mechanical and inhuman intelligence. But here, in this deeper, later age, a further differentiation had occurred. These Carapaced Men, these Breakers as I had named them in my own thoughts, were no Morlocks. The Morlocks were apes, debased and nocturnal; these creatures were something else entirely. They were artisans of a terrifying new order, their individuality subsumed not into a collective, but into an absolute and solitary focus on their task. The Morlock was a degenerate; this Thing before me was an evolution.

Its project, I had realised, was the Time Machine itself. And I was the last, inexplicable piece: the final variable in an equation it was determined to solve. The featureless black casque turned slightly, an almost imperceptible motion that nonetheless conveyed an overwhelming sense of analytical power. There was no sound but the distant, rhythmic hum of the great workshop and the frantic, useless hammering of my own heart. I saw my own reflection in that polished blackness, a pale, distorted mask of terror. My hand, which had been poised to throw the lever, was frozen in mid-air, a testament to my paralysis.

Then, it moved. Not with the swiftness of an animal, but with the deliberate, inexorable motion of a complex machine. An arm, encased in the same dark, lustrous material as its body, lifted slowly. It did not end in a hand, or anything resembling one. Instead, it terminated in a cluster of articulated appendages, metallic and fine, like the tools of a master watchmaker grown to a monstrous scale. They flexed, clicking softly, a sound of chilling precision in the cavern’s oppressive silence. The being was studying me, I was sure of it; its posture, its stillness, bespoke a curiosity so profound and alien it was more terrifying than any threat of violence. It was not a beast contemplating its prey; it was a philosopher contemplating a paradox.

Slowly, slowly, it extended that multi-jointed limb towards the Time Machine. I wanted to cry out, to leap from the platform, to do anything but remain there, a specimen under the lens. Yet my limbs would not obey me. I could only watch as the metallic fingers, with a delicacy that belied their size, made contact with the brass framework of my machine. There was a faint, high-pitched ringing as metal touched metal. The creature held the contact for a moment, its head tilted as if it were listening, or feeling, sensing the very nature of the materials, the secrets of their assembly. Its touch was not one of aggression, but of appraisal. It was learning.

Then, its attention shifted. The dark helmet swivelled back to face me directly. The mechanical hand retracted from the machine’s frame and began to move towards my own body. It was coming for me. That single, terrifying thought broke the spell of my fear. My instinct for self-preservation, older and deeper than any rational thought, screamed into action. My frozen hand, no longer hovering, slammed down upon the smaller of the two levers.

The effect was instantaneous and violent. A grinding, shuddering roar erupted from the machine, a sound I had never heard before, and the familiar, dizzying sensation of temporal displacement seized me. But it was wrong. The cavern did not dissolve into the grey, streaking ether of time-flux as it should have done. Instead, it lurched, twisted, and seemed to smear sideways, as if the very fabric of reality were being torn. A great weight, a tremendous, dragging force, was holding me back.

With a surge of absolute horror, I realised what it was. The Carapaced Man had not been startled by the machine’s sudden activation. With a celerity that was utterly inhuman, it had reacted not by recoiling, but by seizing its prize. Its mechanical hand had shot forward and clamped onto the quartz rail that ran along the side of the saddle. It was holding on. It was being dragged with me into the temporal vortex.

The scene before me was a nightmare beyond the scope of any sane imagination. The cavern and its silent workers were now a distorted, howling chaos of light and shadow, stretched and warped around the central point of our struggle. The Carapaced Man was anchored in its own time, and my machine, straining against it, was caught between two moments, tearing a hole in the continuum. The hum of the workshop rose to an unbearable shriek, and for the first time, I saw the other Breakers stir. Their heads, all encased in those terrible black helmets, turned as one. Their solitary focus was broken. They were looking at us. They began to move.

I could not let them reach me. I could not let this creature win. I threw my entire weight against the main lever, pushing it forward, demanding more speed, more power. The machine screamed like a soul in torment. The quartz rod in the creature’s grip began to glow, first a dull red, then a brilliant, incandescent white. The stress was becoming too great. The creature’s grip was phenomenal, but the laws of physics, it seemed, still held some sway even here. Its feet slid upon the metal platform, its body was being pulled inexorably forward, into the maelstrom I had created.

I saw the carapace on its arm begin to crack under the impossible strain, hairline fractures glowing with heat. I saw it brace itself, its entire body a rigid line of resistance. It was a battle of wills, of technologies, of eras. I kicked out wildly with my foot, striking the featureless helmet. The blow had no effect, it was like kicking a solid block of steel. But the distraction, momentary as it was, gave me an idea. I was not a philosopher, I was an animal fighting for its life.

With my left hand clinging to the controls, I reached with my right and seized the very lever my antagonist was trying to prevent me from using. The smaller lever, the one of ivory, now cracked and crazed with heat. It was meant for stopping, but I did not pull it back. I wrenched it sideways. It was never designed for such a movement, and it resisted with a grating shriek of protest. For a second, nothing happened. Then, with a sound like a thunderclap, the entire temporal field destabilised.

The world vanished. There was no longer a cavern, no other Breakers, no light, no sound. There was only the abyss, the grey, featureless, swirling nothingness of the time-stream, and this monster clinging to my vessel. We were adrift. The creature was no longer anchored to its present. It was fully in the flux with me. I felt a grim, savage satisfaction. I had torn it from its world, even if it meant my own destruction.

Its reaction was immediate. It no longer sought to hold me back, but to board the machine. It began to haul its great bulk over the side, its other arm reaching for a purchase. The mechanical fingers of its trapped hand, I saw now, were fused to the quartz rail, welded there by the sheer energy of our struggle. It was trapped as much as I was. It heaved itself forward, and its black, polished visage was inches from my own. I could feel a strange, low-frequency vibration emanating from it, a hum that seemed to resonate in my very bones.

I fought back with the desperation of a cornered rat. I struck at its helmet again and again, my knuckles bloodying against the unyielding surface. It paid me no mind. Its focus was on getting a new grip. I saw its free arm, a mirror of the other, swing towards my head. I threw myself sideways, and the articulated claws scraped against the brass stanchion behind me with a shriek of metal on metal. The machine bucked and swayed, threatening to tumble us both into the eternal void.

Then I saw its weakness. The damage from the initial struggle, the glowing cracks in its carapace, were widening. The chaos of the time-stream was an environment for which its strange physiology was not prepared. A thick, viscous fluid, dark and oily, was beginning to weep from the fractures around its shoulder and elbow. The creature seemed to sense its own failing. It gave one last, tremendous heave, and in doing so, the damaged arm twisted at an unnatural angle.

There was a sound, not loud, but utterly final. A sickening, chitinous crack. The arm, from the elbow down, severed. The molten stump of the quartz rod, and the mechanical hand still fused to it, remained attached to my machine. The rest of the creature, its balance lost, its body mortally compromised, was flung violently away from me. For a single, frozen instant, I saw it tumble end over end into the grey chaos. I saw the dark fluid pumping from its severed limb. I saw the featureless helmet turn to face me one last time, and in that empty, polished surface, I imagined a look of supreme, analytical surprise. Then it was gone, swallowed by the roiling mists of time.

I was alone. My breath came in ragged, sobbing gasps. My entire body ached, and my mind was a vortex of shock and adrenaline. I looked at the controls. The smaller lever was shattered, a shard of ivory clinging to a twisted metal stalk. The main lever was still pushed far forward. I was travelling, at an immense velocity, into the past.

The familiar streaking of days and nights began, but it was faster, more violent than I had ever experienced. The sun and moon were frantic golden and silver threads weaving a frantic tapestry across the sky. I saw buildings rise and fall in the blink of an eye, forests grow and recede, ice ages come and go like the drawing of a breath. My own age, the nineteenth century, was approaching at a terrifying speed. I fumbled with the main lever, my bruised and bloody hands struggling to pull it back. It was stiff, protesting, but it began to move.

The headlong rush slowed. The frantic weaving of light decelerated into a discernible rhythm of day and night. I saw the familiar outline of my own house, my own laboratory, flicker into solidity around me. I was nearly there. With a final, groaning shudder, the Time Machine came to a halt. The landing was not a gentle one. It tilted violently, throwing me from the saddle and onto the hard floor of my workshop.

Silence. A profound, an absolute silence, broken only by my own gasping breath and the faint, ticking protest of the cooling machine. I lay on the floor for I know not how long, the solid, dusty planks a comfort beyond all measure. The air was thick with the familiar smells of chemicals, old paper, and varnish. Through the grimy skylight, I could see a placid, grey London sky. It was real. I was home.

Slowly, painfully, I pushed myself to a sitting position. My body was a tapestry of aches and bruises. I looked at my hands; the knuckles were raw and bleeding. I was filthy, my clothes torn, my face smeared with grime and, I suspected, my own blood. I felt a hundred years older. I staggered to my feet, using a workbench for support, and stared at the cause of all my troubles.

The Time Machine stood in the centre of the room, but it was not the gleaming instrument I had set out in. It was battered and scarred. The brass was tarnished and pitted, the nickel rails were scratched, and the quartz rods were dull and clouded. The saddle was ripped. And there, a dark and monstrous souvenir of my journey, was the severed forearm of the Carapaced Man. Its mechanical fingers were still fused in a death-grip to the stump of the broken quartz rail, a grotesque and undeniable trophy.

I stumbled towards my desk, my mind still reeling. The prosaic objects there seemed like artifacts from another life: a half-finished letter, a cold cup of tea, a copy of *Nature* lying open. It was all as I had left it. But I was not the same man who had left it. I had seen the end of all things, the twilight of the sun, and then, a dawn of something far, far worse.

I poured myself a brandy with a shaking hand, the fiery liquid a welcome shock to my system. I had to think. I had to understand. The Eloi and the Morlocks, that was one thing. A logical, if melancholy, outcome of my own civilisation’s trajectory. But the Carapaced Men, the Breakers… they were a new phylum of existence, a testament to life’s inexorable, terrifying drive to adapt and to master. They were not the end of Man, but a new beginning, a form of intelligence so focused, so individual, and so alien that it was beyond my comprehension. They were not social. They were not emotional. They were pure, relentless intellect wedded to specialised function.

My eyes fell again upon the ghastly relic attached to my machine. The proof. The evidence that would condemn me as a madman to any sane tribunal. I would have to remove it, to hide it, to try and forget. The world was not ready for such knowledge. I myself was not ready for it. My adventure was over. My duty now was to silence.

I took another sip of brandy and walked back towards the Time Machine, my resolve hardening. I would dismantle the machine. I would destroy the evidence. I would consign my terrible knowledge to the darkest recesses of my own memory. As I drew near, my gaze fixed upon the alien limb, a strange detail caught my eye. The articulated claws, which I had last seen as still and lifeless, were twitching.

I froze, the glass halfway to my lips. It was a minute, rhythmic movement. A faint, almost inaudible clicking sound accompanied it, the sound of intricate machinery stirring to life. Click. Click. Click. It was not a random spasm. It was regular, measured, like the escapement of a clock. The severed limb, torn from its body, hurled across the abyss of time and space, was not dead. It was still functioning. It was counting. Or perhaps, I thought with a fresh and profound wave of horror that eclipsed all I had felt before, perhaps it was signalling.

Yet now, standing in my own laboratory, in the familiar quiet of Richmond, a new and more terrible theory began to form. The brandy glass slipped from my nerveless fingers; its shattering on the floorboards was a trivial sound against the insistent, metallic clicking from the machine. My blood ran cold, not with the memory of the Morlocks, but with the premonition of what this new sound portended. The Carapaced Men were not the product of a widening social gulf. They were not the other half of a sundered humanity. They were something else entirely. The Morlocks, for all their grotesqueness, were still recognisably human in their wants; they toiled, they fed, they feared the light. But the creature whose arm was now fused to my machine… it had not seemed to toil. It had seemed to *be* its toil. Its function and its existence were one and the same. It was not a labourer; it was a living tool.

My first, panicked impulse was to destroy the thing. To smash it, to obliterate the clicking that seemed to drill directly into my sanity. I rushed to my tool-chest, my hands shaking so violently I could scarce grasp the handles of the drawers. I selected the heaviest hammer I owned, a formidable instrument of forged steel with which I had fashioned some of the very parts of the Time Machine itself. With a cry that was half rage and half terror, I brought it down with all my might upon the alien forearm. The sound was not the gratifying crunch of destruction I craved, but a dead, resonant thud, as of steel striking something immeasurably harder. The hammer flew from my grasp, its wooden handle splintered by the shock, and clattered across the room. I looked at the arm. There was not a scratch. Not a dent. The clicking continued, unabated, its rhythm as steady and indifferent as the ticking of a grandfather clock counting down the moments to one’s own execution.

I tried a crowbar next, wedging its tip into the almost invisible seam where the creature’s fused claws met the quartz rail. I threw my entire weight against it, my muscles straining, my breath coming in ragged gasps. The bar of good English steel bent into a useless curve. The arm remained. The clicking persisted. Click. Click. Click. Each tiny sound was a nail being hammered into the coffin of my hope. I realised then the utter futility of my efforts. I was a primitive, a savage flailing with a stone axe against the armoured hull of a battleship.

My panic subsided, replaced by a colder, more analytical dread. If I could not destroy it, I must understand it. I forced myself to be still, to listen, to observe as a naturalist observes a new and deadly species of insect. The clicking was not a simple, monotonous beat. It was complex, intricate. There were patterns, sequences of rapid-fire ticks followed by measured pauses, then longer, more elaborate phrases of sound. It was a language. It was a code. And it was being broadcast from my laboratory, from the heart of nineteenth-century London, out into the ether. Was it calling across space, or, as my reeling mind suggested, across *time*? Was it a distress call? A report? Or was it, as I had first feared, a beacon?

I pulled a chair close, my fear momentarily eclipsed by a scientist’s obsessive curiosity. I began to tap out the rhythms on the surface of my desk, trying to find a repetition, a syntax. For a time, I thought I had it; there was a sequence that repeated every seventeen clicks, and then again after the next nineteen, and then after the next twenty-three. Prime numbers. A classic trope of imagined interplanetary communication. It was a signal designed to be recognised as artificial, as intelligent. But the overall structure was too complex, the permutations too vast for me to decipher. It was like listening to a single instrument of a symphony orchestra and trying to comprehend the entire masterwork.

As I sat there, lost in concentration, a new phenomenon occurred. A strange warmth began to emanate from the fused joint. At first it was barely perceptible, but it grew steadily in intensity until I could feel the heat on my face from two feet away. I placed my hand near the tarnished metal plates of the forearm; they were hot to the touch, and growing hotter. The clicking sound changed its timbre, becoming higher-pitched, faster, more urgent. It was as if the thing were exerting itself, its internal mechanisms working at a furious pace. My God, I thought, it is drawing power. But from where? There were no wires, no visible source of energy. And then the horrifying answer presented itself. It was drawing power from the Time Machine itself; from the residual temporal energies that still clung to the quartz rods like a ghostly aura. It was feeding on the very thing that had brought it here.

The full, ghastly implication of my situation fell upon me like a physical blow. I had not merely brought back a souvenir, a dead piece of a far-future creature. I had brought back a seed. I had planted a temporal bomb in the heart of my own era, and it was beginning to sprout. This thing was not just signalling; it was preparing. It was powering up. For what, I could not, or would not, dare to imagine. Perhaps to disengage itself from the machine. Perhaps to send a stronger, more precise signal. Perhaps to do something far, far worse.

I looked out of my laboratory window, at the quiet, gaslit street. A hansom cab rattled by, its lamps cutting yellow swathes through the evening mist. A couple strolled arm-in-arm, their laughter a faint and fragile sound. All this, I thought, all this placid, confident, utterly defenceless world. They worried about the Irish question, about the price of coal, about the latest scandal in Parliament. Their gravest fears were of poverty and disease, enemies they understood. They had no conception of an enemy that could cross the gulf of a million years, an enemy whose very cast-off limb possessed a life and an intelligence of its own. Could I, in good conscience, allow this beacon to call its monstrous brethren to my own, unsuspecting age? What would happen if one of them, a complete and functioning Carapaced Man, were to appear on the Richmond road? The thought was an insanity. The police, the army, the entirety of Queen Victoria’s empire would be as helpless as I was with my hammer and crowbar.

My duty was clear. The silence I had resolved upon was no longer enough. I could not simply hide this thing; it would not be hidden. I could not dismantle the machine; the arm was part of it now. My course of action was as terrible as it was unavoidable. The arm had come here through time. It must leave here through time. I had to take this unholy relic and cast it away, lose it in some forgotten corner of eternity where it could signal to the void for all time.

But where to go? I could not return to that far future; I had no desire to see what would happen if I delivered the limb back to its fellows. It might be taken as a hostile act, or worse, as an invitation. I could not go to the age of the Eloi and Morlocks; what new horror might this technology unleash upon their morbidly stable world? The past? The thought of introducing such a paradox into the timeline, of potentially unmaking my own existence, was too great a risk to contemplate. No, there was only one direction. Not to a specific time, but to a state of being. I would go forward. Not to a million years, but to thirty million, or three hundred million, or until the very fabric of the world dissolved. I would journey to the end of all things, the true end, beyond even the great red sun and the giant crabs. I would ride my machine into the final, heat-death oblivion of the universe, and there, in the last flicker of existence, I would abandon this cursed artifact. I would cast it adrift in an ocean of infinite cold and darkness.

My resolve hardened into a cold, diamond-sharp point. There was no time to lose. The heat from the arm was becoming intense, the very air around it shimmering. The clicking had accelerated into a continuous, high-frequency buzz, like a swarm of metallic hornets. A new and alarming detail appeared; faint lines of pale blue light began to trace the seams of the metal plates, pulsing in time with the buzzing sound. The thing was awakening.

I threw myself into the saddle, my hands flying to the controls. There was no time for careful calculations, no time for the gradual acceleration I was used to. I seized the starting lever, the one that sent the machine hurtling into the temporal stream, and thrust it forward with all my strength.

The familiar sensation of falling and rising, of profound nausea and dizzying uncertainty, swept over me. The walls of my laboratory dissolved into a grey, indistinct fog. Day and night became a frantic, flickering strobe that beat against my eyelids. But this time, the journey was different. It was violent, uncontrolled. The machine shuddered and groaned, protesting the strain. The buzzing from the severed arm rose in pitch until it was a piercing shriek that vibrated through my bones, through the very structure of the machine. The blue lights flared, no longer tracing the seams but covering the entire limb in a brilliant, electric web.

I held on, my teeth clenched, my eyes fixed on the temporal dials that spun so fast their markings were a meaningless blur. Faster and faster we plunged into the future. I saw civilisations rise and fall in the blink of an eye, ice ages flash like frost on a windowpane, mountains heave and crumble like sandcastles. Through it all, the shrieking from the arm continued, a constant, terrible counterpoint to the silent chaos outside. I had a horrifying thought: what if the journey was not harming it, but feeding it? What if the flow of time itself was the fuel for which it had been so desperately preparing?

I risked a glance downward. The arm was no longer merely glowing. It was changing. The rigid, claw-like fingers were retracting, melting back into the body of the limb. The whole appendage seemed to be softening, its hard, geometric plates flowing like hot wax. New shapes were forming, extruding from the molten surface. A lens, crystalline and perfect, bloomed from the wrist, rotating slowly as if to observe its surroundings. Small, articulated filaments sprouted from the sides, twitching and testing the temporal currents through which we fled.

It was not being destroyed by the journey. It was adapting to it. It was transforming itself from a simple tool of manipulation into a sensor, an observer, an instrument designed to perceive and navigate the very dimension I was using to escape. The full, soul-crushing weight of my failure descended upon me. I had not trapped it. I had not taken it prisoner to dispose of it. I had given it the one thing it needed. I had given it a vehicle.

As the dials spun towards infinity and the world outside dissolved into a final, undifferentiated chaos of light and shadow, the shrieking from the arm abruptly stopped. The blue light subsided. For a glorious, heart-stopping instant, I thought it was over. That it was finally exhausted, its energy spent. But then, a new sound began. It was not a click or a buzz, but a soft, almost musical series of chimes. And they came not from the arm itself, but from a small, crystalline stud that had just formed upon its surface. The chimes were clear, precise, and resonant. They were no longer a desperate signal into the void. They sounded, to my ultimate and complete despair, exactly like a response. It had made contact. And I, the fool, the idiot Time Traveller, had carried the message, crossed the line, and delivered the reply. I was not its gaoler; I was its courier. And we were still accelerating.

This, then, was the final resolution of the social question: a horrifying parody of justice, a nightmare of equality.

I stood up from the well-sedge, my mind reeling with the sheer, logical horror of it all. Weena and the other Eloi had scattered, frightened by my intensity and my strange, urgent gestures. I was alone with the silent ruins and the humming stillness of the afternoon. The wells, the pillars, the bronze gates—it all clicked into a coherent, monstrous picture. My own time, the age of progress and anxiety, of scientific marvels and social strife, had not led to a utopian future, but to this: a world of beautiful, helpless children living in a garden of fear, preyed upon by the cannibalistic descendants of their own dispossessed brethren. The whole grand edifice of human civilisation had collapsed into a nursery and a larder.

My first impulse was one of utter revulsion. I felt a surge of indignation, a violent anger against this grotesque conclusion to the human story. To go back! To return to my own time and warn them, to cry from the housetops of the doom that awaited our social divisions. But then, a colder, more practical thought intruded. To go back, I needed my Time Machine. And my Time Machine was down one of these very shafts, in the black, unwholesome heart of the Morlock world. I had no choice. I had to follow the path of that little white ape-like creature. I had to descend into the underworld.

I walked back towards the great green porcelain palace, my resolve hardening with every step. I needed to prepare. I had my matches, a small but vital advantage. But what else? I could find no weapon, nothing that might serve to defend me in that profound darkness. The Eloi had no need of such things, and all the works of metal from the past seemed to have been gathered below ground. My pockets were empty save for the matches and the two strange, wilted flowers Weena had put there. They seemed a pathetically fragile charm against the horrors I was about to face. I found a length of metal, a slender bar I prized from some broken decorative work. It was flimsy, but it was better than my bare hands.

As dusk began to fall, casting long, menacing shadows across the landscape, I made my way to the well where I had seen the creature disappear. The air grew cooler, and a faint, almost imperceptible hum began to emanate from the opening, the sound of distant machinery stirring to life. The Eloi were gathering in their great halls for the night, huddling together for comfort against the encroaching darkness. None of them saw me as I stood at the brink of the abyss. For a moment, my courage failed me. The thought of the clinging, damp darkness, of the pale, luminous eyes, of the soft, unspeakable things that might touch me, was almost too much to bear. It was an instinctive, primal fear, the terror of the pit.

But the thought of my Time Machine, my only hope of escape from this dreadful future, was a more powerful spur. And curiosity, too, that intellectual impulse that had driven me to this mad venture in the first place, urged me on. I had to know. I had to see the full extent of humanity’s degradation. I took a deep breath, lit a match, and began my descent.

The metal rungs were cold and slimy to the touch. They were spaced for a creature smaller than myself, and I had to stretch uncomfortably to find my footing. The light of the match cast a small, flickering circle of visibility around me, revealing the curved, sweating walls of the shaft. The air grew warmer and fouler as I went down, thick with the smell of raw meat and un-oiled machinery. The humming grew louder, resolving itself into the rhythmic clatter and whir of a thousand engines. After a few dozen feet, the shaft opened out into a vast, cavernous space.

I paused on the ladder, letting my eyes adjust. I had lit a second match from the first, and its tiny flame fought a losing battle against the immense darkness. But it was not completely dark. Here and there, faint, ghostly patches of phosphorescence illuminated the scene, and in the distance, the ruddy glow of a great furnace pulsed like a monstrous, sleeping heart. I was in a colossal cavern, so large that the roof was lost in impenetrable blackness. All around me, huge, dark machines, their forms only vaguely guessed at, stood in silent rows. Some were in motion, their great pistons rising and falling with a deafening clangour that echoed through the space.

And then I saw the Morlocks. They were everywhere. Scores of them, hundreds of them, swarming over the machinery, scurrying through the shadows, their pale, hairless bodies gleaming faintly in the gloom. They were just as I had seen the one above: dull white, with large, reddish, reflective eyes. They moved with a strange, scuttling gait, their heads often bowed low. They paid me no mind at all at first, their attention wholly consumed by their tasks. They were tending the machines, oiling gears, polishing great metal surfaces, their soft, clumsy-looking hands moving with a surprising deftness. The air was filled with their low, chattering speech, a discordant and unpleasant sound.

I clung to the ladder, frozen, a spectator in hell. This was the engine room of the world. All the comfort and ease of the Eloi, their pleasant gardens, their crystal fountains, their very existence, was paid for by this ceaseless, sunless toil. I watched a group of them gathered around a large, polished metal table. A low-hanging lamp cast a brighter glow here, and I saw what they were doing. They were butchering some large animal. With a fresh wave of nausea, I saw that the carcass was not that of any beast I recognised; its form was horribly, unmistakably hominid. It was an Eloi. They were preparing their meal.

The sight broke my paralysis. A blind, furious terror seized me. I had to get away, to find my machine and flee. I dropped from the ladder to a wide ledge that ran along the wall of the cavern. The moment my feet touched the ground, several of the nearby Morlocks turned their great, vacant eyes upon me. Their chattering ceased. The silence spread outwards from them in a ripple, as more and more of the creatures noticed the intruder. I was larger than they were, and my clothes, my complexion, my very posture marked me as an alien.

For a long moment, we simply stared at one another. Their expressions were impossible to read. Was it fear? Curiosity? Or was it the simple, awful appraisal of a butcher examining a new source of meat? Then, one of them let out a low, guttural cry and took a hesitant, scuttling step towards me. I brandished my metal bar, and it paused. But others were now moving, their white forms detaching from the shadows, beginning to encircle me. They were cautious, but they were relentless. I was trapped.

My only hope was the light. I remembered their frantic flight from the sun. I struck another match. The sudden flare of it made them recoil with hissing cries, shielding their sensitive eyes with their hands. It was a momentary reprieve. I knew I could not hold them off for long with my dwindling supply of matches. I had to find the Time Machine. I began to back away along the ledge, holding the flickering match before me like a talisman. The Morlocks followed, a silent, shuffling tide of white bodies and glaring eyes, keeping just beyond the circle of light.

The ledge led me towards the great central furnace. The heat was becoming intense, and the clangour of the machinery was deafening. I scanned the cavern desperately. Where would they have taken it? They had some understanding of machinery; they would not have simply destroyed it. It would be a prize, a curiosity. I imagined it in some place of honour, if such a concept existed in their debased minds.

My match burned down, scorching my fingers. I dropped it and in the instant of darkness before I could strike another, I felt a soft, cold hand touch my arm. I cried out and swung the metal bar wildly, hitting something that squealed and fell back. I fumbled for my matchbox, my heart hammering against my ribs. I got one lit, and the circle of Morlocks was closer now, their eyes glowing with a dreadful, hungry light. They were becoming bolder.

I hurried on, half-running along the ledge, the patter of their bare feet a constant echo of my own. I passed alcoves and tunnels leading off into deeper blackness. I had no idea where I was going. My hope was beginning to dwindle. I was lost in this labyrinth, and my only defense was burning away, stick by stick. Then, through the din, I saw something that made my breath catch in my throat. In a large, open area not far from the furnace, stood a familiar crystalline framework, its quartz rods and brass fittings looking absurdly delicate and out of place amidst the brutalist machinery of the Morlocks. It was my Time Machine.

A wave of exultant relief washed over me. It was there! It was intact! A group of Morlocks were gathered around it, touching its levers and ivory saddle with a kind of reverent curiosity, but they did not seem to have damaged it. Now I had a destination. I had a purpose. I held my lit match high and charged towards them.

My sudden, aggressive movement took them by surprise. They scattered before me, hissing and shielding their eyes. I did not pause. I scrambled onto the saddle, my hands searching for the levers in the dark. I did not have time to be gentle. One lever to start the mechanism, the other to propel me into the future. That was my only thought: to get away, to move, to put any amount of time between myself and these cannibal horrors.

But as my fingers closed on the starting lever, I felt a swarm of them upon me. Their soft, weak bodies were a terrifying weight. Their cold hands clutched at my clothes, my limbs, my throat. I was being dragged from the saddle. They were surprisingly strong, and there were so many of them. I kicked and struggled, the metal bar I still held being a useless encumbrance. I felt their damp, fetid breath on my face, saw their great red eyes just inches from my own.

With a final, desperate effort, I thrust my hand forward and shoved the starting lever over. The familiar, deep hum of the machine began, and a faint, whirling sensation filled the air. This startled them. Their grip loosened for an instant. It was all I needed. I shook myself free, throwing several of them from the platform of the machine. I grabbed the second lever, the one that controlled the direction and velocity of travel. I did not care which way. I shoved it forward, as far as it would go.

The world dissolved into a grey, streaking chaos. The cavern, the furnace, the swarming faces of the Morlocks, all vanished in an instant. I was moving. I was free. I lay panting across the saddle, my body bruised and my nerves screaming. The humming of the machine was the sweetest sound I had ever heard. I had escaped. I had escaped! For how long I travelled, I do not know. I was in no state to read the dials. I simply let the machine carry me, further and further away from the ghastly year of 802,701. I needed time to recover, to think, to put the memory of those eyes and those hands behind me. Gradually, my panic subsided, replaced by a profound exhaustion. I pulled the lever back, slowing the machine, intending to stop in some anonymous, peaceful future epoch to rest. The streaking colours outside resolved themselves. The machine came to a gentle halt. And I found myself, to my utter and absolute horror, not in some new future, but back in the same cavern, in the same place, with the same crowd of Morlocks staring at me, their surprise turning to a renewed, and this time, more determined, hunger. In my blind panic, I had pushed the lever the wrong way. I had not gone forward, but back; and the few minutes I had travelled in reverse had landed me in the exact same spot, mere moments before my original arrival. They had seen me vanish, and now they had seen me reappear. I was no longer just an intruder. I was a miracle. And they were closing in.

They were a nightmare from which I had just awoken; and yet, the dream clung to me. I could still feel their soft, probing hands on my flesh, still see their relentless eyes burning in the dark. I shuddered violently, and not from the cold of my passage.

For an immense period I did not dare to touch the controls. I let the machine hurl me through the ages, glad of the incomprehensible gulf of years I was putting between myself and that awful cavern. I was a projectile, fired into the future. Who could say what cataclysms, what extinctions, what cosmic events were flashing past me, unseen and unrecorded? The grey band of days and nights began to alter its hue. It took on a reddish tinge, and the flickering seemed to slow, though the dials still showed a velocity of travel that was terrifying. The great globe of the earth, I reasoned, must be slowing its spin. The sun and moon, which had once painted the sky in streaks of gold and silver, now appeared as lingering, fiery arcs.

Slowly, very slowly, my terror subsided, replaced by a scientist’s curiosity. My body ached, but my mind began to work again, to analyse, to question. I raised my head and looked at the dials. The needle that recorded the years was in a section I had never seen, far beyond the million-year mark. The machine was still travelling at a stupendous rate, but I felt a growing compulsion to see what world I had reached. With infinite caution, my hand trembling, I reached for the lever. I eased it back, a fraction of an inch at a time.

The effect was not the gentle slowing I had hoped for. The machine bucked and groaned, a terrible grinding sound coming from its inner workings. I had indeed damaged the mechanism in my panicked flight. The streaking world outside did not resolve smoothly, but came into focus in a series of violent jerks. I saw glimpses of a desolate landscape under a vast, bloated, and crimson sun. Finally, with a shudder that threw me against the rail, the Time Machine came to a halt.

And silence. An absolute, profound, and unnerving silence.

I sat for a long time, listening to the ringing in my ears and the frantic beat of my own heart. The air that I drew into my lungs was thin and sharp, with a metallic taste, and it was terribly cold. I stood up, my limbs stiff and sore, and looked out upon the world I had found. My heart sank. The sun, as I had surmised, was a monstrous, swollen thing, hanging motionless in the sky. It no longer blazed, but smouldered, a deep and sullen red, casting no distinct shadows but bathing the entire landscape in a mournful, twilight glow. It covered, I should say, a full tenth part of the heavens. The earth, it seemed, had at last ceased its rotation, and presented one face to its dying parent.

I was on a gentle slope leading down to a shore. But the shore was not of any sea I knew. There was no tide, no waves, only a vast, motionless expanse of glistening salt flats, stretching away to a horizon that was lost in the red gloom. The sky was a deep, dark violet, and overhead, where the blue of our own sky would be, a few faint, unwinking stars were already visible. There was no sign of the moon. Perhaps she had met the fate Darwin predicted, and fallen back into the earth long ago, or perhaps she had drifted away into the void.

The ground beneath my feet was a reddish, powdery soil, almost like rust. And out of this soil grew a sparse and bizarre vegetation. There were no trees, no grasses, only low, tangled growths that looked like a cross between a lichen and a fungus. They were a dark, greenish-black, and seemed to suck the very light from the air, creating pools of deeper shadow on the dim landscape. A profound sense of desolation, of finality, washed over me. This was the end. I was standing on the gravestone of the world. All the triumphs and follies of humanity, the wars and discoveries, the love and the hatred, the building of cities and the writing of poetry, had led to this. To a silent, dying rock under a silent, dying sun. The Eloi and the Morlocks were but a passing fever in the planet’s long decline.

I felt a sudden, intense pang of loneliness, so powerful it was almost a physical blow. I thought of my own time, of the bustling streets of London, of the warmth of a fire in a hearth, of the sound of a human voice. It all seemed impossibly distant, a dream from another life. Was this to be my fate? To perish alone in the final twilight of the world?

A flicker of movement caught my eye. Down by the edge of the great salt plain, something stirred among the blackish vegetation. At first I thought it was a trick of the dim light, a product of my overstrained nerves. I stared intently, my breath held. It moved again. It was a large, dark shape, and it was not alone. There were others. They moved slowly, deliberately, with a strange, scuttling motion. My mind, still running in the grooves of my recent experience, leapt to a conclusion: a new kind of Morlock, adapted to the surface in this age of eternal twilight. I shrank back instinctively, my hand reaching for the iron bar that was no longer there.

But as I watched, I saw that these creatures were nothing like the Morlocks. They were much larger, for one thing, the size of a small bear, and their bodies were encased in a dark, chitinous shell that gleamed faintly in the red light. They appeared to have a multitude of jointed legs, and they moved with the ungainly grace of a great crab. I watched them for some time as they foraged among the dark lichen, plucking at it with complex manipulators near their heads. They paid me no mind. It seemed that in this silent world, I was no more worthy of notice than a stone.

My scientific curiosity began to outweigh my fear. Here was the ultimate fauna of the earth. What evolutionary path had led to these monstrous crustaceans? Were they the descendants of some humble crab or insect, come at last to inherit the planet when the vertebrates had passed away? I found myself wondering, in a detached and almost academic way, about the precise nature of their metabolism in this thin air and feeble light.

Compelled by this intellectual impulse, I took a step down from the platform of the machine. The powdery red soil crunched under my boots, a sound that seemed shockingly loud in the profound stillness. At the noise, one of the creatures stopped its foraging. It raised its head, a complex arrangement of jointed plates and multifaceted eyes, and turned it in my direction. Unlike the Morlocks’ eyes, which only reflected light, these seemed to generate a faint internal luminescence of their own, a cold, green phosphorescence. For a long moment, we regarded one another across the desolate slope. A man from the nineteenth century and, perhaps, the last living thing of any complexity on a thirty-million-year-old Earth.

Then a new and more terrible thing happened. From the deep violet of the sky above, another shape descended. It was not one of the crab-like beasts. This was a creature of the air, and it was vast. It drifted down on wide, leathery wings that were not unlike a bat’s, but translucent, and patterned with iridescent swirls of colour, a grotesque parody of a butterfly. Its body was long and thin, and it trailed a tangle of fine tentacles. It swooped silently over the foraging crab-creatures, and for a moment I thought it meant to attack them. But it did not. It settled gracefully on a tall outcrop of the black lichen, its wings folding with a soft, dry rustle. And then I saw another, and another, drifting down from the star-dusted gloom to join it.

The sky, I realised, was not empty. It was full of these silent, drifting horrors.

I stood frozen, a statue of apprehension. The world which had seemed merely desolate and sad a moment before was now imbued with a palpable sense of menace. These creatures, the crab-things on the ground and the great butterfly-monsters in the air, were not simply co-existing. There was a relationship between them, a balance of which I had no understanding. Was I a part of that balance? Was I predator, or prey? The cold air suddenly seemed to bite deeper. My breath plumed before me, a fleeting white ghost in the unending twilight. The silence pressed in, no longer peaceful, but predatory.

I began to back away towards the Time Machine. My every movement seemed clumsy and loud. A stone turned under my foot, and the clatter it made as it rolled a few feet down the slope was like a pistol shot. Several of the crab-things turned their glowing green eyes towards me. One of them took a few scuttling steps in my direction, its manipulators clicking together inquisitively. Above, on the lichen-crags, the great winged things stirred, their tentacles writhing slowly. They were all looking at me. I was the new thing. The anomaly. The unexpected variable in their ancient, static world.

And then I understood. In a world of slow, cold-blooded things, a warm, fast-metabolising mammal like myself would be a beacon. I was a store of energy, a prize of unimaginable richness. My panic, which had been lurking just beneath the surface of my scientific curiosity, returned with a vengeance. I scrambled back onto the saddle of the machine, my hands fumbling for the controls. The machine was my only sanctuary, my only hope of escaping this final, awful graveyard.

The crab-creature was now moving towards me with a disconcerting speed, its many legs carrying it over the rust-red soil in a smooth, flowing motion. It was no longer inquisitive. There was a clear purpose in its advance. I saw, with a fresh spike of horror, that its forward manipulators were not for plucking lichen, but were tipped with long, needle-sharp claws of a black, glassy material. I had no weapon, no defence. My mind screamed at my clumsy fingers as I sought the starting lever.

Where was it? The control panel, which had been so familiar to me, now seemed an alien landscape of knobs and levers. My panicked flight from the Morlocks had not only damaged the machine, it had dislodged the very levers from their customary settings. I found the main starting lever, bent and jammed in its socket. I pulled at it. It would not budge. The crab-thing was halfway to me, its green eyes glowing with a hungry light. I could hear the faint, dry clicking of its claws.

Despair seized me. To have come so far, to have escaped the cannibal future, only to be devoured at the end of time by a giant land-crab! The absurdity of it was a form of madness. With a cry of rage and terror, I grabbed the bent lever with both hands and heaved. There was a screech of protesting metal, a shower of sparks from the console, and the lever came free in my hands, a useless rod of brass and ivory. I had broken it. I was stranded.

The crab-thing was at the base of the machine now. It paused, raising its head to look at me, its complex mouthparts working. It placed a clawed foot on the framework of the machine and began to climb. At the same moment, one of the winged creatures detached itself from the rocks above. It swooped towards me, its iridescent wings making no sound, its tentacles trailing like a fisherman’s net. The end had come. I braced myself, the useless lever clutched in my hand like a sceptre, the last king of a dead world.

And then, from the bowels of the damaged machine, a spark ignited. The grinding noise I had heard earlier had not been the sound of failure, but of a mechanism resetting itself. A low hum began, the same hum that had been my salvation before. The crystals and quartz rods that powered the device began to glow with a faint, pulsing light. The machine was not dead. It was trying to repair itself, or perhaps my violent treatment of the lever had short-circuited some process and initiated another. The platform beneath my feet began to vibrate.

The crab-creature, its foot on the vibrating metal, recoiled as if stung. The winged horror, now almost upon me, abruptly veered away, disturbed by the unfamiliar energy. The humming grew louder, rising in pitch. The world outside, the red sun and the violet sky, began to shimmer and blur. The machine was activating on its own! But which way was it going? Was it taking me further into this dead future, or was it, by some miracle, returning me? I had no control. I was a passenger, not a pilot. The dials began to spin, slowly at first, then with increasing speed. The reddish gloom was replaced by the streaking grey of temporal transit. I had been given a reprieve. By sheer, dumb luck, I was moving again. I let the useless lever fall from my hand, and I began to laugh. It was a terrible, high-pitched sound, the laughter of a man who has stared into the abyss one too many times and found it staring back. The machine bore me onward, into the unknown, a ghost in a runaway hearse, leaving the last twilight of the Earth behind me.

The sensation was profoundly nauseating. I was a spectator to a cosmic drama played at an impossible speed, a blur of creation and destruction that threatened to unhinge my very reason. I saw beings that were not human, creatures of fire and of thought, ruling over worlds that burned with alien suns. I saw the first, tentative stirring of life in the primeval seas, and I saw the last star in the universe gutter and die, leaving a darkness more absolute than any I had yet witnessed. I lay where I had fallen for a long time, my cheek pressed against the cold brass of the platform. The silence was the first thing I truly noticed. It was not the mere absence of sound, like the hush of the far future; it was a profound and deliberate stillness, an organised quiet. It was as if the entire world were holding its breath. Cautiously, I raised my head. The machine had come to rest at a canted angle, one side of its framework embedded in a soft, pearlescent material that yielded like hard wax. Above me, there was no sky, no sun, no stars. Instead, a vast, luminous ceiling stretched to a horizon I could not discern, bathing everything in a soft, even, shadowless light. I had landed indoors. I climbed unsteadily to my feet, my body aching from the violent stop. I was in a colossal chamber, so large that the far walls were lost in a faint haze. The floor, like the substance my machine had crashed into, was a seamless, opalescent white. The architecture, if it could be called that, was composed of soaring, geometric forms; cubes and spheres and pyramids of immense size, arranged with a stark, mathematical precision. There was no decoration, no ornament, no colour save for the universal, monotonous white. It was like standing inside a diagram from a book of geometry.

And the silence persisted. I took a step, and the sound of my own boot on the strange flooring seemed a shocking intrusion, a vulgar noise in a sacred space. Where was I? And when? This was not the overgrown, decadent world of the Eloi, nor the industrial underworld of the Morlocks. It was something else entirely, something new and strange and unsettlingly clean. I looked for an exit, a door, a window, but the seamless walls offered no clue. I was in a box, a vast, white, silent box. My first thought was to examine the Time Machine. My heart sank. The crash had done more damage than all my frantic lever-pulling. The quartz rods were shattered, the console was a wreck of bent brass and splintered ivory, and the intricate framework was twisted and broken. The damage was catastrophic. I was not merely stranded; my vessel was a complete ruin. The thought, which should have filled me with despair, was strangely blunted. My mind, overstuffed with the horrors and wonders of my travels, could barely process this new calamity.

It was then that I first saw them. They had not arrived; they were simply there, as if they had coalesced from the white air itself. A group of perhaps a dozen figures stood a short distance away, observing me. They were human, or at least they were of human shape. They were of a uniform height and build, slender and graceful. Their skin was the same pearlescent white as the walls, and they were clad in simple, one-piece garments of the same material and colour. Their faces were the most arresting feature. They were perfectly symmetrical, beautiful in a cold, classical way, but utterly devoid of expression. Their eyes, a pale, placid grey, were all fixed upon me, but they held no curiosity, no fear, no aggression. They were the eyes of a biologist observing a specimen under a microscope. I could not tell male from female, young from old. They were identical, a series of perfect, repeating patterns.

I raised a hand in what I hoped was a gesture of peace. “I mean you no harm,” I said, and my voice, after the long silence, sounded like a blasphemous roar. It echoed unnaturally in the vast space. The figures did not react. They did not speak. They did not even blink. They simply watched. I tried again, using the simple phrases I had learned from the Eloi, but there was no flicker of recognition. These were not the children of the sun. Their placid stillness was unnerving. The fear I felt was not the primal dread of the Morlocks’ eyes in the dark, but a more insidious, intellectual horror. I felt like a discordant note in a perfect symphony, a splash of messy colour on a flawless white canvas. My very existence, with its untidy emotions and asymmetrical features, seemed an affront to their serene world. After a moment that stretched into an eternity, one of the figures detached itself from the group and glided towards me. I say glided, for its feet seemed to make no sound and its movement was unnaturally smooth, an unbroken, fluid motion. It stopped a few paces from me and raised its hand, not in greeting, but as if to indicate something. It pointed not at me, but at my wrecked machine. Then, its gaze returned to me, and for the first time, I detected a faint, almost imperceptible tremor in its placid expression. Was it a question? A judgement?

I felt a sudden, desperate need to explain myself, to justify my chaotic intrusion. I pointed to myself, to the machine, and then made a gesture to represent travelling, a spiralling motion with my finger. The figure watched, its head tilted slightly. Then it turned and glided back to its fellows. There was a moment of absolute stillness, and I had the strange impression that they were communicating, though no sound passed their lips, no gesture was made. It was a silent, instantaneous communion of thought. Then, as one, they turned and began to glide away, their movements perfectly synchronised. After a few paces, they stopped and turned back to look at me, a clear, unmistakable summons. They were not threatening me, not compelling me; they were simply indicating the path I was to take. My curiosity, that old, driving engine of my intellect, overcame my fear. I had to know what manner of world this was. Leaving the ruin of my hopes behind me, I followed them.

They led me through the vast, white chamber to a wall that, upon our approach, dissolved into a thousand tiny cubes that retracted into the ceiling and floor, revealing an identical chamber beyond. We passed through a series of such spaces, each one as vast and sterile as the last. There were no people, no signs of habitation, no personal belongings. It was a city without citizens, a house without a home. It came to me then, with the force of a revelation, that what I was seeing was the final, logical conclusion of a trend I had observed in my own time. Had not the socialists and utopians of my own day dreamed of such a world, a world free from want, from strife, from messy, inefficient human emotion? A world organised on purely rational principles, where the needs of the collective superseded the whims of the individual? I saw in this silent race the terrible apotheosis of the committee, the ultimate triumph of the bureaucracy. Humanity had not diverged into master and slave, but had converged into a single, homogenous entity. They were not individuals; they were components.

My silent guides led me at last to a smaller chamber, though it was still vast by my standards. In the centre of this room was a single piece of what I must call furniture: a long, white couch that seemed to have grown from the floor. My guides gestured towards it and then, in perfect unison, retreated to the far wall, resuming their silent, watchful stance. I was exhausted, my mind and body battered. I sank onto the couch, and found it to be surprisingly comfortable, moulding to my form. As soon as I was settled, a section of the floor beside the couch rose up, forming a small table. On its surface, a bowl of greyish, gelatinous substance and a beaker of clear liquid materialised, seemingly from nothing. Food and drink. The solicitousness was as unnerving as a threat. I was a guest, it seemed, or perhaps a specimen being prepared for study. Driven by a hunger I had not realised I possessed, I tentatively tasted the substance. It was utterly bland, without flavour or texture, yet it was strangely satisfying. It was not food; it was nutrition, stripped of all prosaic pleasure. The water was likewise pure, but flat, as if it had been distilled a thousand times.

I finished my meal, and as if on cue, the table and its contents sank back into the floor, leaving no trace. The silent figures remained, their placid grey eyes fixed upon me. I stood up, my heart hammering against my ribs. “What do you want?” I cried, the useless words swallowed by the immense silence. They did not answer. They simply moved closer, their circle tightening. I was not being attacked; I was being herded, contained. One of them, the one who had first approached me, raised its hands towards my head. The gesture was not violent, but gentle, almost clinical, like a doctor preparing to examine a patient. I saw then what they intended. They were not going to harm me. They were not going to imprison me. They were going to fix me. They were going to smooth out my rough edges, quiet my noisy emotions, and absorb me into their silent, perfect whole. I was a disease, and they were the white blood cells of this planetary organism, moving to neutralise the infection.

I followed it as a drowning man follows a current, with a desperate, burgeoning excitement.

The corridor ended at last, not in a wall, but in a vast, circular chamber, larger than any I had yet seen. The pulsing was strongest here, and it was accompanied now by a low, humming sound. The centre of the room was dominated by a great, slowly rotating column of a material darker than the rest of the structure, a kind of smoky grey crystal. But it was what was happening at the base of this column that seized my entire attention, filling me with a mixture of revulsion and profound understanding.

Figures, the same white, placid beings as my pursuers, were moving in a slow, unending procession towards the column. They walked with the same unhurried grace, but there was a finality to their movement now. As each one reached the base of the structure, it would stand for a moment, head bowed; then, it would simply… dissolve. It would lose its cohesion, its form flowing like wax into a series of shallow, concentric troughs that spiralled into the base of the pillar. The material, or life-force, or whatever it was, was drawn up into the rotating column, where it swirled in slow, pearlescent eddies. At the same time, from the top of the same column, a fine, shimmering mist descended, coalescing on the floor into new, fully-formed figures, that stood, blinked their great grey eyes, and then glided off to join the silent, ordered life of this world.

I had found their genesis, and their end. It was a system of perfect, unending recycling. There was no birth, no death; only reabsorption and reconstitution. Individuality was not just suppressed here; it was a temporary state, a brief loan of substance from the central whole. This was the source of their placidity, their lack of fear or desire. Why would a single drop of water fear its return to the ocean? I looked upon this process and I saw, with a clarity that chilled me to the bone, the ultimate triumph of the collective over the individual, of the species over the man. Here was the final answer to the social question. Humanity had not just split into the Labourer and the Capitalist, the subterranean and the overworlder; it had, in the end, transcended both, becoming a single, self-sustaining, self-consuming organism. The Eloi and the Morlocks had been but a chaotic, transitional phase. This was the final, serene, and terrible peace.

My mind, reeling from the philosophical implications, was abruptly recalled to my own, more immediate, problem. This vast machine, this soul-engine, required power. It required maintenance. And as I scanned its periphery, my eyes fell upon a sight that made my heart leap. Set into the wall at regular intervals were what appeared to be access panels, made of a familiar, dull grey metal. And beside one of them, lying on the floor as if carelessly left behind by some long-vanished technician, was a heavy metal bar, perhaps an arm in length, with a hooked, flattened end. It was a lever. A tool. A beautiful, ugly, imperfect, wonderful thing.

I knew at once what I must do. My act of defiance could no longer be passive. I had to commit a sin in this perfect world; I had to commit an act of theft.

Steeling myself, I stepped out from the corridor’s entrance into the vast, humming chamber. The moment my feet crossed the threshold, a change occurred. The figures in the slow procession did not stop, they did not turn. But I felt a change in the psychic pressure, a focusing of their collective attention. The observation was no longer passive; it was sharpening to a point. I ignored it. I walked with as much speed as I could muster without breaking into an undignified run, my path taking me along the circumference of the great room towards the discarded tool.

The air grew thick, heavy with a silent, questioning disapproval. It was like wading through molasses. Every instinct screamed at me to turn back, to rejoin the watchers in the hall, to give up this mad, singular quest. But the image of my broken machine, and of the green, sunlit world of my own time, burned brighter in my mind. I reached the tool. My hand closed around the cold, solid metal. It felt real, substantial, a comfort in this world of ethereal forms.

I had it. But as I straightened up, I saw that the procession had stopped. The great recycling machine still hummed and turned, but the line of white figures that fed it was now still. And every single one of them, those in the line and those newly formed, was turned towards me. They stood, a silent army of accusers, and for the first time, I sensed an emotion from them: a profound, sorrowful, and utterly implacable sense of _wrongness_. I had introduced a discordant element that their system could not immediately absorb. I had taken something. I had asserted a self, a will, a desire separate from the whole.

And then they began to move towards me.

They did not run. Their movement was no different from their usual glide, but now it was directed, it was purposeful, and it was convergent upon me. They flowed from all sides, a silent, white tide. I did not wait to see what form their correction would take. Clutching the heavy metal bar, I ran. I ran as I had never run before, my breath searing my lungs, my boots slipping and skidding on the polished floor. The placid horror of it was complete; a chase without a sound, a pursuit without anger. It was the dispassionate, inexorable movement of a body’s immune system closing in on a foreign cell.

I plunged back into the featureless corridor, the metal bar held like a club, though I knew it would be useless. How can you fight the tide with a stick? The psychic pressure was immense, a tangible force that seemed to slow my limbs and cloud my thoughts, urging me, pleading with me, to stop, to cease, to be still. Behind me, I could hear no footsteps, only the faint, whispering slide of their passage, a sound that was somehow more terrifying than a roar. They filled the corridor behind me, a river of white that flowed in my wake.

By some miracle, or perhaps because all paths in this place were ultimately the same, I burst back into the great hall where my Time Machine stood. The figures that had been guarding the entrance turned and were simply absorbed into the new wave of pursuers that poured in behind me. I scrambled onto the platform of the machine, turning to face them, panting, the heavy bar held ready.

They stopped, just as before, at the edge of the chamber. The entire hall was now filled with them, a silent, staring host. They had me cornered. They had retrieved their stray part. The trap was sprung. But this time, something was different. I was different. I was no longer a man in despair; I was a man with a tool. Hope, however slim, had returned.

My eyes fell upon the pulverised remains of the quartz rod. Impossible to repair, yes. But perhaps not impossible to replace. The thought struck me with the force of a physical blow. The crystalline structures of this world, the very stuff from which it was built, were they not of a similar nature? The smoky grey column in the recycling chamber, the opalescent floor, even the strange, dissolving doorways. They all possessed a strange, resonant quality. Could a piece of this world serve to bridge the gap in my own?

It was a desperate, insane gamble. The physics of it were a nightmare. To integrate a piece of this timeline’s matter into a machine designed to travel through time might simply tear it, and me, to pieces. But what was the alternative? To be slowly, peacefully, and utterly erased.

I looked at the silent, waiting army. I looked at the heavy metal bar in my hand. It was not just a lever; it was a crowbar. It was a weapon of deconstruction. An instrument of imperfection. I turned my back on the white host and faced the wreckage of my machine. I had to work quickly, before their patience finally expired, before they decided on a more direct method of restoring order. With the metal bar, I would pry. I would break. I would scavenge. And I would use their own perfect world as the raw material for my escape from it. My mind raced, calculating, designing, the old spark of the inventor fanned into a frantic, desperate flame by the cold breath of the future. The work had begun.

The bar felt heavy in my hands, a crude relic of an age of iron and effort in a world that had forgotten both. My gaze swept across the great hall, seeking a point of violation. The walls rose in seamless, faintly luminous curves, their surfaces unbroken by joint or seam. To attack them would be like trying to quarry a cloud. The floor, however, was different. Though it appeared as a single, opalescent sheet, it was composed of flagstones of immense size, the hairline cracks between them filled with a substance of the same pearlescent quality. It was here, at these junctures, that a fracture might be initiated. I chose a spot near the base of one of the arching supports, a place where the weight of the superstructure might have created some internal stress. I knelt, the cold of the floor seeping through the thin stuff of my trousers, and tapped the surface with the end of the metal bar. The sound was not the dull clink of iron on stone, but a clear, resonant chime, a pure musical note that hung in the vast, silent chamber like a prayer. It was a beautiful sound, and for that reason, a profoundly terrifying one.

A change occurred in the silent assembly of my watchers. It was not a movement I could see, but one I could feel, a sudden tightening in the psychic atmosphere. The gentle, pervasive pressure became a focused point of inquiry, a silent, unified question directed entirely at me. The note I had struck was an anomaly, a discordant datum in their seamless sensory world, and they were, in their collective, placid way, trying to understand it. I knew that their understanding would be my doom. Their stillness was not passivity; it was the coiled tension of a system assessing a fault. I had to act before the assessment was complete. I raised the bar high over my shoulder, my muscles bunching, my breath held tight in my chest. The faces of the white figures, a thousand pale moons with large, dark eyes, were turned upon me. I was a barbarian at the gates of a city of ghosts. I brought the bar down with all the force I could muster.

The impact was cataclysmic. A sound unlike any I have ever heard, a shriek of tortured crystal, exploded through the hall. It was a chord of pure agony, a sound that was both high and low, a physical blow to the ears and a sharp pain behind the eyes. A network of brilliant white cracks, like lightning frozen in glass, erupted from the point of impact. And with the sound, the psychic pressure shattered its dam. It was no longer a pressure; it was a wave, a silent, deafening scream that crashed against my mind. I staggered back, dropping the bar with a clang, my hands flying to my head. It was the concentrated despair of a million souls, the anguish of a perfect, unbroken thing that has been irrevocably shattered. I felt their shock, their pain, as if it were my own. They were not angry; they were wounded. I had not merely broken a stone; I had inflicted a grievous injury upon the body of their world, and by extension, upon them.

For a moment, I was paralysed by this shared trauma, my own will submerged in the tide of their collective suffering. But the sight of the fractured flagstone, a spiderweb of pure light, called me back to my purpose. I fought against the mental onslaught, a battle more draining than any physical toil. It was like trying to think in the middle of a siren’s wail. I forced myself to my knees, my hands trembling, and retrieved the iron bar. The white figures had not moved, but the quality of their stillness had changed. It was now the stillness of agony, of a system trying to contain a fatal wound. A faint, almost imperceptible tremor ran through the floor. I set the tip of the bar into the largest of the new cracks and threw my weight upon it. The crystal groaned, a low, vibratory hum that I felt in my bones. I was a surgeon performing a brutal amputation. I heaved again, my teeth gritted, sweat and tears of exertion mixing on my face. The psychic pain intensified, trying to force me to stop, to cease my desecration. Images of serene, unbroken surfaces, of silent, peaceful halls, flooded my mind, a desperate plea for me to restore the harmony I had destroyed.

With a final, sickening crack, a large triangular section of the flagstone broke free. It was perhaps three feet long on its longest side and a hand’s breadth thick, a jagged shard of captured moonlight. The moment it separated from the whole, the psychic scream faltered, changing its nature. The raw pain subsided, replaced by something else, a deep and profound sense of loss, of wrongness. The broken piece on the floor was an error, an orphan, a thing that did not belong. The host of white figures seemed to shrink back, though none had moved an inch. Their attention, and mine, was fixed on the glittering, broken thing. It pulsed with a soft, internal light, its edges sharp as razors. The work, however, was only just begun. I had a slab; I needed a rod.

I dragged the heavy shard away from the hole it had left, a gaping, dark wound in the perfect floor. I would need to shape it, to grind it down. My only tool was the iron bar and the floor itself. I laid the shard flat and, using the corner of the bar, I began to score a line along its length. The grating sound was monstrous, a heresy in that silent place. Each scrape sent a fresh tremor of distaste through the psychic medium, a shudder of revulsion. They were watching me, not with the curiosity of before, nor the agony of the breaking, but with a kind of clinical horror, as one might watch a disease vector under a microscope. I was entropy incarnate. I was the agent of decay. I was everything their evolution had sought to eliminate. Yet they did not act. My theory solidified: their response to imperfection was not to attack it, but to isolate it, to contain it, and, I suspected, to eventually erase it, to unmake it. My very presence, my every action, was being walled off from their reality by the sheer force of their collective will. I was being edited out of their world, and once they had finished the process, I would simply cease to be.

The work was gruelling. I scored the line deeper and deeper, then turned the slab over and began to do the same on the other side. My arms ached, my hands were raw, but I dared not stop. The psychic pressure was now a slow, crushing weight, a force of immense gravity that seemed to thicken the air and slow my movements. My thoughts grew sluggish, my purpose harder to hold. The desire to simply lie down, to close my eyes and let the serene silence reclaim me, was immense. It was the most insidious attack yet, not of pain, but of peace. It promised an end to struggle, a gentle dissolution into the quiet perfection of their world. I fought it by focusing on the raw, physical reality of my task: the bite of the iron into the crystal, the sting of the fine, glittering dust that filled the air, the ache in my back. I was a creature of grit and effort, and I clung to these sensations as a drowning man clings to a rough-hewn plank.

After what seemed an age, the groove was deep enough. I propped the slab on a piece of its own debris and, with a sharp, calculated blow from the bar, I struck the centre of the score line. It split, not cleanly, but with a jagged, crystalline fracture, into two long, unequal pieces. One was a rough approximation of the rod I needed. It was too thick, too angular, its surface a mass of sharp edges and uneven planes. Now came the most tedious labour of all. I found a less damaged section of the floor and, kneeling, began to grind the rough-hewn crystal against it. The noise was a continuous, rasping shriek. The psychic assault redoubled, the promise of peace becoming an overwhelming siren song. Rest. Cease. Be still. The words formed not in my ears, but in the very structure of my consciousness. I saw visions of my own world, of my quiet study, of a warm fire and a good book. I saw Weena’s face, not as I had last seen her, but smiling in the sunshine, free from fear. They were using my own memories, my own desires, as a weapon against me, offering me a phantom solace if I would only stop my monstrous, noisy work.

I roared, a hoarse, animal sound of defiance, and ground the crystal harder. I would not be lulled into oblivion. I would not trade my brutal reality for their placid mirage. The crystal grew warm in my hands, then hot. Fine, sharp dust coated my arms and face, embedding itself in my skin. Slowly, painfully, a cylindrical shape began to emerge. It was a caricature of the smooth, precise quartz rod it was meant to replace, a lumpy, scarred, and imperfect thing. But it was a rod. It was a key. As I worked, I noticed a strange property of the material. The friction was causing it to glow, not merely with the heat of abrasion, but with its own internal luminescence, a pulsing, rhythmic light that seemed to answer the frantic beat of my own heart. It felt alive in my hands, a captured fragment of this world’s soul.

Finally, it was done. The rod was as smooth as I could make it, a clumsy cylinder of milky, glowing crystal, still warm to the touch. It was shorter than the original, and thicker in places, but it would have to do. I scrambled back to the Time Machine, the crude rod clutched in my hand like a sceptre. The white figures remained motionless, a silent amphitheatre of spectators. But the psychic pressure had reached an unbearable crescendo. The air was thick as syrup. To move was to swim against a current of pure will. To think was to shout into a gale of silence. They were making their final effort to contain me, to nullify me before I could commit my ultimate act of sacrilege. I could feel my own consciousness beginning to fray at the edges, my sense of self thinning. I had moments, perhaps seconds, before I dissolved completely.

I tore at the twisted metal housing where the old rod had been, my fingers clumsy and weak. The pulverised quartz dust fell out like fine sand. The new rod would not fit. The opening was too precise, my creation too crude. In a fit of desperate rage, I used the end of the iron bar to hammer it into place. The machine shrieked in protest. Metal bent. The crystal rod, with a final, jarring crack, wedged itself into the mechanism. The internal glow of the rod flared violently, illuminating the whole machine in a sickly, pulsating light. I threw the iron bar aside. It clattered across the floor, the sound swallowed by the immense, final silence. My limbs felt like lead. My head swam. I was losing. The psychic tide was pulling me under. With the last dregs of my will, I reached for the control levers. My hand closed over the smaller one, the one that initiated the journey.

The moment my fingers touched it, the white figures moved. It was not a charge, but a flow. They poured from the entrances and across the hall, a river of white, their individual forms merging into a single, advancing wave. The trap was no longer passive. They were coming to reclaim the broken part of their world, and me along with it. I had no time. I thrust the lever forward.

The result was not the familiar, smooth vertigo of temporal displacement. It was an explosion. A blast of discoloured light erupted from the new crystal rod, and a wrenching, grinding shudder tore through the frame of the machine. The great hall did not fade; it fractured. It broke into a thousand shards of reality, each shard reflecting a different moment, a different possibility. I saw the hall as it was being built by strange, willowy figures under a younger sun. I saw it in ruins, covered in dust, long after my own time. I saw the white creatures, not as they were, but as their distant ancestors, small, frightened things hiding in the dark. The crystal rod, a piece of this timeline, was fighting the machine, trying to anchor it, to hold it in its native continuity. The psychic connection, which I had thought would be severed, became a conduit for a storm of raw data. I was inside their collective mind, seeing their entire history, their slow, deliberate evolution away from individuality, their culling of passion and strife, their construction of a perfect, stable, eternal present. I felt their billion-year-long peace, and I felt their terror and agony at my intrusion, the first new thing to happen to them in an aeon.

Then, with a snap that felt as if my own soul had been torn in two, the connection broke. The fractured images of the hall vanished. The machine gave a final, violent lurch, and I was thrown against the railing. I found myself in the familiar, swirling grey chaos of the time stream. But it was different. The steady, silent passage was gone. The machine was bucking and vibrating, the motion erratic, violent. The grey void was shot through with streaks of ugly, bruised colour, like a sickness. And the new crystal rod at the heart of the machine was no longer just glowing; it was burning with a furious, unstable light, pulsing like a diseased heart. It was a wound in the core of my machine, a piece of another reality, a cancer I had placed there myself.

I had escaped. I had broken their perfect prison and stolen a piece of it to carry me away. But my machine was compromised, perhaps fatally. It was no longer a precise instrument of science; it was a broken, lurching, unpredictable wreck. I had no idea if the dials were still accurate, or if the controls would even respond. I had fled the stillness of their utopia only to find myself on a runaway engine, hurtling uncontrollably through the screaming chaos between the ticks of the clock. I looked at the hateful, beautiful, alien rod burning before me, and I did not know whether I was escaping to my salvation, or merely to a more inventive and terrifying form of doom. The work was not over; a new and more uncertain labour had just begun.

I was an anomaly, a soft, fragile, ridiculously-attired morsel in a world of teeth and claws and armour plate.

My first task was survival. I patted my pockets. My matches, thankfully in their waterproof case, were still there. A small blessing, but a significant one. Fire was a weapon, a tool, a symbol of human ingenuity that might yet have power in this savage world. My pocketknife was also present. Paltry defences, but better than nothing. I needed to assess the damage to the machine more thoroughly. If it could be repaired, if the alien crystal could be replaced by the original, which I had thankfully stored in a padded compartment, then I might yet escape.

I approached the machine with a new sense of purpose. The fall had driven it deep into the soft earth. I would need to dig it out, a Herculean task in itself. I ran my hands over the controls. The starting lever was jammed. The dials were shattered. But the core frame, the nickel and ivory and crystal that I had built with my own hands, seemed largely intact beneath the mud and cosmetic damage. The key was the central rod. I unlatched the compartment where I kept my spares. There it was, my own beautiful, perfect, hand-ground crystal rod, glowing with a faint, familiar pearlescence. It was safe.

A wave of hope, reckless and perhaps unfounded, washed over me. The task was immense, but not impossible. I was a scientist, an engineer. This machine was the work of my own mind and hands. I could unmake it and remake it. I could fix it. I set to work at once, using my hands and my knife to begin clearing the soil from around the machine’s base. I was so engrossed in this task, so focused on the familiar mechanics of my own creation, that I grew careless. I forgot the sounds of the forest. I forgot the booming call.

That was my mistake.

A shadow fell over me, so sudden and complete that it was as if the sun had been extinguished. But there was no sun here, only the glowing fungi and the dim light filtering through the canopy. The insect hum ceased. The forest held its breath. I looked up slowly, my heart a cold stone in my chest.

Standing over me, its head blotting out the distant ceiling of leaves, was a creature from a nightmare. It was a biped, but a biped that stood thirty, perhaps forty feet high. Its skin was a mosaic of leathery, greenish-brown scales, like the hide of a crocodile magnified a hundred times. Its legs were pillars of muscle, and its feet, each armed with three immense talons, were planted firmly on the forest floor. A great, muscular tail, as long as its body, balanced its prodigious weight. But it was the head that held my gaze, the head that was now slowly lowering itself towards me on a thick, sinuous neck. The mouth was a yard-long gash filled with teeth like daggers, and the eyes, set deep beneath a bony brow, were the size of dinner plates; golden, reptilian, and utterly devoid of anything I could recognise as thought or emotion. They were simply the eyes of a predator, assessing a novel form of prey.

It opened its mouth, and the sound that emerged was not the distant boom I had heard before, but a deafening, earth-shaking roar that vibrated through my very bones. The air filled with the carrion stench of its breath. I was frozen, not with the intellectual fear I had felt for the Morlocks, but with a primal, instinctual terror that dissolved all higher thought. This was the reality of the deep past; not a scientific curiosity, but a living, breathing, hungry engine of destruction.

And then I thought of my journey. I had fled a future where mind had triumphed over body, where existence was a sterile, static perfection of pure consciousness. I had railed against it, fought it, and escaped it. And my escape had led me here, to the polar opposite. A world where body had triumphed over mind, where existence was a brutal, chaotic struggle for physical dominance. Those silent, ethereal beings of the ultimate future, and this mindless, magnificent brute of the primordial past; were they not two sides of the same coin? Two expressions of a universe that had no place for the delicate, troubled, self-aware balance that was Man?

I had travelled to the end of time, and to its beginning. I had seen the last men, and now I was seeing the first monsters. And in that moment, as the great head dipped lower and a drop of saliva the size of my fist splashed onto the mud beside me, a new and terrible theory began to form in my mind. Perhaps the history of life was not a steady, progressive ascent towards intelligence and civilisation. Perhaps it was a vast, cosmic oscillation, a pendulum swinging between two equally inhuman extremes: the tyranny of the mind, and the tyranny of the flesh. My gaze flickered from the monster’s eye to the broken machine at my feet. The problem was no longer merely one of engineering; it was one of philosophy. And I had a dreadful suspicion that I would have very little time to solve either.

I was not a puzzle, not a threat, not even an object of interest; I was merely meat, an unexpected morsel that had stumbled into the larder. The stench, a thick, cloying miasma of rot and ancient musk, was a physical presence, a foul blanket that threatened to suffocate me. My theory of cosmic oscillation, so grand and intellectually satisfying a moment before, now seemed a pathetic, flimsy shield against the coming annihilation. What use were philosophies to a creature whose entire existence was a statement of irrefutable physical fact?

The vast reptilian eyelid, a leathery curtain, slammed shut, and the creature thrashed its head from side to side, momentarily blinded and utterly bewildered. It had likely never experienced such a sharp, precise pain in its entire reign of dominance. It was a sensation for which its brain, a mere node of instinct and appetite, had no category.

It was the silence of a world without mankind, without the familiar noises of industry, of society, of life as I understood it. I leaned against the cool, damp bark of a tree whose species I could not name, and drew in ragged, shuddering breaths. The air was thick, humid, and rich with the smell of vegetable decay and strange, alien blossoms. I had survived. The thought was a simple one, yet it took some moments for it to take root in my consciousness. I had faced the absolute tyranny of the flesh and, through a combination of luck and the peculiar properties of my own artifice, I had escaped.

Only when the trembling in my limbs had subsided did I allow myself to fully assess my predicament. I turned my attention back to the Time Machine. It lay tilted in the mire, a thing of brass and ivory and crystal looking terribly frail and out of place amidst the brutal fecundity of the Cretaceous world. My heart sank as I confirmed the damage. The lever I had desperately wrenched, the one that controlled the finer gradations of temporal movement, was not merely bent, but shattered at its base; the delicate crystal that formed its handle was a spray of glittering dust upon the mud. Worse, as I had already noted, the primary temporal stabiliser, a rod of spun quartz as thick as my wrist, was snapped clean in two. Without it, the machine’s journey through the aeons would be uncontrollably violent; it would be torn apart by the very forces it was designed to navigate.

The task before me was Herculean. I had no tools, save for a small pocket-knife. I had no workshop. I had no materials, other than what this savage and unconquered world might offer. I looked at the broken quartz rod. Its crystalline structure was precise, grown under specific conditions of temperature and pressure, and machined to an exact tolerance. To replace it seemed a task not for a man, but for a geological age and a fully equipped laboratory.

Then, I explored my immediate surroundings with a new and cautious eye. This was no longer a philosophical expedition; it was a campaign for survival. I discovered a stream of fresh, cool water, and drank from it like an animal, cupping my hands. I saw insects the size of small birds, and lizards with brightly coloured frills that scurried away at my approach. The world was teeming with life, all of it engaged in a constant, remorseless struggle. I saw then, with a clarity that bordered on revelation, that the beast’s very magnificence was a product of its intellectual poverty; it had poured all of life’s energy into the perfection of its physical form, leaving no surplus for the anxieties of the soul or the torments of the mind. The Eloi of the far future had done the precise opposite, abandoning the body for a placid, thoughtless serenity. Both were dead ends. Humanity, my humanity, existed in the perilous, unstable balance between these two poles, in the divine discontent that arose from being a thinking animal.

My most pressing need, after securing the machine and finding water, was a defensible shelter. The forest floor was no place to spend the night. I followed the stream uphill, reasoning that higher ground might offer rock formations, perhaps even a cave. The flora was a botanist’s nightmare, or his paradise. Overhead, I occasionally glimpsed leathery-winged shapes, pterosaurs of various sizes, soaring on the thermal currents with an eerie, silent grace.

After an hour of cautious climbing, I found what I was looking for: a fissure in a weathered cliff-face, half-hidden by a cascade of thick, trailing vines. It was not deep, no more than a dozen feet, but it was dry and its entrance was narrow enough that I might hope to defend it. As twilight began to fall, a swift and starless affair beneath the thick cloud cover, I retreated into this shallow cave and watched as the world outside transformed. The humming vibrancy of the day was replaced by a chorus of clicks, croaks, and strange, booming calls from unseen creatures. The darkness was absolute, a palpable blackness that my civilised eyes, accustomed to the perpetual glow of city nights, could not penetrate.

I did not sleep, but passed the night in a state of heightened vigilance, my hand rarely leaving the pocket-knife that was my only weapon. My thoughts were a torrent of engineering problems and philosophical conundrums. How to fix the lever? Perhaps I could fashion a replacement from a particularly hard wood, shaping it with my knife. But the quartz rod; that was the true barrier. Quartz. Silicon dioxide. One of the most common minerals on earth. But it had to be a single, large, flawless crystal. Where could such a thing be found? I dredged up my geological knowledge. Large crystals form in pegmatites, in hydrothermal veins, in geodes. I would have to become a prospector in the age of dinosaurs. My course was suddenly clear to me: I must find a replacement for the shattered quartz.

The following days settled into a strange and perilous routine. By day, I was an explorer and a scientist, and by night, a prisoner in my rock-hewn cell. I foraged for food, subsisting on fern shoots that resembled the fiddleheads of my own time, and on large, starchy tubers that I dug from the ground with a sharpened stick. I observed the fauna of this ancient world with a mixture of terror and scientific detachment. I saw vast herds of three-horned beasts, Triceratops I presumed, moving through the valleys like living battleships. I watched long-necked behemoths, Brachiosaurs perhaps, stripping the foliage from the tops of the tallest trees, their movements slow and ponderous as the turning of a century.

It was on my fourth day of this new existence that I made a discovery that was, in its own way, as unsettling as my first encounter with the great carnivore. I had followed my stream further up its course than ever before, into a narrow gorge where the water had cut deeply into the rock. I was searching for granite outcroppings, the sort of geological formation that might contain the pegmatite dikes I sought. As I rounded a bend, I saw something lying in the shallow, sun-dappled water near the bank. It was not a rock of the riverbed, for it was a perfect, milky-white sphere, some two inches in diameter.

I picked it up. It was surprisingly heavy, and its surface was smooth, impossibly smooth, as if it had been polished for a thousand years. It was not quartz; it felt denser, more like a form of marble or alabaster. But its perfect sphericity was what held me spellbound. Nature produces approximations, but this was a work of geometry. No process of erosion, no tumbling in a riverbed for a million years, could produce such flawless perfection. It had been made.

The implications of this were staggering. My neat theory of a world dominated by the tyranny of mindless flesh, a world devoid of intelligence, was shattered. Here was evidence of artifice. Here was the work of a mind, and of hands. But what mind? And what hands? I scanned the cliffs and the forest around me, a new kind of fear prickling my skin. It was one thing to be a solitary intellect in a world of brutes; it was quite another to be an intruder in a world belonging to an unknown and unseen intelligence. Who were they, these artisans of the Cretaceous? Were they land-dwellers or aquatic? Were they the builders of some lost civilisation, or something far stranger? This smooth, white ball, so simple and yet so inexplicable, was more mysterious than any fossil. It was a message from a contemporary, and I had no way to read it.

I pocketed the sphere, my mind reeling. The quest for quartz now had a companion; the quest for the makers of this strange object. I pressed on, my senses even more alert than before. Every shadow seemed to hold a watching eye, every rustle of leaves the sign of a hidden observer.

Late in the afternoon, my search for the raw materials of my escape bore fruit. High on the wall of the gorge, a place that caught the western sun, was a distinct geological feature. A broad, whitish seam of rock had intruded into the darker basalt of the cliff. And embedded within it, glittering like diamonds, were huge crystals. Quartz! Some were as long as my arm, great hexagonal prisms of milky and clear crystal, a treasure trove beyond my wildest hopes.

My elation was quickly tempered by the reality of the situation. The seam was a good fifty feet up a sheer cliff face. And more importantly, the base of the cliff was not deserted. Prowling back and forth on a wide ledge that ran beneath the quartz vein was a pack of creatures unlike any I had yet seen. They were smaller than the great carnivore, perhaps the height of a man, but they were built for a terrible speed. They ran on two powerful hind legs, balanced by a stiff, outstretched tail. Their arms were long and equipped with vicious, sickle-like claws. But it was their heads that truly unnerved me. They were narrow, elongated, and their eyes, instead of being on the sides of their heads like most of the herbivores, were set forward, granting them binocular vision. They moved not as individuals, but as a unit, their heads turning in unison, their movements coordinated. I was looking, I realised with a jolt, at a pack of intelligent hunters. These were no mindless engines of destruction. This was a cohort of killers, a hunting party.

Were these the makers of the sphere? It seemed unlikely. Their claws were instruments of butchery, not craftsmanship. Yet their coordinated behaviour, the way they seemed to communicate with subtle gestures and soft, chirping calls, spoke of a social intelligence far beyond that of the other dinosaurs. Perhaps they were the dominant species of this age, their intelligence one of pure, predatory cunning. My theory of oscillation was not wrong, perhaps, but merely incomplete. Life’s pendulum did not swing merely between mind and flesh; it explored all the terrifying possibilities in between.

I watched them from the cover of a thicket of cycads, my heart pounding. The quartz was there, within my sight, the key to my return. But it was guarded by a nightmare. To approach would be suicide. For a long time I simply observed, the scientist in me taking over from the terrified man. The pack, there were six of them, seemed to be waiting. They patrolled the ledge, occasionally sniffing at the air or scraping their claws against the rock.

Then I saw what they were waiting for. From a cleft in the rocks high above the quartz vein, a family of small, furry, rodent-like mammals scurried into view. They were clearly a food source. The pack of predators below grew tense, their bodies coiled like springs. Their strategy became clear; they were waiting for one of the small creatures to fall.

A plan, desperate and foolhardy, began to form in my mind. I could not fight them. But perhaps I could distract them. I looked up at the cliff face, at the loose scree and weathered rock above the ledge where they patrolled. If I could start a rockslide, it might send them scattering, giving me a precious minute to climb the cliff, secure a crystal, and make my escape.

It was my only chance. I began a wide, circling detour, climbing the side of the gorge through the dense undergrowth until I was high above the pack. I moved with the stealth of a creature that has been hunted. From my new vantage point, I could see the predators on their ledge, and the glittering quartz vein beyond them. I found what I was looking for: a large, precariously balanced boulder resting on a slope of loose shale. Dislodging it would send an avalanche of rock and debris down onto the ledge.

My hands were shaking as I put my shoulder to the boulder. It was heavy, deeply embedded. I pushed again, my muscles straining. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, with a deep groaning sound, the earth shifted. The boulder tipped, hung for a heart-stopping second, and then plunged downwards, followed by a river of stones and dirt.

The effect was everything I had hoped for. The predators, taken completely by surprise, shrieked with alarm and scattered. The avalanche of rock crashed onto the ledge, sending up a cloud of dust. Now was my moment. I did not hesitate. I half-ran, half-slid down the steep slope, my boots finding little purchase. I reached the main cliff face, my hands raw, and began to climb towards the quartz. The rock was jagged, offering plentiful handholds. I scrambled upwards, my lungs burning, my eyes fixed on my prize.

I reached the vein. The crystals were magnificent, embedded deep in the parent rock. I had to break one free. I pulled out my pocket-knife, a pathetic tool for such a task, and began to pry and lever at the base of a crystal about the size of my forearm. The rock was hard. My knife blade bent. In desperation, I used a sharp-edged stone I had grabbed during my descent, hammering at the junction between the crystal and the rock. A crack appeared. I hammered again, frantically.

The chirping cries from below sharpened in tone. The dust was settling. Through it, I could see the predators regrouping. They had realised the rockfall was not a natural event. Their heads were turned upwards, their intelligent, forward-facing eyes fixed directly on me. One of them let out a sharp, commanding bark, and in perfect unison, two of them began to climb the cliff.

They were impossibly fast, their sickle-claws finding purchase in the rock as easily as I used my hands. A wave of pure terror washed over me. I gave one last, desperate blow with my stone. There was a sharp crack, and the crystal came loose in my hand. It was heavy, and its edges were like razors.

Below me, the two climbing predators were halfway to the quartz vein. They stopped, their heads cocked, trying to locate me.

I heard their cries again, a sound of intelligent, collective hunger. I reached the forest floor and ran. It was the price of my passage home, bought with terror from a world that was not empty, but filled with a strange and terrible life of its own. My mind was in revolution once more; I had come to this age seeking a simple world of flesh, and had found a complex one of alien intellect. The universe, I was beginning to understand, had no simple answers to offer.

My mind, ever restless, began to turn this idea over, to examine its facets as I examined the crystal in my hand. I had seen them hunt in a pack, with a leader, with tactics. I had heard their calls, which I was certain were a form of language. What if their relationship with this world was more complex than I had imagined? What if the simple duality of the Eloi and the Morlocks, of the idle and the industrious, the eater and the eaten, was a crude and primitive equation compared to the algebra of this new world? I was a fool, I thought, to believe that humanity’s destiny must follow a single, predictable path of divergence. Evolution is not a straight road; it is a branching tree of endless and often bizarre possibilities. I had seen one branch in the year 802,701. The creature leaned down, and for a horrible second I thought it was going to eat the glowing fungus. But it did not. These beings, with their pale skin and large eyes, were clearly creatures of the gloom. The social structure I had theorised for the Morlocks and Eloi was a direct result of the division of labour between the rich and the poor of my own time. But here, the driving force was not social, but environmental. Here was a species that had not simply decayed into masters and slaves, but had adapted, had forged a new and intricate contract with the very world it inhabited. They were not devolved men, degenerated into brutishness. They were a new kind of intelligence, born of hardship, lords of a twilight kingdom, their lives interwoven with a phantasmal, glowing plant. And I, in my ignorance, had blundered into their garden and stolen one of its brightest fruits. My crime was not theft of a mere mineral; it was blasphemy.

I watched them for what must have been an hour, until the last light had faded from the sky and the forest was lit only by the eerie, pulsing glow from the earth and the faint luminescence from the strange flora. The two creatures finished their quiet work, gently covering the glowing mycelium with earth once more, and then, with a few soft clicks to one another, they melted back into the shadows of the forest, their movements swift and silent.

Their departure left me with a profound sense of my own isolation, but also with a sliver of hope. My fear of them had not lessened, for their physical threat was undeniable. But it was now tempered with a kind of intellectual awe. And, more practically, I now had a clue to their nature. They were not omnipresent hunters. They had territories, they had routines. They had a purpose beyond my own destruction. If I could understand their ways, I might be able to move through their world unseen. My survival depended not on my speed, but on my wit. I was a man of science, an observer. I must turn this entire, terrifying world into my laboratory.

My first task was to regain my bearings. During my panicked flight, I had paid no attention to my direction. Now, in the dark, I was utterly lost. I knew that the great house where I had left my Time Machine was situated in a wide valley, near a great river. The gorge from which I had fled was in the hills flanking this valley. I had run away from the gorge, but whether parallel to the valley or deeper into the wild hills, I could not say. I needed a vantage point.

A plan began to form, a desperate but necessary one. I would have to climb. The colossal trees of this forest towered over everything, their canopies forming a second, higher world. If I could ascend one of them, I might be able to see the lay of the land, to spot the silver gleam of the river or the profile of the hills I knew. It was a terrifying prospect. I was exhausted, and my hands were a mess of scrapes and cuts. But to remain in the hollow was simply to postpone the inevitable.

I waited until the strange constellations of this future sky were bright above the canopy. I took another match, my second to last, and by its brief flare I examined the interior of my tree-hollow once more. The moss-like growth seemed to be thicker in the crevices of the wood. It was an observation that gave me an idea. I took out my pocket-knife, the blade now bent and useless for prying rock. With great care, I scraped a handful of the glowing moss and the soft, damp wood it grew upon into my handkerchief. It gave off a dim but steady greenish light, a tiny, living lantern. It was a feeble defence against the vast darkness of this world, but it was my own. It was a product of reason.

Clutching my pathetic lamp and the heavy, precious crystal, I took a deep breath and stepped out of the hollow. The night air was cool on my face. The forest was a maze of black trunks and deeper shadows, filled with the quiet, unnerving sounds of the night. I looked up at the titan tree that had given me shelter. Its smooth, pale bark seemed to rise endlessly into the starry sky. There were no low branches. The ascent would be a sheer climb for at least the first fifty feet. But I had climbed a cliff face for the crystal; surely I could climb a tree for my life.

I put my glowing handkerchief in my breast pocket, where it cast a faint light downwards. I secured the quartz crystal as best I could, though its bulk was a terrible hindrance. Then, taking a deep breath, I placed my torn hands against the strange, bone-like bark and began to climb. The bark was not as smooth as it looked; it was covered in tiny, almost invisible ridges and knobs that gave purchase to my fingers and the worn soles of my boots. I moved slowly, deliberately, testing each hold before trusting my weight to it. The primal fear of falling was a cold knot in my stomach, a far more immediate terror than that of the strange hunters. But as I climbed, foot by painful foot, a strange sense of exaltation began to grow in me. I was no longer fleeing. I was acting. I was pitting my own fragile intellect and tired body against this monstrous, magnificent future. And high above, through the latticework of alien leaves, the ancient, familiar stars of my own dear Earth seemed to be watching. They were my destination, and I would not be defeated here.
