Alistair Blackwood first registered the anomaly as a flicker in his peripheral vision, a fault in the gaslight perhaps. He was a man of precise habits, and the evening ritual of cataloguing his latest acquisitions was not to be disturbed by a faulty mantle. He set down his pen, annoyed. The flicker came again, not from the lamp, but near the tall, curtained window. A distortion, like heat haze over a winter road. “Curious,” he murmured, and pushed his spectacles up his nose. He was a Fellow of the Antiquarian Society; he believed in dust and decay, not in optical tricks.

He stood up, the floorboards groaning a familiar complaint. Then he heard it, a sound like a rustling silk dress, though there was nothing there to rustle. A cold draught, sudden and unnatural, touched the back of his neck. All rational men possess a kernel of ancient fear. The sensation that is named “disquiet” settled upon him. He took a step towards the window, his hand outstretched. “Is someone there?” he called out, his voice firmer than he felt. The silence that answered was thick, expectant.

Then, with a jolt, he saw it. A silver locket, one he had been examining not an hour before, was hovering in the air. It hung suspended three feet from the floor, its chain dangling as if from an invisible neck. He stared, his mouth agape. It was an empty space, a locket resting on nothing, quite impossibly. He would have lunged for it, but a sudden pressure on his shoulder stopped him, and a woman’s voice spoke, impossibly close to his ear.

“Blackwood,” said the Voice.

“What?” said Alistair, stumbling back a step.

“Do not be alarmed,” said the Voice. “I am a little displaced.”

Alistair’s heart hammered against his ribs; he stared at the spot where a person should be. “Displaced,” he repeated, the word tasting like nonsense.

“I am displaced,” the Voice affirmed. “The story you dismissed at the Society meeting last week, the one about temporal fractures. It wasn’t just a theory.” The memory of Professor Albright’s wild lecture surged through Alistair’s mind. He does not seem to have been entirely terrified, not at first. The sheer absurdity of it was a buffer against panic. “That was lunacy,” he said. His most immediate thought was his own strident rebuttal of the Professor’s work. “Are you wearing my locket?” he asked.

“Yes,” said the displaced woman.

“Ah!” said Alistair, and then collected himself. “I say! But this is preposterous. A stage magician’s trick.” He thrust a hand forward, aiming for the locket, and his fingers brushed against the cold, smooth fabric of a glove. He recoiled, a gasp escaping his lips.

“Be still, Blackwood, for pity’s sake! I need your assistance. Stop!” The gloved hand seized his wrist. The grip was shockingly firm. He tried to pull away.

“Blackwood!” cried the Voice. “Blackwood! Be still!” and the grip became a band of iron. A desperate need to break free consumed him. The hand released his wrist only to grab his lapel, and he was violently spun and thrown into his own wing-backed chair. He opened his mouth to shout, and a corner of the velvet curtain was stuffed against his lips. The Unseen woman had him pinned, but his arms were free and he flailed, trying to land a blow on empty air.

“Will you just listen?” said the woman, her body pressing him down despite a kick that connected with what felt like a shin. “By God, you are as stubborn as he said!

“Lie still, you fool!” hissed the Voice in his ear. Alistair struggled for a moment more, then went limp.

“If you make a sound, I’ll knock you senseless with this bookend,” said the woman, removing the curtain. “I am displaced in time. It is not a trick, and it is not a ghost. I am real. And I need your help. You must remember me, Alistair. Annelise, from the observatory?”

“Let me sit up,” said Alistair, his voice hoarse. “I will not move from this chair. And let me think for a moment.” He sat upright and touched his throat.

“I am Annelise, from the Greenwich Observatory, and I have become… untethered from my own time. I am an ordinary woman, a scientist you have met, made unstable.”

“Annelise?” said Alistair.

“Annelise,” the Voice repeated. “A tall woman, with dark hair pinned too tightly, who argued with you about stellar parallax, who spilled tea on your star-charts.”

“I am bewildered,” said Alistair. “My thoughts are a riot. What has this to do with Annelise?”

“I _am_ Annelise.”

Alistair thought. “It’s monstrous,” he said. “But what madness must occur to make a person unstable?”

“It is not madness. It is physics, brutal and absolute.”

“It’s monstrous!” Alistair insisted. “How in heaven’s name, ?”

“It is monstrous enough. But I am weak, and cold, and fading… Dear God! Alistair, you are a reasonable man. Be calm. Fetch me some brandy, and let me sit down.”

Alistair stared as the locket moved towards the fireplace, then saw his Moroccan leather footstool slide across the rug and come to a stop. It groaned, and the leather cushion compressed by an inch or so. He blinked and rubbed his temples. “This is worse than spirits,” he said, and gave a short, choked laugh.

“That’s a good sign. Thank heavens, you are regaining your senses!”

“Or losing them entirely,” said Alistair, and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. “Fetch you some brandy. I must be mad.”

“It felt quite real, did it not? Where is it? If I stand, will I fracture something? _There_! That’s better. Brandy? Yes. Where should I take it from you?”

The footstool creaked and Alistair felt the snifter drawn out of his trembling hand. He released it with a supreme act of will; his every nerve screamed against it. The glass came to rest, hovering six inches above the cushion of the footstool. He watched it, utterly perplexed. “This is… it must be… some new form of mesmerism. You have put a suggestion in my mind.”

“Rubbish,” said the Voice.

“It’s mad.”

“Listen to me.”

“I proved conclusively at the Society,” began Alistair, “that temporal displacement, ”

“Never mind your proofs! I’m freezing,” said the Voice, “and this blasted drawing room is draughty for a woman only partly here.”

“Food?” said Alistair.

The brandy snifter tilted. “Yes,” said Annelise, setting it down with a click. “Have you a spare shawl?”

Alistair muttered something under his breath. He went to a chest by the door and took out a thick woollen shawl. “This one?” he asked. It was pulled from his grasp. It hung slack for a second in mid-air, then draped itself oddly, rose, and settled around the space above the footstool. “Biscuits, cheese, anything would be a blessing,” said the Unseen, her voice muffled by the wool. “And more brandy.”

“Anything. But this is the most demented situation I have ever encountered in my life!” He went to the sideboard to retrieve the decanter and a plate of biscuits. He returned, pulled over a small table, and set them before his guest.

“No need for ceremony,” said his visitor, and a biscuit lifted into the air, vanishing with a sharp crack. “Unstable!” said Alistair, and sank back into his own chair.

“I always feel more substantial after I’ve eaten,” said Annelise, with a full mouth, chewing audibly. “Strange, is it not?”

“I suppose that hand is solid now,” said Alistair.

“Trust me,” said Annelise. “Of all the damnable, impossible things, ”

“Indeed. But it’s peculiar I should be the one you appear to. My first piece of bad luck! I intended to anchor myself in this house. You must tolerate that! It is a terrible inconvenience, this flickering, isn’t it? Almost faded entirely over there. I become more solid as I concentrate, you see. It’s only the present moment I can affect, and only when I am mostly in it. I have been in this room for six hours.”

“But how is it done?” began Alistair, his tone one of pure frustration. “Damn it all! The entire business, it’s illogical from start to finish.”

“Quite logical,” said Annelise. “Perfectly logical.” She reached out and the brandy decanter lifted. Alistair watched the devouring shawl. A sliver of gaslight, passing through a momentary transparency in her left shoulder, cast a strange, wobbling patch of light on the wall behind her. “What was the explosion?” he asked. “How did the accident begin?”

“There was a brilliant fool of a man, my own Professor Albright, curse his ambition!, who tried to breach the barrier. _Has_ breached it.”

“Is _he_ unstable too?”

“No.”

“Well?”

“Can I not have more to eat before I recount the entire disaster? I’m exhausted, in pain. And you want a narrative!”

Alistair stood up. “_You_ didn’t cause the explosion?” he asked.

“Not I,” said his visitor. “Some safety valve Albright overlooked failed catastrophically. The whole laboratory went. They were all caught in it. Curse them! I say, I require something more sustaining than biscuits, Alistair.”

“I’ll see what cook left in the larder,” said Alistair. “Not a great deal, I expect.”

After she had eaten, and she made a surprisingly large meal of cold beef and bread, the Invisible Woman demanded a cigarette. She bit the end off impatiently before Alistair could find a match, and swore when a flake of tobacco fell on the shawl. It was bizarre to see her smoke; her lips and the inside of her mouth became momentarily visible as a shimmering, greyish outline, a three-dimensional sketch made of smoke.

“This blessed habit of smoking!” she said, and puffed greedily. “I am fortunate to have stumbled upon you, Alistair. You have to help me. Fancy finding you of all people! I’m in a dreadful mess, I’ve been frantic, I think. The things I have seen! But we can still set things right. Let me explain, ” She helped herself to more brandy. Alistair got to his feet, looked around his disordered study, and poured a measure for himself. “It’s wild, but I suppose I am allowed to drink.”

“You haven’t a choice.”

a choice.” said the Voice from the scarlet dressing-gown. “Any more than I had.” Alistair stared at his glass of brandy, then drank it in two gulps. The spirit burned his throat but did little to steady the riot in his head. He had a choice, of course; he could run, shout, summon the constable. But the memory of that invisible grip, of the sheer physical power that had thrown him to the bed, arrested the thought. He was a prisoner in his own study. More than that, a strange and terrible curiosity was taking hold of him, an intellectual fascination that was, for the moment, stronger than his fear. He was a scientist. And here, sitting in his chair, was a phenomenon that spat in the face of all known science.

“Explain,” he said, his voice hoarse. He sat down again, slowly, as if afraid of disturbing the air. “You spoke of an explosion. Of your Professor Albright. Of a barrier.”

“Yes,” said the shawl. The smoke from the cigarette coiled upwards, a ghostly pillar in the dim light. “Albright. He was a genius, Alistair, a true, unadulterated genius. And like all such men, he was a hair’s breadth from being a lunatic. You knew him by reputation, I suppose? At Cambridge?”

“The etheric resonance theory,” Alistair said, nodding slowly. “Considered brilliant but unsound. Wildly speculative. He proposed that space was not a void but a fabric, and that different realities, different possibilities, vibrated at different frequencies, like notes on a string. Most of the Royal Society considered it poetry, not physics.”

“It wasn’t poetry,” said the Voice, and there was a note of grim triumph in it. “He was right. He built a machine, Alistair. A resonator. Not to travel to these other worlds, not at first. Just to see. To create a window. He called it the Barrier, the veil between what is, and what might be.” A shudder seemed to pass through the dressing-gown. “For five years we worked. Refining the calculations, building the coils, winding the silver filaments. It was my project as much as his. He was the architect, I was the engineer. He saw the grand vision; I saw the thousand ways it could kill us all.”

Alistair leaned forward, his fear forgotten, his mind alight with a terrible excitement. “So the machine worked?”

“It worked too well. Last night. We activated the final sequence. At first, there was nothing but a low hum, a vibration that you felt in your bones. Then the air in the centre of the room began to… shimmer. Like heat haze over a road. Albright was ecstatic, scribbling notes, shouting about phase alignments. But the shimmer grew. It became a distortion, a lens. Through it, we saw… things.”

“What things?” Alistair’s voice was a whisper.

The cigarette glowed brightly, illuminating a phantom mouth for a moment. “Another room. Like ours, but not. The light was wrong, a sort of violet colour. And the shadows were too sharp. There were people, or shapes like people, moving in it. They were indistinct, like watercolour paintings left in the rain. Albright wanted a clearer view. He began increasing the power, overriding the governors I had installed. The fool! The glorious, brilliant fool!” The voice cracked with a mixture of fury and remembered awe.

“I shouted at him to stop. The hum was a scream by then. The air smelled of ozone and something else, something like cut flowers and burnt metal. The distortion began to pull at the edges of the room, warping the shelves, bending the light. Then came the flash. Not light, not really. It was… a flash of absence. A wave of pure nothing that swept through the laboratory. It wasn’t an explosion of force, but an explosion of reality itself. It didn’t throw me. It unravelled me.”

Alistair stared, speechless. The cigarette dropped from an unseen hand and fell onto the Persian rug, where it smouldered. Instinctively, he stepped forward and crushed it out with his heel.

“I remember the sensation of being pulled apart,” the Voice continued, softer now, exhausted. “Like every atom was being asked to choose which reality it belonged to, and couldn't decide. Then, darkness. When I came to, the laboratory was a ruin. But a quiet ruin. No fire, just… wreckage. Albright was gone. The others, my two assistants, they were gone too. Not dead, Alistair. Gone. Pushed through. The window became a door, and they were swept away by the current.”

“And you?” said Alistair, his mind racing to grasp the impossible physics of it. “Why were you left?”

“I don’t know. Perhaps I was standing in the right place. Or the wrong one. I was… caught in the doorway when it slammed shut. I am not wholly here. I flicker. When I lose concentration, when I am tired or in pain, I begin to fade. I can feel the other place, the violet light, pulling at me. My own body is no longer a constant. It’s a probability. An argument between being and not being.”

She fell silent. The red dressing-gown sat still in the chair. Alistair walked to the window and looked out at the quiet, respectable square, at the gaslights casting their steady, familiar glow. It was all so solid. So real. He turned back to the phenomenon in his study.

“This is insanity,” he said, but without conviction. “A story from a penny dreadful.”

“Is it?” said the Voice, and the sleeve of the dressing-gown lifted. “Look.” The fabric of the cuff seemed to grow translucent. For a sickening moment, Alistair could see the pattern of the wallpaper on the far wall through the red cloth and the arm it contained. Then it solidified again. “My existence is a constant effort. It takes all my will to hold myself together. When I eat, when I feel the substance in my grasp, I become more real. When I touch something solid, like your arm, I anchor myself. That is why I had to come here.”

“To my house? Why me?”

“I have been wandering for hours. A ghost in my own town. The pain from the fall I took escaping the university grounds was making me fade. I was becoming a whisper. I knew I needed help. A rational man. A man of science, who might not run screaming but might, just might, be intrigued enough to listen. I knew your work on quantum mechanics. You, of all people, might understand. I saw the light in your study window. It was a stroke of luck. Or desperation.”

Alistair ran a hand through his hair. His mind, trained in logic and evidence, was a battlefield. One part of him screamed that this was a complex hallucination, a breakdown brought on by overwork. Another part, the larger part, was grappling with the terrifying consistency of her story. It was a monstrous theory, but it explained the monstrous facts before him. The flickering, the invisibility, the disembodied voice.

“You said Albright and the others were ‘pushed through’,” he said, seizing on a detail. “To this other reality. Do you think they are alive?”

“I don’t know what ‘alive’ means there,” she said. “I saw them, Alistair. Just for a second. In the wreckage. They were like after-images burned onto the air. Transparent, shimmering figures, howling without a sound. Then they faded. They are trapped there. And I am trapped between.”

A profound silence filled the room, broken only by the ticking of the mantel clock. The sound was a steady, reassuring anchor in a sea of madness.

“Horrible,” Alistair said at last. “Utterly horrible.”

“Yes,” said the Voice. “And it will happen to me, too. I can feel myself slipping. The resonance of my own body is decaying. Unless I can stabilize it, I will simply… dissolve. Fade away into that violet hell. That is why I need your help.”

“My help? What in God’s name can I do? I’m a theoretical physicist, not a magician!”

“You have a laboratory,” Annelise stated. It was not a question. “In the basement. Well-equipped. I saw the delivery crates from Siemens last month. You have galvanometers, cathode-ray tubes, high-voltage generators.”

“How could you possibly know that?”

“I have been… observing you. For a few weeks. Not with any particular intention, you understand. You were simply a point of scientific interest. Another man working on the fringes. I thought you were a rival. Now I think you are my only hope.” The dressing-gown leaned forward, an empty, imploring gesture. “The theory is sound. My instability is a form of decoherence. I need to create a localized field, a sort of… personal anchor. To reinforce my quantum state and bind me fully to this reality. It would require a resonator, a small one, tuned precisely to my body’s unique frequency.”

Alistair stared at her, his mind reeling. She spoke of the most outlandish impossibilities with the calm, technical language of a laboratory assistant discussing a titration. Decoherence. Quantum states. It was the vocabulary of his own most advanced, most private speculations.

“You’re proposing we build another one of those… those machines?” he said, horrified. “Here? After what you’ve told me?”

“Not a window. An anchor! A small, passive device. To reinforce what is, not to gaze upon what might be. The principles are the same, but the application is inverted. It is the only way. You have the equipment. I have the knowledge. Without me, you are a craftsman with tools but no blueprint. Without you, I am an architect with a plan but no hands, and my foundation is turning to sand. We need each other.”

He paced the room, his footsteps muffled by the thick rug. His study, his sanctuary of reason and order, had been invaded by the impossible. And the impossible was asking for his collaboration. To refuse meant, if her story were true, condemning a human being to a fate worse than any he could imagine. To accept meant stepping into the same world of lunacy that had consumed Professor Albright.

“And if we fail?” he asked, stopping to face the seated robe. “If we build this… anchor… and it does not work? Or it works incorrectly?”

The shawl was still for a long moment. “Then it might push me through entirely,” the Voice said, flat and devoid of emotion. “Or it might destabilize this area completely. It might even open the window again. From this side.”

A cold dread, deeper than any simple fear, settled in Alistair’s stomach. An open window. The violet light. The silent, howling figures. Here. In his house.

“No,” he said. “Absolutely not. The risk is too great.”

“The alternative is my dissolution,” she countered, her voice hardening. “Which I assure you is already happening. Is that a risk you are more comfortable with? To stand by and watch a fellow scientist unravel into nothingness? You, who wrote so eloquently on the moral responsibility of discovery?”

He flinched as if she had struck him. She had read his papers. She knew his work, his ideals. She was using his own mind against him. He felt a surge of anger, then a wave of helplessness. He was trapped. Not just by her physical presence, but by his own nature. He could not, in the end, turn his back on the greatest scientific puzzle ever presented to a man. He could not let her simply fade.

“Very well,” he said, his voice a low groan of surrender. “God help us. Very well. What do you need? What is the first step?”

A palpable sense of relief seemed to come from the dressing-gown. It sat up straighter. “Thank you, Alistair. I knew I was right about you. First, I need paper and a pen. I must draw the schematics for the resonance coils. The winding is the most critical part. And I need… I need more food. This effort, this… talking, has exhausted me. I am growing faint.”

As if to prove her point, the right shoulder of the robe flickered again, becoming hazy and transparent for a brief, terrifying instant. Alistair saw the leather back of his armchair through her. He swallowed hard.

“Stay here,” he said. “Don’t move.” He fetched a notepad and a fountain pen from his desk and placed them on the small table beside her. Then he turned and practically fled the room, heading downstairs to the cold larder, his mind a whirlwind of impossible equations and terrifying possibilities.

He returned with a whole cold chicken, a loaf of bread, and a bottle of claret. He placed them on the table. “Will this suffice?”

“Admirably,” said the Voice. The chicken leg lifted into the air, and a savage tearing sound followed. Alistair tried not to watch. He poured himself another brandy, his hand shaking slightly, and went to his own desk. He tried to think, to impose some order on the chaos that had erupted in his life. He could hear the pen scratching furiously on the paper behind him, a sound of frantic, brilliant creation mixed with the grisly sounds of a disembodied meal.

An hour passed. The chicken was largely demolished. The bottle of claret was half empty. Annelise had filled a dozen pages with complex diagrams and calculations that made Alistair’s head spin. They were beyond anything he had conceived, yet they possessed a terrifying, internal logic.

“The primary difficulty,” she said, her voice stronger now, more solid, “will be sourcing the core filament. It must be a silver-iridium alloy. And it must be drawn to a precise gauge. Nothing you would have in your laboratory.”

“I can order it,” Alistair said, examining a particularly complex diagram for a focusing lens. “It will take a week. Perhaps longer.”

“We do not have a week,” she said, her tone sharp. “I may not have two days. At this rate of decay… no. There is another way. Albright kept a supply in a secure safe in his private study at the university. The main laboratory was destroyed, but his office was in the old wing. It may have survived.”

“Go back there?” Alistair exclaimed. “To the site of the… accident? It will be guarded, sealed off!”

“Which is precisely why you must go,” she said calmly. “I cannot. A watchman would see a floating safe door opening by itself. But you… you are a known figure there. A colleague. You could express your professional condolences, your scientific curiosity. You could gain access.”

“And commit burglary?” he said, aghast. “For a woman who may or may not be a phantasm of my overworked brain?”

“I am real enough to have eaten your chicken,” the Voice retorted dryly. “And real enough to be fading. Look at this.” She held up a hand. The sleeve was full, but at the wrist, where the cuff of the dressing-gown ended, there was nothing. No bandage. No hint of a hand. Just empty air. But then the pen, which had been lying on the table, lifted and spun between invisible fingers. “I am losing cohesion at the extremities. It is accelerating. You must go, Alistair. Tonight.”

Before he could protest further, a sound from outside froze them both. It was a faint, metallic scraping from the street below, followed by a sharp crack, like a stone striking a windowpane. But it was the window of the house opposite. Alistair rushed to his own window, peering through the gap in the heavy curtains.

At first, he saw nothing. The square was empty, bathed in the serene glow of the gaslights. Then he saw it. A flicker. Near the base of the lamppost across the street. It was like the effect Annelise had described, a patch of air that seemed to shimmer and distort, a momentary blurring of the ironwork and the cobblestones behind it. It was there for a second, a patch of wrongness in the fabric of the night, and then it was gone.

“What was that?” he whispered, his heart pounding.

He felt a sudden chill in the room, a draft that had nothing to do with the window. He turned. Annelise, or the dressing-gown she inhabited, was standing, rigid.

“He’s back,” she breathed, and her voice was filled with a new and dreadful terror. “One of them. He’s come back.”

“One of who? Albright?”

“No. Not Albright,” she whispered. “Wren. My assistant. The ambitious one. The one who watched Albright with such… hunger. He must have found a way back through. And he knows, Alistair. He knows about the anchor. He knows it’s the only way to become whole.”

The scraping sound came again, closer this time. From the railings of his own house. Alistair’s blood ran cold. The impossible horror in his study was one thing. But now, the impossible horror was outside, and it was trying to get in.

“He will try the door first,” Annelise’s voice was a ragged whisper, the dressing-gown shrinking in on itself. “He was always methodical. The windows are a last resort. Too much noise.” Alistair backed away from the window, his gaze fixed on the stout oak door of his study. He felt a ridiculous impulse to pile furniture against it, like a character in a cheap melodrama. But what good was a barricade to a man who could walk through walls? No, that was ghosts. This was different. This was, somehow, worse. “Can he… can he not just become visible to open a door?” he stammered, the logic of it fighting against the sheer insanity. “It’s not a coat you can take on and off!” the Voice snapped, its terror sharpening into irritation. “The process is total. Irreversible, without the anchor. To become visible would be to… die. The cellular structure would destabilise. He is as trapped as I am.” The scraping stopped. A profound silence descended on the house, a silence more terrifying than the noise it replaced. Alistair found he was holding his breath. He could hear the frantic drumming of his own heart, the rustle of the silk dressing-gown as its unseen occupant shifted. Then came a new sound. A tiny, metallic click from the ground floor. From the front door. “The lock,” Alistair breathed. “But how? My keys are…” “He doesn’t need keys,” Annelise whispered. “He was always clever with his hands. A piece of wire… a hairpin… he boasted he could open any lock in the university.” Alistair stared at the study door, his mind racing. He was on the first floor. The intruder was at the front door. He had seconds, perhaps a minute. He spun around, his eyes scanning the room for a weapon. A heavy brass paperweight. A letter opener, pitifully small. The fireplace poker. Yes, the poker. He snatched it from its stand, its weight a small, cold comfort in his hand. “Stay here,” he commanded the dressing-gown. “And be silent.” He crept to the study door, easing it open a crack. The landing was dark, lit only by a sliver of moonlight from the tall window at its end. He could hear nothing but the ancient groans of the house. And then, the distinct, unmistakable sound of the front door swinging slowly open on its well-oiled hinges. A draft of cold night air snaked up the staircase. Alistair’s knuckles were white on the poker. He was a man of science, a man of reasoned debate and empirical evidence. He was about to engage in a brawl with a scientific impossibility in his own hallway. The sheer, gibbering lunacy of it all threatened to overwhelm him. He took a steadying breath and stepped out onto the landing. Below, in the hall, a patch of moonlight on the floor was suddenly interrupted. A shadow fell where no man stood. Alistair saw the polished floorboards depress slightly, heard the faintest creak. Someone was on the stairs. Coming up. He flattened himself against the wall, the poker held ready. He tried to track the ascent by sound; a soft footfall, a whisper of displaced air. The presence was halfway up the stairs now, moving with a confident, deliberate slowness that was unnerving. He was being hunted. “Annelise,” a new voice called out, a man’s voice, low and resonant but with a cruel, mocking edge. It was not loud, but it filled the house. “I know you’re in here. Did you really think the old fool’s town house was a safe place to hide? I could smell your perfume on the air a street away. Fading, aren’t you? I can feel it. The loose ends of you, fraying into nothing.” The voice was almost at the top of the stairs. Alistair could feel a change in the air pressure, a strange cold spot moving past him. He swung the poker with all his might at the space where a man’s head ought to be. There was a dull thud, not of metal on bone, but of metal on something softer, yielding. A grunt of pain. “Ugh! Clever, Professor!” the voice, Wren’s voice, hissed right beside his ear. An invisible force slammed into him, sending him stumbling back against the wall. The poker was wrenched from his grasp, clattering loudly as it was thrown down the hallway. “But not clever enough!” Alistair scrambled backwards, his hands up to defend himself. He was shoved, hard, and tripped over his own feet, crashing into a small side table that went over with a splintering crash. A vase of dried flowers shattered on the floor. A heavy weight pressed down on his chest, and unseen hands gripped the lapels of his jacket. He could feel hot, sour breath on his face. “The anchor! Albright’s notes! She brought them to you, didn’t she? The sentimental fool.” “I don’t know what you’re talking about!” Alistair gasped, struggling for air. “Liar!” The grip tightened, lifting him half off the floor. “I followed her from the ruins. Now, where is it?” Suddenly, the study door flew open. “Leave him alone, Wren!” It was Annelise, her voice trembling but defiant. The scarlet dressing-gown stood in the doorway, its empty sleeves extended. Wren laughed, a genuinely amused, chilling sound. The pressure on Alistair’s chest vanished. He scrambled away, crab-like, towards the stairs, his chest heaving. “I could have killed you a dozen times since I came into this room,” the Voice continued, rising in volume and fury. “I could kill you now, so easily. A touch here,”, Kemp felt an icy pressure on his temple, “a twist there. Who would ever know? An unexplained death. A man of forty found dead in his study. Heart failure, the coroner would say. But I don’t want to kill you, Kemp. I want you to help me. You will help me. Won’t you?”

Kemp’s throat was dry. He could feel the cold spot on his temple where the invisible finger had touched him. The reality of his position was closing in on him like the walls of a tomb. He was alone, locked in a room with an unseen, homicidal maniac who held all the power. His mind raced, searching for some way out, some strategy. He could not fight him. He could not reason with him. So he must feign agreement. He must play for time.

“Yes,” Kemp whispered, his eyes fixed on the empty air. “Yes, of course. You’re right. It was just... a shock.”

The tension in the room seemed to lessen. The dressing-gown relaxed, sinking back into the armchair. “Good,” said the Voice. “Good. I knew you’d see sense. A man like you... you couldn’t fail to see the possibilities.” It gave a dry, invisible chuckle. “Now, about those confederate duties. First, I need to sleep. Somewhere safe. I haven’t slept in a bed for three days. And then, I need my books. My notes. They are the key to everything. The key to reversing the process, should I ever wish to. And the key to making more. Imagine it, Kemp! An army of us!”

Kemp felt a fresh wave of nausea. “Your books? Where are they?” he asked, forcing his voice to sound steady, even interested.

“In a little inn, in a village called Iping,” said the Invisible Man. “Wrapped in a bundle. A fat, meddlesome landlady has them. A Mrs. Hall. We shall have to retrieve them. That will be your first task. You will go there for me.”

“And you?” Kemp asked, a plan, desperate and half-formed, beginning to stir in his brain.

“I shall stay here. I shall rest. I shall heal,” said the Voice. “This is my headquarters now, Kemp. Our headquarters.” The smoking cigar, which had been forgotten, rose again into the air, and a fresh puff of smoke traced the ghastly outline of a lung. “First thing in the morning, you will write a note. To a certain Colonel Adye, the chief of the Burdock police. You will tell him you have a story for him. A very important story. You will tell him to come here. Alone.”

Kemp stared, bewildered. “The police? Why?”

The invisible head tilted, and the smoke swirled. “Because, my dear, sensible Kemp,” said the Voice, with a tone of silky menace, “a Reign of Terror, as some might call it, is best begun with a demonstration. And a chief of police makes a very fine demonstration indeed.”

Kemp felt the blood run cold in his veins. He looked from the devouring dressing-gown to the shattered glass on the floor, to the locked door of his study. He was a prisoner. But he was also a biologist. He knew that even the most formidable predator has a weakness. He had to find Griffin’s. He had to stay alive long enough to find it.

“Of course,” Kemp said, slowly moving to his desk and sitting down. “Whatever you say, Griffin. A demonstration.” He picked up a pen, his hand shaking only slightly. He looked at the blank paper before him. The night was young, and it was already full of horrors. But the morning, he now realised with a certainty that was as cold and hard as steel, was going to be infinitely worse. He began to think, not of escape, but of war.

His mind raced through the implications. Adye. A good man, but a policeman, not a soldier prepared for this kind of insanity. An ambush. A public execution by an invisible hand. It was monstrous. It was also a clue. Griffin wanted an audience. He wanted fear. He craved the power he spoke of, not just to have it, but to be seen having it, or rather, to have its effects seen. Pride, then. Arrogance. That was the first weakness.

“What shall I write, precisely?” Kemp asked, his tone now one of careful subservience. He dipped the pen in the inkwell.

“Tell him,” began the Voice, and there was a lazy satisfaction in it now, the sound of a beast fed and content for the moment. “Tell him that Dr. Kemp has made a discovery of national importance. Tell him the safety of the entire county depends upon his discretion. Insist that he comes to this house, at eleven o’clock sharp. And that he brings no one. Make it sound urgent, mysterious. You’re a man of letters; you can manage the tone.”

Kemp began to write, his pen scratching in the profound silence. He wrote the lines as instructed, but his brain was working on a different problem. Griffin was wounded, tired, overconfident. He was also, for all his bluster, just a man. A man without clothes in a chilly room. A man who needed to eat, and sleep, and who bled when he was cut. A man whose very invisibility was, in its way, a prison. He could not interact with the world without giving himself away. He could not carry money without it appearing to float. He could not eat in public. He was a phantom, and a phantom can be fought.

“Is that all?” Kemp asked, after a moment.

“Yes. For now.” The dressing-gown stirred again. “Now... I must sleep. This room will do. Lock the door from the inside. Put the key on the mantelpiece. I need to know I will not be disturbed. I trust you not to try anything foolish, Kemp. I can hear a pin drop. And I sleep very lightly.”

“I understand,” said Kemp. He stood up, folded the note, and placed it on his desk. He walked to the door, turned the key in the lock, the sound unnaturally loud. He then crossed the room and placed the heavy brass key on the marble mantelpiece, next to a bronze clock.

The dressing-gown had shed itself onto the floor in a scarlet heap. Kemp could hear the faint sound of the basket chair creaking again, and then the sigh of the mattress on his own bed as an unseen weight settled upon it. He stood frozen, a sentry in his own prison. He listened to the breathing, a soft, regular rhythm in the darkness. He thought of the note on the desk. He thought of Colonel Adye walking into this appalling trap.

No. He could not allow it.

He waited. He counted the ticks of the clock on the mantelpiece. One minute. Two. The breathing of the Invisible Man deepened, became heavier. Was he truly asleep? Or was this another test? Kemp’s heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drum against the slow, steady beat of the clock. He took a single, silent step towards his desk. The breathing from the bed did not change. He took another.

He reached the desk. His hand, trembling, reached for the note. He could not destroy it; Griffin would know. But he could add to it. A postscript. A warning. He picked up the pen again, his every movement agonizingly slow. He uncapped it. The tiny click seemed like a gunshot in the silent room. He held his breath. The rhythmic breathing from the bed continued, unbroken.

He bent over the paper. In tiny, cramped letters, at the very bottom of the page, he added a new message. He did not need many words.

_He is here. Invisible. Armed. Do not come alone. Bring everyone._

He blotted the ink with a piece of scrap paper, his hand shaking so much he almost smeared the words. He refolded the note, his fingers clumsy with adrenaline. He placed it back where it had been. He had done it. He had declared war.

A floorboard creaked behind him.

Kemp froze, his blood turning to ice.

“What,” said the Voice, no longer from the bed but from a point just behind his shoulder, a soft, conversational, and utterly terrifying whisper, “are you doing, Kemp?”

Kemp’s blood did not turn to ice; it seemed to evaporate entirely, leaving him a hollow, weightless shell. His heart, which had been hammering, stopped. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, a sound as loud and deliberate as a blacksmith’s hammer on an anvil. The whisper had not been a product of his imagination. It was real, a current of air against his neck, laden with the faint scent of whiskey and cigar smoke. He did not turn. He could not. His spine was a rod of iron, his limbs disconnected from his will.

“I asked you a question, Kemp,” the Voice said, and this time there was a thread of something else in it. Not anger, not yet. It was a sort of weary, disappointed curiosity, the tone a schoolmaster might use for a promising pupil caught cheating. A ghostly pressure, the faintest touch of invisible fingers, rested on his shoulder. “What are you doing?”

Kemp’s mouth worked, but no sound came. His mind was a maelstrom of denial and stark, bottomless terror. He had been so clever, so careful. He had counted the breaths. He had listened for the slightest stir. And all the while, the Invisible Man had been standing, watching, in perfect silence.

“Writing,” Kemp croaked, the word a dry rasp in his throat.

“Yes. I can see that,” the Voice replied, the pressure on his shoulder increasing slightly, becoming a grip. “I am not blind, whatever else I am. The question was not an inquiry into the general activity. The question was specific. What, precisely, were you writing, Kemp, when you thought I was asleep?”

A frantic, hopeless lie formed in Kemp’s brain. An addendum to his own scientific notes. A sudden thought on the properties of refraction. But he knew, with a dreadful certainty, that any lie would be instantly detected. The being behind him was not a man to be trifled with. He was a disembodied intelligence, and his paranoia, Kemp now realised, was a finely honed instrument of survival.

“Answer me,” said the Voice, and now the anger was there, a low, guttural vibration. The grip tightened, the fingers digging into the muscle of his shoulder with surprising strength. Kemp winced. “Do not play me for a fool. I have had a sufficiency of fools for one day.”

“It was… a note,” Kemp stammered, his eyes fixed on the folded paper on his desk. It seemed to pulse with a malevolent light.

“A note,” the Invisible Man repeated softly. “How thoughtful. And to whom were you writing this note, in the dead of night, with such admirable stealth?” He was toying with him. He knew. Of course he knew.

An unseen hand darted past Kemp’s face and snatched the paper from the desk. Kemp flinched back with a cry, stumbling against the edge of the desk. The note hung in mid-air for a moment, a pale rectangle suspended in the gloom, before it began to unfold itself. Kemp watched, mesmerised by the sheer horror of it. Folds flattened, creases opened, all accomplished by no visible agency.

There was a profound silence in the room, broken only by the frantic ticking of the clock and Kemp’s own ragged breathing. He could feel the presence of the Invisible Man, a zone of focused stillness, as he read. The silence stretched, becoming heavier, more oppressive than any shout. Kemp braced himself for the explosion, for the frantic violence he had witnessed earlier.

It did not come.

“‘He is here,’” the Voice read aloud, each word falling into the silence like a stone into a deep well. The tone was unnervingly calm, almost academic. “‘Invisible. Armed. Do not come alone. Bring everyone.’”

Another silence. Kemp could picture the unseen head turning from the note towards him. He felt a gaze upon him that was more chilling than any physical stare.

“‘Bring everyone,’” Griffin repeated, with a soft, dreadful chuckle that had no humour in it whatever. It was the sound of something breaking. “Everyone. You were going to betray me, Kemp. After I came to you. After I trusted you. You, a man of science. I thought you, of all people, would understand.”

“Understand?” Kemp burst out, finding his voice in a surge of indignant terror. “Understand what? A reign of terror? You speak of reason and then behave like a mad dog! You assaulted me! You threw me on my bed!”

“A misunderstanding!” the Invisible Man snapped, the paper crumpling in an invisible fist. “A necessary precaution against a ‘frantic rustic’! And what is my reward? Treachery. The prosaic, predictable treachery of the common man. I offer you a partnership in the greatest revolution in human history, and you scrabble for a pen to call the police. You are no better than the cart-driver with the whip, no better than the fool who shot at me. You see something you do not understand, and your only instinct is to form a mob and destroy it.”

“You are not a revolution,” Kemp retorted, his fear giving way to a hot, righteous anger. “You are a monster! You speak of your suffering, but what of the people you have terrorised, the man you have nearly killed?”

“An unfortunate necessity!” the Voice roared, and suddenly the dressing-gown on the chair was snatched up and flung across the room, striking a bookshelf with a heavy thud. “That confederate, as you call him, that little grubbing tramp, Marvel, he stole my life’s work! My notebooks! Everything! All the formulae, all the procedures, all the means to reverse the process! Do you think I enjoy this state? This damnable, cold, lonely state? I am a man, Kemp! I want to be seen again! And you, you with your comfortable life and your well-fed principles, you would have me hunted down and caged like an animal before I can recover what is mine!”

He was pacing now, a frantic, unseen circuit around the room. Kemp could hear the floorboards creaking in different places, the swish of air, the furious, heavy breathing.

“And you were my one hope,” the Voice continued, the rage subsiding into a tone of utter desolation that was somehow more frightening. “I saw the lights in your house. I knew you. A man of intellect. A man who could grasp the magnitude of it. And what do you do? ‘Bring everyone.’ You would have brought Adye, and his dull-witted constables. They would have come with their handcuffs and their foolish, earnest faces. And what would they have done, Kemp? What do you do with an Invisible Man who does not wish to be caught?”

The question hung in the air. Kemp had no answer.

“I’ll tell you what would have happened,” Griffin said, his voice now a low, conspiratorial whisper. He had stopped pacing. Kemp sensed he was very close again. “There would have been a great deal of shouting. A great deal of confusion. Perhaps some foolish hero would have tried to grapple with the air. And I, Kemp, I would have had to kill him. And then another. And another. Your little note, your little act of civic duty, would have signed the death warrants of a dozen men. All because you lack imagination.”

The crumpled note floated back towards Kemp and was thrust against his chest. “Take it.”

Kemp’s hand closed on it automatically. The paper was creased and damp.

“Now, Dr. Kemp,” the Invisible Man said, with a new and terrible calmness. “We are going to have a new understanding. The partnership I proposed is clearly beyond your moral courage. You are not a fellow revolutionary. You are a tool. And I shall use you.”

“What are you going to do?” Kemp asked, his throat dry.

“First,” said the Voice from beside the fireplace, “we are going to destroy the evidence of your little treason. Go on. Eat it.”

Kemp stared. “What?”

“You heard me. Eat the note. I want to see you chew and swallow every last word of your betrayal. I want to know it is gone. It will be a sacrament, Kemp. The first sacrament of your new service to me. Eat it.”

“This is insane!” Kemp protested, his mind rebelling at the sheer, grotesque humiliation of the demand. “I will not!”

In a flash, his arms were seized from behind and twisted painfully behind his back. The grip was like iron. He was forced to his knees. His head was pulled back by an invisible hand tangled in his hair, exposing his throat. The crumpled ball of paper was pushed against his lips.

“I can break your jaw, Kemp,” the Voice hissed in his ear. “I can force it open. I can shove this paper down your throat until you choke on it. Or, you can do it yourself, and we can move on to more productive matters. The choice is, as they say, yours. But do choose quickly. My patience is quite worn out.”

Tears of pain and fury pricked Kemp’s eyes. He could feel the rough texture of the paper against his teeth. The smell of the ink filled his nostrils. He was utterly, completely helpless. He thought of the alternative, the choking, the brutal force. With a sob of sheer degradation, he opened his mouth. The paper was pushed inside.

“Chew,” commanded the Voice.

Kemp chewed. The paper was pulpy, tasteless, sticking to the roof of his mouth. The ink had a bitter, chemical tang. He retched, but the hand in his hair tightened, forcing his head back.

“Swallow.”

With a convulsive gulp, he forced the wretched morsel down his throat. It felt like swallowing gravel. The hands released him. He collapsed forward onto the floor, coughing and gasping, the taste of paper and shame coating his tongue.

He lay there for a minute or more, his body trembling with reaction. Above him, he heard the sound of the whiskey bottle being lifted from the table, and the glug of a long pour. The basket chair creaked as Griffin sat down.

“There,” said the Invisible Man, his voice restored to that unnerving, conversational tone. “Was that so difficult? Now we have no secrets, you and I. Now we can begin.”

Kemp slowly, painfully, pushed himself up into a sitting position on the floor, leaning against the leg of his desk. He looked at the empty space in the basket chair where his tormentor sat. The scarlet dressing-gown was still a heap by the bookshelf. All he could see was the gentle depression in the cushion.

“Begin what?” he asked, his voice a hoarse whisper.

“My work,” said Griffin. “And your part in it. My plans have been… inconvenienced. I had intended to lie low here, to use your resources to prepare a proper headquarters. To work on the reversal. But your little note has accelerated the timeline. The authorities will be looking for me. Adye will be sniffing around. They cannot know I am here. Therefore, you must provide me with a new sanctuary.”

“A new sanctuary? Where?”

“Not here. This house is compromised. Your loyalty is… questionable. No, I need a place where I am master. Where the locals are simple enough not to ask questions. I had such a place, in Iping. A shabby little room at an inn. It was nearly perfect, until I was hounded out. I shall establish another. And you will help me.”

“How?” Kemp asked, his mind numb.

“You are a man of property, are you not, Kemp?” the Voice mused. “You have this fine house. Do you not own some other, more rustic dwelling? A country cottage? A shooting lodge? Something isolated, away from prying eyes?”

Kemp thought of the little bungalow he owned, a few miles out of Burdock, on the edge of the downs. He had bought it as a quiet retreat for writing, but had rarely used it. It was utterly isolated. The perfect prison. The perfect lair.

“There is… a place,” he admitted reluctantly. “A bungalow. On the downs.”

“Excellent!” The chair creaked with satisfaction. “Describe it.”

Kemp described it in dull, mechanical tones. The two bedrooms, the main living area, the kitchen with its old iron stove. The lack of a telephone. The nearest neighbour being over a mile away.

“Perfect,” Griffin said. “Absolutely perfect. It will be the nerve centre of my new enterprise. From there, I can begin the first phase.”

“The first phase?” Kemp echoed dully.

“The Reign of Terror,” said Griffin, as if discussing a business proposal. “I have been thinking. My previous methods were too haphazard. Frightening barmaids and stealing from vicarages. It is beneath me. I need to be systematic. I need to establish my power. I will declare myself. Not as a man, but as a force. I will issue decrees. The town of Burdock will be my first subject. I will levy taxes. Invisible taxes, collected by an invisible hand. Those who refuse will be made examples of. Not killed, not at first. Just… punished. Imagine, Kemp! A mysterious plague of breakages. Objects flying from shelves. Windows shattering. Fires starting from nowhere. The sheer, delicious panic. They will learn to obey.”

He was leaning forward now; Kemp could hear the intensity in his voice, the passion of a fanatic outlining his utopia.

“They will call me a ghost, a devil. Let them. I will be more real than their own government. And from that position of power, I can negotiate. I can demand my notebooks back. I can demand resources, a laboratory. I can compel the world’s greatest minds to work on my problem. And you, Kemp. You will be my visible agent. My mouthpiece.”

He stood up, the chair creaking his absence. “Your first task will be tomorrow. You will go to this bungalow. You will take supplies. Food, bedding, books. My books. You will stock it as if for a long stay. You will tell anyone who asks that you are preparing it for a tenant. A reclusive tenant who wishes to be left alone.”

He was pacing again, a predator mapping out its territory.

“Then you will begin acquiring things for me from your laboratory. Certain chemicals. Glassware. A microscope. I will give you a list. We must begin the work again, from scratch if need be. The terror is a means to an end, Kemp. The end is my freedom. My return.”

He stopped in front of Kemp. An unseen hand reached down and hauled him to his feet as if he were a doll.

“Do you understand your role now?” the Voice asked, low and intense. “You are not my partner. You are my slave. Your house, your money, your reputation, your scientific knowledge, they are all mine now. And if you even think of betraying me again… if you so much as hesitate in an order… I will not be so lenient. I will not make you eat paper. I will begin with your fingernails, I think. Or perhaps your eyes. I have a great deal of anatomical knowledge, Kemp. I can cause a man a world of pain without killing him. Do we have an understanding?”

Kemp looked into the empty air where the face of his captor should be, and in the depths of his humiliation and terror, a new, cold resolve began to form. He was a prisoner, yes. A slave, perhaps. But he was not broken. He was a man of science. And science was about observation, about understanding weaknesses. Griffin had weaknesses. His temper. His arrogance. His absolute dependence on the visible world.

“Yes,” Kemp said, his voice steady. “I understand.”

“Good,” said the Invisible Man. “Now. Go and fetch me another bottle of whiskey. And then you will sit at that desk and write out a list of everything in your chemical store. The Reign of Terror begins with a proper inventory.”

“You heard me,” said the Invisible Man. The scarlet dressing-gown hitched itself up and crossed to the drinks table. A bottle neck was tilted, and for a moment the stream of golden-brown liquid was visible before it vanished into an unseen gullet. “Whiskey first,” said the Voice. “A man cannot properly inaugurate a new epoch on a dry throat. Then the inventory.” Kemp stood frozen for a second, a man confronted with a demand so mundane it bordered on the surreal. The Reign of Terror. An inventory. His mind, still reeling from the physical assault and the sheer impossibility of his situation, latched onto the task as a drowning man might grasp a piece of driftwood. It was, at least, a concrete action in a world that had dissolved into nightmare. “Very well,” he said, his own voice sounding distant and hollow in his ears. He walked over to his writing desk, a heavy piece of mahogany that had been his father’s, and sat down. He felt the familiar comfort of the worn leather seat, the solidity of the wood beneath his hands. For a moment, he could almost pretend this was a normal evening, that he was about to work on his latest paper. He pulled a fresh sheet of foolscap towards him and dipped his pen in the inkwell. The scratch of the nib was unnervingly loud in the silent room. “Where do you want me to begin?” he asked, not looking up. “Acids,” said the Voice, from the direction of the armchair. The dressing-gown had settled back into it, a grotesque parody of a lounging gentleman. “List all your acids. Strengths and quantities. Be precise, Kemp. My entire future, and by extension your continued good health, may depend on the accuracy of this list.” Kemp began to write. Nitric Acid. Sulphuric. Hydrochloric. He listed them methodically, his scientific training taking over, providing a thin wall of procedure between himself and the gibbering panic that threatened to overwhelm him. He could hear the faint, rhythmic slosh of whiskey being swirled in a glass. “You are a doctor of medicine, Kemp,” the Voice mused, breaking the silence. “Not a proper chemist. I never understood that. You had the brain for it. Why stoop to patching up grocers and hysterical women?” “It is a respectable and useful profession,” Kemp said, his pen pausing. “Useful!” The word was a snarl. “So is bricklaying! Science is not about being ‘useful’ in that paltry, bourgeois sense. It is about power! It is about tearing the veil from the face of Nature and forcing her to give up her secrets! You chose to be a servant, Kemp. I chose to be a master.” The glass was set down with a sharp click. “What else? After the acids. The alkalis.” Kemp’s hand trembled slightly as he wrote. Caustic Potash. Soda. He was acutely aware of the invisible presence, the sheer physical fact of Griffin in the room. He could smell the whiskey on his breath, the faint, metallic scent of blood that still clung to the air. Every creak of the floorboards, every rustle of the dressing-gown, sent a fresh wave of dread through him. He was a prisoner in his own home, a scribe to a madman. “Your notes,” Kemp said, forcing himself to speak. “You mentioned your notes. The ones that were stolen. They contain the full process?” “Everything!” The voice was sharp, suspicious. “Why do you ask?” “Scientific curiosity,” Kemp said, trying to keep his tone level. “This is… unprecedented. You say it is not magic. A process. My mind struggles to grasp it. A complete refractive index of air? In every cell? The blood, the nerves, the very bones?” “Ah, the blood!” The voice held a note of bitter triumph. “That was the devil of it. It took me weeks. I thought it had beaten me. The red colouring matter, the hæmoglobin. It would not decolourise. It would not! I had to replace the blood. An entire transfusion, Kemp, with a saline solution and a dozen other reagents, done on myself, by myself, while the process was already underway! Do you have any idea of the pain? The risk?” “It is incredible,” Kemp breathed, and for a moment, his fear was eclipsed by a flash of genuine scientific awe. “And you kept notes of all this?” “Of course I kept notes! Meticulous notes! Three big, leather-bound books full of them. Equations, procedures, failures. The work of years. And that snivelling little worm, Marvel, has them. He ran off with them, and my money, the little traitor.” The dressing-gown stood up abruptly. “But I will get them back. Oh, yes. When I have established my rule, when the whole country is shivering at the mention of my name, I will find Mr. Marvel. And I will have my books. And I will have a very, very long chat with him about loyalty.” The unseen form began to pace again, a restless, caged energy that filled the room. “Continue with the list, Kemp. I want to know everything you have. Every salt, every metal, every scrap of reagent. We must see what we can recreate from your pitiful little stores.” For the next hour, Kemp wrote. He filled one page, then another, his hand cramping. He listed powders and crystals, solutions and distillates. And all the while, Griffin prowled the room, muttering to himself, drinking, and occasionally firing a sharp question at Kemp. “Strychnine? You have strychnine?” “A small amount. For… experiments on nervous tissue.” “Excellent! We may find a use for that. A most persuasive chemical, strychnine. What about potassium cyanide?” “No,” said Kemp, a cold knot tightening in his stomach. “I have no call for that.” “A pity. Still, one cannot have everything.” The pacing stopped behind Kemp’s chair. He felt a sudden chill, the unnerving proximity of the unseen man. He could feel breath on the back of his neck. “You are doing well, Kemp,” the Voice whispered, a conspiratorial, almost friendly tone that was more terrifying than the shouting. “You are being sensible. I knew you had it in you. You see the logic of the situation. You see the power. We are men of science, you and I. We are above the common herd. We should have been partners from the start.” A heavy, invisible hand fell on his shoulder. Kemp flinched violently, a jolt running through his entire body. “Steady,” said the Voice, the grip tightening. “You are still jumpy. You will get used to it.” Suddenly, there was a sharp rap at the front door. Both men froze. The sound, an ordinary, domestic interruption, was an explosion in the tense silence of the study. Kemp’s heart hammered against his ribs. The hand on his shoulder squeezed painfully. “Who is that?” Griffin hissed, his voice a blade of ice. “I don’t know,” Kemp whispered, his throat dry. “It is past eleven. No one should be calling.” “You are not to answer it,” Griffin commanded. “They will go away.” The rapping came again, louder this time, more insistent. A voice called out. “Dr. Kemp? A moment, if you please! From the station.” “The police,” Kemp mouthed, his eyes wide with terror. The hand was gone from his shoulder. He heard a swift, silent movement towards the window. The curtain was tweaked aside. “It is one constable,” the Voice reported, low and tense. “Alone. What does he want?” “I don’t know,” Kemp repeated, feeling his world shrinking to the space between the constable at the door and the invisible murderer at the window. “If I don’t answer, he will become suspicious. With all the fuss in the town today…” “What fuss?” “About you! An Invisible Man! There have been sightings, incidents. The whole town is in an uproar. A man was shot at this afternoon.” “That fool Wicksteed,” Griffin muttered. “He got in the way. It was not my fault.” “The constable will have been told to check on any strange occurrences. My lights are on. If I do not answer, he may try to force the door. He may fetch help.” There was a moment of tense silence, broken only by the third and most impatient summons from the door. “Dr. Kemp! Are you in there?” Kemp looked from the door to the empty space where Griffin stood. He saw his chance, a sliver of possibility so thin and sharp it made him dizzy. If he could get to the door. If he could speak to the constable. One word. One shout. But the invisible hand was already on his arm, the grip like iron. “You will go down,” Griffin whispered, his voice dangerously calm. “You will open the door no more than a crack. You will tell him you were engrossed in your work and did not hear him. You will be polite, you will be bored, and you will get rid of him. If you give any sign, Kemp, any sign at all that I am here, I will break your neck before he can take two steps up the path. And then I will kill him. Do you understand me?” Kemp nodded, unable to speak. The grip on his arm propelled him from the chair and towards the study door. “And Kemp,” the Voice added, a final, chilling threat. “I will be right behind you.” He walked out of the study, his legs feeling like lead. He was aware of the faint displacement of air behind him, the almost inaudible footfalls on the runner in the hall. It was like being escorted to the gallows by a ghost. He reached the front door and paused, his hand hovering over the bolt. He could hear the constable shifting his weight on the gravel outside. This was it. The moment of decision. His mind raced. He could shout, scream for help. Griffin might kill him, but he would be exposed. The reign of terror would end before it began. But the memory of that grip, the raw, brutal strength that had thrown him across the room, was fresh and vivid. And the promise to kill the constable… Kemp was no hero. Kemp stared, horrified. This was not the talk of a desperate fugitive. This was the talk of a burgeoning tyrant. The scientific genius had curdled into a monstrous megalomania. He was not just a man who had made himself invisible; he had made himself a god in his own mind.

“You’re talking about murder,” Kemp said, his voice a whisper.

“I am talking about making a point,” the Voice said coldly. “The world must learn to fear me. Then, it will obey me. And you, Kemp, you will be my visible partner. My agent. My hand in the world of men. You have the house, the reputation, the resources. I have the power. Together, we are invincible.”

Kemp’s mind raced. He had to keep him talking. He had to understand the depths of this madness. “But the books… if this Marvel has them, he could sell them. Someone else could learn the secret.”

“No!” The Voice was sharp. “Never. The process is complex. It is written in a cipher of my own devising, mixed with Greek and Russian script. No one could decipher it without my key. No one. The books are useless to them. But they are everything to me. They are the record. And they are the way back.”

“The way back?” A spark of hope, faint and treacherous, ignited in Kemp’s chest. “You can reverse it?”

There was a long pause. The cigar smoke eddied and thinned. “The theory is there,” Griffin said, his tone suddenly subdued, almost sullen. “It requires certain reagents. A process of reintegration. But I have not had the chance to test it. I have been too busy… surviving. It is not as glorious as it sounds, Kemp. This invisibility. I am cold. I am naked. I cannot eat or drink in public. Every stray dog is a menace. Dust and mud reveal my outline. Rain makes me a shimmering phantom. It is a cursed gift. I need my books to become whole again. But first… first I will have my revenge. And my reign.”

He stood up. Kemp flinched as the scarlet dressing-gown rose to its full height, a headless, shapeless terror. The slippers padded across the floor, the sound a soft, rhythmic counterpoint to the ticking clock.

“Tomorrow,” the Invisible Man declared, pacing back and forth, “you will go out. You will walk to the post-office. You will ask questions. You will listen to the gossip. Find out what is known about me. Find out where the police are looking. You will be my eyes and ears. And you will purchase for me a few items. A revolver, for one. A good, heavy calibre. And a box of cartridges.”

“A revolver?” Kemp’s blood ran cold.

“One must be prepared for a hostile world,” said Griffin calmly. “And I need clothes that are less conspicuous than this absurd garment. A hat. A pair of dark glasses. Gloves. The usual accoutrements of the invisible. You will procure these things for me. You will not draw attention to yourself. You will be a model of bourgeois propriety.”

“And if I refuse?” Kemp asked, the words forced from him.

The pacing stopped. The dressing-gown turned slowly to face him. An empty sleeve lifted and pointed, a limp, accusatory finger. “Then I will make an example of you instead of Marvel. But it will not be a quick death, Kemp. I know you. I know your work. I know your ambitions. I will begin by destroying your laboratory. All your precious research, all your meticulous notes, turned to ash and broken glass. Then I will burn your house down around you. And when you run out into the street, I will be with you, a torment you cannot see. I will break your bones. I will blind you. I will leave you a broken, screaming wreck for the whole world to pity. And then, only then, will I grant you the mercy of death. Do you understand me?”

Kemp swallowed, his throat clicking. He could feel the sheer, psychotic force of the man’s will pressing down on him. “I understand,” he said, his voice barely audible.

“Good.” The dressing-gown moved back towards the armchair and sat down. “Then we have an accord. Now, I shall try and get some sleep. The whiskey has… mellowed me. Do not disturb me. Keep watch. And in the morning, our work begins.”

The scarlet robe settled. The faint sound of breathing, deep and regular, began to fill the silence. Kemp sat frozen in his chair, a prisoner in his own study. He stared at the grotesque figure in the armchair, the slumped shoulders, the empty collar where a head should be. He was trapped. Griffin was too clever, too paranoid, too dangerous. There was no drugging him, no tricking him. Any overt move would mean a horrific death.

But as he sat there, his scientific mind, the mind Griffin had both praised and scorned, began to work again. It moved past the immediate terror and began to analyse the new data. Griffin needed him. Griffin had admitted his own vulnerabilities. Cold, rain, dust. And he needed a visible agent. Kemp was not just a prisoner; he was a necessary tool. And a tool can be turned against its master.

He could not act alone. That much was clear. He needed help. But how to get it? A letter? Griffin would surely search him before he left the house. A coded message? To whom? He had to get word to the authorities. To the police. To someone who could act.

He glanced at the writing desk. At the pens, the inkwell, the pristine white paper. An idea began to form, a desperate, intricate, and terribly dangerous plan. It was a plan that relied on observation, on nerve, and on the particular brand of methodical thinking that Griffin so despised.

His eyes moved from the desk to the sleeping, invisible form. Griffin was a man of science. But his vanity was his undoing. He had laid out his weaknesses, confident that Kemp was too cowed to use them. He had described his own limitations. And in doing so, he had handed Kemp the very weapon he needed.

Kemp rose from his chair, his movements slow and silent. He crept to the window and looked out into the moonlit garden. The world outside was quiet, peaceful, blessedly sane. Down the hill, the lights of Burdock twinkled. Somewhere down there was help. He had until morning. Until morning, to set his own trap. He looked back at the slumped scarlet dressing-gown. The game was not over. It had just entered a new, and far more deadly, phase.
