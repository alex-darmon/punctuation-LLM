"Here is a miniature on paper, dating from the late 17th century, depicting a gentleman in a periwig. A particularly challenging example of biodeterioration and acid degradation. Do you see the pervasive brown spotting, known as 'foxing,' across the entire surface? And the general darkening and embrittlement of the paper support?"

Miss Vance observed the photograph thoughtfully. "Foxing, if I recall correctly, is often attributed to fungal growth or metallic impurities in the paper reacting to humidity, or a combination of both."

"Precisely," Mr. Sedgwick confirmed. "In this case, it was a severe manifestation. The paper, being an organic material composed primarily of cellulose, is highly susceptible to both intrinsic degradation and external attack. The iron impurities within the paper, common in historical paper manufacture, had reacted over time with atmospheric moisture and acidic components, catalyzing oxidative degradation and forming these rust-colored spots. Simultaneously, there was evidence of dormant fungal spores, which, given prolonged exposure to high humidity, had proliferated, creating the darker, more organic-looking spots that often accompany foxing. The paper itself had also become very acidic over centuries, leading to a general yellowing and a significant loss of mechanical strength. It was brittle and fragile, almost crumbling at the edges."

"Treating paper, especially painted paper, must be incredibly delicate," Miss Vance remarked. "Wet treatments can cause bleeding of the pigments, and dry cleaning might not address the chemical degradation."

"You are absolutely correct," Mr. Sedgwick agreed. "This miniature presented a complex conundrum. The pigments, likely watercolors and gouache, were soluble in water. Therefore, traditional aqueous deacidification baths, often used for paper, were out of the question. Our strategy had to be multi-faceted. First, we addressed the surface grime and any loose particulates with very gentle mechanical cleaning using specialist conservation brushes and erasers. Then came the challenge of deacidification and foxing reduction without introducing moisture. We opted for a non-aqueous deacidification solution, applied as a fine mist in a controlled chamber. This solution contained an alkaline reserve, which neutralizes the acids within the paper and leaves behind a buffering agent to protect against future acidification, all without significantly wetting the delicate paint layers. This step arrested the internal degradation of the cellulose."
