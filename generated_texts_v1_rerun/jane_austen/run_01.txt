Indeed, Miss Hastings, Mr. Julian Thorpe began, his gaze lingering on the intricate scrollwork of the botanical drawing Elara held, one cannot but admire such patient application. How many hours do you imagine have been bestowed upon these delicate petals? I dare say your future gardens will bloom with an unparalleled precision, the envy of all less assiduous cultivators. Do you suppose your dear aunt, Mrs. Finch, quite appreciates the depth of such devotion? Or does she, perhaps, merely behold the beauty of the flower, quite unconcerned with the labour of the hand that renders it? He paused, still smiling, a hint of mischief in his bright eyes.

Elara felt a flush rise to her cheeks. She disliked being the subject of such direct, if lightly veiled, scrutiny. My aunt is very kind, sir, to indulge my trivial amusements, she managed, her voice barely above a whisper.

Trivial amusements? Mr. Thorpe echoed, raising an eyebrow playfully. Nay, Miss Hastings, such pursuits are the very bedrock of domestic felicity. I have no doubt that certain individuals, perhaps even some presently engaged in the less refined business of the world, might find such a gentle mastery of nature quite irresistible. Indeed, I often wonder if the Colonel who admired your earlier landscapes might not find these even more compelling. What does Miss Charlotte Davies say to such matters of the heart and the garden spade?

Miss Charlotte Davies, who had been observing the exchange with a quiet amusement, now interjected, I imagine, Mr. Thorpe, that a gentleman of true discernment appreciates many things, not merely the hand that holds the spade, but the mind that conceives the design. And as for matters of the heart, I find they are often best left to discover their own proper season, much like these very roses.

Mrs. Finch, who had been listening with rapt attention, now chimed in, Oh, indeed, Mr. Thorpe, Elara is a marvel! Such a hand with a pencil, just like her dear mother, who could draw the most exquisite patterns for her lace. And only yesterday she was helping me sort out the pantry cupboards, quite a talent for arrangement, my dear, quite. Mr. Alistair Compton, I often tell him, should see how neatly Elara arranges her little sewing box, every thread in its proper place. But then, Mr. Compton has a rather practical turn of mind, does he not? More concerned with the harvests, I daresay, than with lace patterns or pantry order, though one quite leads to the other, does it not? A good housewife, you know, makes for a prosperous farm, or so my grandmother always said. Do you imagine, Mr. Thorpe, that he will call today? I sent him a note about the stray dog that worried Farmer Giles's sheep, such a sad business, but I felt it my duty to inform him.

Mr. Thorpe exchanged a quick, amused glance with Miss Davies, who merely raised an eyebrow. Mr. Compton, madam, is a man of many duties, and it would not surprise me if the well-being of Farmer Giles's flock were paramount. However, I believe even the most practical of gentlemen possess an eye for beauty, particularly when combined with such admirable diligence. He turned his attention back to Elara, who was now meticulously rolling up her drawing. I wish I could claim such steady hands for my own trivial pursuits. What nonsense one talks, Miss Davies, when attempting to compliment without being entirely sincere. Your real artists, I suppose, hold their tongues; but we gentlemen idlers, if we get hold of a word... He gestured vaguely. There, it is done. Miss Hastings, you must permit me to say, your talents are a credit to your sex and a delight to your beholders.

Elara offered a strained smile, grateful that the conversation had shifted slightly, though she still felt uncomfortably exposed. Mr. Thorpe, however, seemed to thrive on such exchanges, and, perceiving her discomfort as a challenge, moved to the pianoforte, which stood in a corner of the room. Miss Hastings, he called, do not run off with your artistic treasures just yet. Will you not favour us with a tune? If you are very kind, it will be that sprightly little air we heard at the Gardiner's ball last week. Let me relive those delightful moments. You appeared somewhat abstracted then, I thought, perhaps contemplating the next stroke of your charcoal; but I, for my part, would have gladly foregone all mundane thought for another half-hour upon that polished floor.

Elara hesitated, but Mrs. Finch urged her, Oh, do play, dear! Mr. Thorpe quite loves your playing, and indeed, it is a joy to us all. You play 'The Shepherd's Lament' so beautifully, though that is not a dancing tune, of course.

Elara sat at the instrument, her fingers a little tremulous. She played the requested air. What felicity it is to hear a tune again which has made one happy! Mr. Thorpe exclaimed, leaning against the pianoforte. If I mistake not that was one of the pieces you practised with such dedication when preparing for the Assembly at Highbury. Elara looked up at him for a moment, a deeper blush spreading across her face, and immediately began a different, less familiar melody.

Mr. Thorpe, undeterred, picked up a sheaf of music from a nearby table. Here is something quite new to me. Do you know it? A collection of Highland Melodies. And here, a series of sonatas by a new German composer. This was all sent with the new harmonium delivered to Mrs. Finch last month. Very thoughtful of her cousin, was it not? He knew Miss Hastings could have no music here of her own. I honour that part of the attention particularly; it shews it to have been so thoroughly from the heart. Nothing hastily done; nothing incomplete. True affection only could have prompted it.

Miss Charlotte Davies, who had moved closer, caught Elara's eye. Elara, despite her heightened colour, managed a faint, almost imperceptible smile of secret pleasure. Miss Davies felt a flicker of amusement, acknowledging the hint of satisfaction beneath the blush. This admirable, seemingly reserved Miss Hastings was apparently not entirely immune to Mr. Thorpe's particular brand of flattery. Mr. Thorpe brought all the music to Elara, and they looked it over together. Miss Davies took the opportunity of whispering to Elara, He speaks too plainly. He means to be understood. Elara merely shook her head slightly, avoiding Miss Davies's gaze.

Just then, Mrs. Finch, who had been peering out of the window, exclaimed, Goodness me, Elara! It is Mr. Compton, I do declare, on horseback, just turning into the lane! He must have received my note. I must speak to him, if possible, just to assure him the dog has been apprehended. I will not open the window here, it would give us all a dreadful draught; but I can slip into the little parlour, you know. I daresay he will come in when he knows who is here. Quite delightful to have you all meet so! Our little room so honoured!

She was in the adjoining chamber while she still spoke, and opening the casement there, immediately called Mr. Compton's attention. Every syllable of their ensuing conversation was as distinctly heard by the others as if it had passed within the same apartment.

How do you do? How do you do? Very well, I thank you. So obliged to you for your prompt attention to the matter of Farmer Giles's sheep. We were quite concerned. Pray come in; do come in. You will find some friends here. So began Mrs. Finch.

Mr. Compton, in turn, seemed determined to be heard, for most resolutely and commandingly did he say, How is your niece, Mrs. Finch? I want to inquire after you all, but particularly your niece. How is Miss Hastings? I hope she took no chill last evening walking home from the village. How is she today? Tell me how Miss Hastings is. And Mrs. Finch was obliged to give a direct answer before he would hear her in anything else.

The listeners, including Mr. Thorpe, were evidently amused. Mr. Thorpe, catching Miss Davies's eye, gave a faint, knowing smirk. Elara, however, maintained a steady composure, though her fingers had stilled upon the pianoforte keys.

So obliged to you! So very much obliged to you for your kindness regarding the dog, resumed Mrs. Finch.

He cut her short with, I am on my way to the market town. Can I do anything for you there?

Oh, dear, the market town, are you? Mrs. Davies was just saying the other day she needed something from the draper's in the market.

Mrs. Davies has servants to send. Can I do anything for you?

No, I thank you. But do come in. Who do you think is here? Miss Hastings and Mr. Thorpe; so kind as to call to hear the new harmonium. Do put up your horse at the Red Lion and come in.

Well, said he, in a deliberating manner, for five minutes, perhaps.

And here is Miss Charlotte Davies too! Quite delightful; so many friends!

No, not now, I thank you. I could not stay two minutes. I must get on to the market as fast as I can.

Oh, do come in. They will be so very happy to see you.

No, no; your room is full enough. I will call another day, and hear the harmonium.

Well, I am so sorry! Oh, Mr. Compton, what a delightful evening we had at the Gardiner's last week; how extremely pleasant. Did you ever see such dancing? Was not it delightful? Miss Hastings and Mr. Thorpe; I never saw anything equal to it.

Oh, very delightful indeed; I can say nothing less, for I suppose Miss Hastings and Mr. Thorpe are hearing everything that passes. And, raising his voice still more, I do not see why Miss Charlotte Davies should not be mentioned too. I think Miss Davies has a remarkably steady head for the quadrilles; and Miss Hastings, though perhaps more reserved in company, is a most graceful dancer when she pleases. Now, if your friends have any gratitude, they will say something pretty loud about you and me in return; but I cannot stay to hear it.

Oh, Mr. Compton, one moment more; something of consequence, so shocked! Elara and I are both so shocked about the apricot preserves!

What is the matter now?

To think of your sending us all your last year's preserves. You said you had a great many, and now you have not one left. We really are so shocked! Cook may well be vexed. Mrs. Pearson mentioned it here. You should not have done it, indeed you should not. Ah, he is off. He never can bear to be thanked. But I thought he would have stayed now, and it would have been a pity not to have mentioned...

Well, returning to the drawing room, I have not been able to succeed. Mr. Compton cannot stop. He is going to the market town. He asked me if he could do anything...

Yes, said Elara, her voice still low but with a hint of quiet amusement, we heard his kind offers, we heard everything.

Oh, yes, my dear, I dare say you might, because you know, the parlour door was open, and the window was open, and Mr. Compton spoke loud. You must have heard everything to be sure. 'Can I do anything for you at the market?' said he; so I just mentioned... Oh, Miss Davies, must you be going? You seem but just come, so very obliging of you.

Miss Charlotte Davies, perceiving the morning to be well advanced and wishing to escape the continued flow of Mrs. Finch's discourse, took her leave, Elara accompanying her to the door, a slight smile playing on her lips.

Elara returned to the drawing room, the slight smile still lingering about her mouth, a subtle acknowledgment of the morning's social intricacies. Mrs. Finch, who had settled herself back onto the sofa, a cushion carefully adjusted behind her back, looked up with an expression of mild reproach, though tempered by her usual good nature.

"Well, my dear," she began, her voice retaining a note of her previous disappointment regarding Mr. Compton's swift departure, "it is a pity Miss Davies could not stay longer. Such a dear girl, and always so ready to oblige. Though I must confess, I fear I overwhelmed her a little with my concern for Mr. Compton's preserves. One does forget how quickly the time passes when one is engaged in such important discourse."

Elara seated herself in a chair opposite her aunt, selecting a piece of embroidery from a basket beside her. Her needles began their rhythmic dance, a quiet counterpoint to Mrs. Finch's ceaseless flow. "Indeed, Aunt," she replied, her voice soft, "Miss Davies had, I believe, several errands to attend to in the village. The morning, as she observed, was well advanced."

"Oh, yes, the village," Mrs. Finch mused, her thoughts already drifting to new channels. "Such a bustle it will be today, with the market. I quite envy Mr. Compton his expedition, though I should not like to contend with the crowds myself. One meets such curious individuals, and the dust, my dear, the dust! It quite sets my teeth on edge. But it does remind

But it does remind me, Elara, of the dreadful affair with the butter at the Christmas market two years ago. Such a scandal, my dear, when Mrs. Davison discovered her purchase was quite rancid. And the grocer, Mr. Hopkins, refused to refund her, saying she had inspected it herself. It caused quite a stir, I assure you, quite a stir. One never knows what one might encounter among the stalls.

Elara’s needles continued their delicate work, a miniature garden of silken threads unfolding beneath her deft fingers. She offered a soft, noncommittal murmur. “Indeed, Aunt. Such incidents are, I suppose, an inevitable part of public gatherings.”

“Inevitable, perhaps,” Mrs. Finch conceded, fanning herself lightly with a hand, though the morning was not particularly warm. “But entirely disagreeable. And quite unnecessary, if people would only exercise a little more honesty and a little less obstinacy. Mrs. Davison, poor woman, was quite inconsolable. She declared she would never again purchase anything from Mr. Hopkins, and I must say, I entirely understood her sentiment. Though one does eventually forget such things, does one not? Or at least, one finds oneself in need of butter again, and Mr. Hopkins’s is, after all, generally quite palatable, despite that one unfortunate episode. It is the way of the world, I suppose, to forgive and forget, or at least to forgive and then conveniently remember only the convenient parts. But I digress, my dear, I quite digress.”

She paused, as if gathering her thoughts from the various corners of the drawing room. “My point was merely that the market, while offering its conveniences, also presents certain perils, and one must be ever vigilant. Particularly for a gentleman such as Mr. Compton, who is, one might say, rather too trusting. He possesses a goodness of heart that, while admirable, does not always serve him well in the rough and tumble of market transactions. He might be persuaded to buy some inferior article, or to pay too much for a perfectly ordinary one. I recall his purchasing a set of gardening tools once, quite enthusiastically, only to discover they were all rusted through beneath a superficial coat of paint. He was so disappointed. And it was such a bother for Cook, for she had quite set her heart on having him assist in turning over the vegetable patch that very afternoon.”

Elara raised her gaze from her embroidery for a moment, a faint smile touching her lips. “I believe Mr. Compton is quite capable of discerning a good bargain, Aunt, and of avoiding obvious impositions. His cheerfulness does not, I imagine, entirely obscure his good sense.”

“Perhaps not entirely,” Mrs. Finch allowed, though a look of mild doubt still clouded her features. “But it is a constant source of concern for me, Elara, that such truly good-natured individuals are so often taken advantage of. It is why I always try to offer guidance, when I can. Though Mr. Compton, as you know, has a certain independence of spirit which can, at times, make him rather impervious to advice. A fine quality in some respects, of course, a truly English spirit, but not always practical in the day to day affairs of life, particularly when dealing with the tradesmen. They see a kind face, you see, and they quite exploit it. And then one is left with the consequences. Like the apricot preserves, for example. I do wish he had not been so very generous. Not for our sake, of course, but for his own. To part with one’s entire stock, when one had such a bountiful harvest the previous year. It quite defies explanation.”

Mrs. Finch sighed, a sound that seemed to emanate from the very depths of her benevolent concern. “And speaking of the preserves, Cook is still quite put out. She says she had planned a delightful tart for this very evening, a special treat, and now she must resort to plum, which, while perfectly acceptable, lacks the distinctive zest of the apricot. It is a small matter, of course, but these small matters, my dear, often contribute to the general harmony of a household, or, conversely, to its occasional disquiet. Cook, as you know, is a most excellent woman, but she does have her principles, and she feels that Mr. Compton ought to have at least consulted us before making such an extravagant gesture. Not that it was not kind, you understand, merely imprudent. Oh, if only he were a little more inclined to heed the advice of others, particularly those who have his best interests at heart.”

Elara nodded slowly, her expression thoughtful. She understood her aunt’s anxieties, even if she did not always share their intensity. Mrs. Finch, for all her amiable disposition, possessed a remarkable capacity for fretting over minor domestic occurrences and social nuances. It was a well-meaning trait, Elara knew, but one that could, at times, become rather exhaustive. She thought of Mr. Compton, his good humor, his unassuming nature. It was true he was perhaps less worldly than some, less guarded, but his very openness was part of his charm. He gave freely, without calculation, and if that occasionally led to minor inconveniences, it also spoke of a generosity of spirit that Elara found quite refreshing.

“Perhaps,” Elara ventured gently, “Mr. Compton derives more pleasure from giving than from retaining. His simple acts of kindness, however ill-advised they may sometimes appear from a practical standpoint, are, I believe, deeply appreciated, even by Cook, despite her present vexation.”

Mrs. Finch considered this, her head tilted slightly to one side. “Well, that is a very charitable interpretation, my dear, and perhaps there is some truth to it. He is a truly benevolent soul, one cannot deny that. But benevolence, Elara, must sometimes be tempered with prudence. That is all I mean to say. One must think of the future, and of one’s own requirements. For what will he do next year, when the apricots are perhaps not so plentiful, and he has no reserves to fall back upon? It is a question that weighs upon my mind.”

She then seemed to shake off her concern, as if deciding it was unproductive to dwell too long on such matters in Mr. Compton’s absence. Her gaze drifted towards the open window, through which the faint sounds of the village, a distant cart, a child’s cry, occasionally permeated the quiet of the drawing room.

“Speaking of which, Elara, have you given any further thought to our visit to Mrs. Harrison next Tuesday? She mentioned she was expecting her niece, Miss Isabella Travers, to arrive from Bath. A very accomplished young lady, I understand, with a considerable talent for the pianoforte. I do hope she is not one of those excessively brilliant young women who make one feel utterly inadequate. Though, of course, one must always strive to be agreeable, regardless of one’s personal feelings of inferiority.”

Elara set aside her embroidery, carefully placing it in the basket. The subject of social visits and potential new acquaintances was always one of Mrs. Finch’s preferred topics. “I had quite forgotten about Miss Travers, Aunt. I trust she will prove to be a pleasant addition to our local society. Bath, as you say, is a centre of considerable culture, so we may expect much from her.”

“Indeed we may,” Mrs. Finch replied, a spark of anticipation in her eyes. “And I do hope she brings with her a breath of fresh air, a certain… <i>éclat</i>, if you will. Our village, charming as it is, can sometimes feel a little too settled, too predictable. A new face, particularly one with such accomplishments, can enliven things wonderfully. And, Elara, it might even be an opportunity for you to expand your own social circle. Young ladies of your age ought not to confine themselves entirely to the company of their immediate family, however delightful that company may be.” She offered Elara an affectionate smile, which Elara returned with a dutiful one.

Elara knew her aunt’s well-intentioned hints. Mrs. Finch was perpetually, if subtly, seeking opportunities for Elara’s advancement, for her to meet suitable young gentlemen, for her to form connections that might lead to a desirable establishment. It was the common concern of every respectable aunt or mother for a young woman of Elara’s age and circumstances, and Elara accepted it with her customary quiet resignation. She was not averse to society, nor was she particularly desirous of it. She found pleasure in her books, in her quiet walks, in the simple routines of their household, and in the occasional stimulating conversation. Her aspirations, if she had any grand ones, were not those of immediate matrimony or dazzling social triumphs.

“I shall certainly endeavor to be as agreeable as possible, Aunt,” Elara assured her. “Perhaps Miss Travers will have brought with her some new sheet music, or a fascinating account of the latest fashions in Bath.”

“Oh, the fashions!” Mrs. Finch exclaimed, clasping her hands together. “That would be quite delightful! My dear, I have been meaning to consult you on the matter of my new bonnet. Mrs. Pearson, when she called, rather implied that the ribbons I had chosen were a trifle too… vigorous, for the season. She suggested a paler shade, something more in keeping with the delicacy of the new muslins. But I rather liked the spirited hue, you know, Elara. It adds a certain dash. What do you think, honestly, my dear?”

Elara suppressed a small sigh. The bonnets and ribbons of Mrs. Finch were a frequent and recurring topic, often debated at length. “Aunt, your taste is always impeccable. If you preferred the spirited hue, I am certain it was entirely appropriate. Mrs. Pearson, while well-meaning, does occasionally adhere to the more rigid conventions of taste. Personal preference, I believe, should always take precedence, within reason.”

“Within reason, yes, precisely!” Mrs. Finch declared, looking much relieved. “You always understand, Elara. That is what I appreciate about you. You have a sensibility that is quite uncommon. And I was beginning to think I had made a dreadful blunder. To be thought vigorous when one intends merely to be sprightly! It quite puts one out of countenance. But you are quite right. One must have some individuality, must one not? Otherwise, we would all look precisely alike, and where would be the charm in that? Life would be quite tedious, would it not, if everyone conformed to the precise dictates of Mrs. Pearson?”

She smiled at her niece, her good humor fully restored. “Perhaps, my dear, when Mr. Compton returns from the market, he will bring with him some small item of interest. He is always so kind in thinking of us, even when he has been expressly told not to trouble himself. I particularly recall the time he brought back those rather unusual gooseberries, so much larger and sweeter than our own. Cook was quite delighted, despite her usual disdain for anything novel. And I confess, I found them quite superior in my tart.”

Elara smiled faintly. Her aunt’s concerns, however varied, often circled back to food and Mr. Compton. It was a comfortable pattern, a familiar rhythm to their days. She picked up her embroidery once more, her needles beginning their silent dance. The morning light deepened, casting longer shadows across the polished floorboards. A gentle breeze rustled the curtains, bringing with it the scent of early summer flowers from the garden.

The quiet domestic scene continued undisturbed for some time, punctuated only by the occasional rustle of Mrs. Finch’s dress as she shifted on the sofa, or the soft click of Elara’s needle against the fabric. Mrs. Finch had settled into a comfortable silence, a rare occurrence, which Elara attributed to the combined effects of her recent pronouncements and the soothing presence of her niece. She appeared to be contemplating something deeply, her gaze fixed on a distant point beyond the window.

Presently, she stirred, a small sigh escaping her lips. “You know, Elara, it often strikes me how swiftly time passes. Only yesterday, it seems, you were but a child, a mere slip of a girl, and now… now you are quite grown, a young woman of discernment and sense. It is a peculiar thing, this passage of years. One scarcely has time to adjust to one’s present state before it has already become the past. And then one wonders what the future holds.”

Elara paused her stitching. This was a more reflective tone from her aunt, one that Elara always found a little poignant. “Time is indeed a swift current, Aunt. But I find some comfort in the enduring nature of our routines, and in the presence of those we hold dear.”

“Yes, my dear, you are quite right, quite right,” Mrs. Finch agreed, reaching out to pat Elara’s hand. “And we are most fortunate, are we not, to have such a pleasant home, and such agreeable company. Though I sometimes wish for a little more… adventure, perhaps. Not anything truly alarming, you understand, merely a slight stirring of the waters. A grand ball, perhaps, or a lengthy journey to the coast. Something to enliven the spirits and provide fresh topics for conversation.”

Elara imagined her aunt at a grand ball, or attempting a lengthy journey, and a small, almost imperceptible tremor of amusement passed through her. Mrs. Finch, for all her desires for adventure, was deeply rooted in her comfortable drawing room and her established routines. Any deviation, however minor, usually brought with it a corresponding degree of anxiety.

“Perhaps Miss Travers will bring with her some tales of the diversions of Bath, Aunt,” Elara suggested, steering the conversation back to a safer, more predictable channel. “That might provide some agreeable variety without the attendant inconveniences of actual travel.”

“Ah, yes, Miss Travers,” Mrs. Finch said, her eyes brightening once more. “Indeed, she will be a most welcome diversion. And I do hope she is not too shy. Some young women, particularly those who are accomplished, can be rather retiring. One has to draw them out, you see, and that can be quite exhausting. Though I do not mind the effort, of course, if the result is a truly stimulating exchange of ideas. One does crave intellectual companionship, does one not, Elara? Especially in a village where conversation can sometimes veer rather dangerously towards the mundane, such as the price of oats or the latest bout of indigestion suffered by Mrs. Henderson’s sister-in-law. Not that I mean to disparage such topics, for they are, I suppose, important in their own sphere, but one does occasionally long for a more elevated discourse.”

Elara perfectly understood her aunt’s sentiment. While she herself was quite content with quiet observation, she recognized her aunt’s need for intellectual stimulation, or at least for topics that allowed for expansive, detailed discussion. Mrs. Finch loved to dissect social interactions, to analyze motivations, and to offer her considered opinions on every conceivable subject.

“I am sure Miss Travers will prove to be a most engaging conversationalist, Aunt,” Elara replied. “Her experience of Bath society alone should provide ample material for several lengthy discussions.”

“One certainly hopes so,” Mrs. Finch mused, her thoughts already spinning ahead to Tuesday’s visit. “And I must remember to instruct Cook to prepare her very best ratafia biscuits. Miss Travers, coming from Bath, will undoubtedly have a refined palate, and one wishes to make a good impression. Though I do hope she is not excessively critical. Some individuals, particularly those who have resided in fashionable resorts, acquire a certain fastidiousness which can be rather trying for their provincial hosts. Not that I fear criticism, you understand, but one does prefer one’s efforts to be appreciated.”

Elara smiled. “I am quite certain your hospitality will be most warmly appreciated, Aunt, regardless of Miss Travers’s background. Your kindness is universally acknowledged.”

“You are very sweet, my dear,” Mrs. Finch said, beaming. “And I do believe that is quite true. One does try to be kind, does one not? It is, after all, the foundation of all good society. Without kindness, and a proper consideration for the feelings of others, the world would be a very bleak and disagreeable place. Imagine, Elara, if everyone behaved as rudely as that dreadful man, Mr. Thorpe, who insulted poor Miss Jenkins so abominably at the assembly last year. I still quite shudder to think of it. He declared her gown to be quite out of fashion, and her dancing entirely devoid of grace. Such an odious individual! And Miss Jenkins, poor girl, was so mortified she quite retired from society for a full fortnight. It was a most distressing affair.”

Elara nodded, recalling the incident. Mr. Thorpe was indeed a most unpleasant man, known for his ill-mannered remarks and pompous self-regard. “It was a regrettable display, Aunt, and quite uncalled for. Miss Jenkins, I thought, bore it with remarkable composure.”

“Remarkable composure, indeed!” Mrs. Finch agreed emphatically. “But it should never have been necessary. No gentleman, properly raised, would ever inflict such pain upon a lady, regardless of the merits of her gown or her dancing. It is a matter of breeding, Elara, pure and simple. And Mr. Thorpe, for all his pretensions, has none of it. I often wonder what his poor mother must have done to produce such a specimen of incivility. Though one should not speak ill of the dead, of course, and Mrs. Thorpe was, I believe, a perfectly respectable woman, if a trifle indulgent of her son’s unfortunate tendencies. But I digress, my dear, I quite digress. My point was merely that kindness is paramount, and I trust Miss Travers, being from a good family and residing in Bath, will possess that fundamental virtue.”

The conversation then drifted to the merits of various confectioners in Bath, a topic that occupied Mrs. Finch’s attention for a considerable time, allowing Elara to return to her embroidery with only an occasional interjection of assent. The thought of Mr. Compton and the market, however, remained subtly present in Elara’s mind. She wondered what small discovery he might make, what unexpected turn his simple errand might take. He possessed an uncanny knack for stumbling upon curiosities, for bringing home objects that, however humble, always held a story or an element of the unexpected.

It was this quality in Mr. Compton, she mused, that made him such a singular character in their otherwise predictable village. He was not grand or intellectual, not particularly witty or refined, but he possessed an unaffected cheerfulness and a genuine delight in the small wonders of the world that Elara found quite appealing. His enthusiasm was contagious, his good nature unfeigned. And while her aunt worried about his lack of prudence, Elara saw in it a refreshing lack of artifice.

Her thoughts were interrupted by the distant sound of a carriage approaching the house. It was not the familiar rumble of a tradesman’s cart, nor the lighter clatter of a gig. This sound was more substantial, more formal. Mrs. Finch immediately straightened on the sofa, her hand going instinctively to her hair.

“Good heavens, Elara! Who can that be at this hour? It is far too early for callers, and certainly not the day for it. Have you forgotten any appointments, my dear?”

Elara shook her head. “None that I recall, Aunt. And I believe our last visitor, Miss Davies, only just departed.”

The sound grew closer, and then the distinct crunch of carriage wheels on the gravel drive announced its arrival at the front door. A moment later, the sharp, decisive rap of the knocker echoed through the house.

Mrs. Finch looked quite flustered. “Well, this is entirely unexpected! I do hope it is not Mrs. Henderson, come to complain about something else. She has been in a most difficult mood ever since the matter of the new parson’s surplice. Cook, if you please!” she called, raising her voice slightly towards the kitchen regions. “See who is at the door, if you would be so kind. And do make haste!”

A few moments later, Cook’s rather hurried footsteps could be heard, followed by the muffled murmur of voices at the entrance. Then, Cook reappeared in the doorway of the drawing room, her face a picture of mild surprise and a hint of trepidation.

“It is a gentleman, ma’am,” she announced, addressing Mrs. Finch, though her gaze flickered towards Elara. “Mr. Reginald Hartley, ma’am. He says he is come on behalf of his uncle, Mr. Thomas Hartley, from London. And he enquires for Miss Elara. He begs your pardon for the early call.”

Mrs. Finch gasped, her hand flying to her chest. “Mr. Reginald Hartley! From London? Good heavens, Elara, what can this mean? Why, I have not seen young Reginald since he was but a schoolboy, quite the scamp, as I recall. And his uncle, Mr. Thomas Hartley, is a most respectable man, a solicitor, I believe, though I confess I had quite lost touch with that branch of the family. To think of it! Cook, show Mr. Hartley in immediately, do not keep the poor gentleman waiting on the doorstep! And perhaps a fresh pot of tea, and some of those shortbread biscuits you made yesterday.”

Mr. Reginald Hartley. The name was vaguely familiar, certainly. She knew of the Hartley family, distant connections of her own deceased father, but she had never met this particular cousin. The next morning, Elara was once again at Mr. Hartley’s office, the offending letter laid flat on his desk between them. Mr. Hartley read it carefully, his brow furrowed deeper with each sentence.

“As I suspected,” he murmured, tapping the letter with a precise finger. “A classic deflection. He acknowledges your letters to appear reasonable, dismisses your concerns as unfounded without offering any substantiation, and then distances himself by directing you to a subordinate. It is a subtle but effective maneuver designed to discourage further pursuit.”

“But what does it mean, Mr. Hartley?” Elara asked, her voice trembling slightly with suppressed emotion. “He says there are no discrepancies. Could he be telling the truth?”

Mr. Hartley fixed her with a direct gaze. “Miss Ashton, while it is always possible, it is highly improbable that a man of Mr. Henderson’s experience would react in such a manner if everything were truly in order. A simple meeting, a quick review of the bank statements and trust deeds, would clear his name entirely. His refusal to engage directly, especially after the second letter, speaks volumes. It suggests he either cannot, or will not, provide the clarification we seek.”

“So, what now?” Elara felt a surge of cold determination. “He has left me no choice. What are the ‘further means’ we discussed?”

Mr. Hartley picked up a pen, twirling it thoughtfully between his fingers. “Well, the most direct approach would be to issue a formal legal summons. This would compel him, or his firm, to produce the relevant documents for review. However, Miss Ashton, this is a serious step. It moves the matter from a private inquiry to a public dispute. It would, inevitably, invite the very scandal your aunt fears.”

Elara swallowed hard. “And if I do not?”

“If you do not, then Mr. Henderson will have effectively won,” Mr. Hartley said plainly. “He will have evaded accountability, and your father’s trust, as it stands now, will remain a mystery. You will have no recourse, no means of verifying that your inheritance has been handled as your father intended.”

The image of her father, his kind face, his unwavering belief in honesty and justice, flashed before Elara’s eyes. She recalled the pride in his voice when he had spoken of her future security, the careful planning he had undertaken to ensure her well being. To simply walk away now, to accept Mr. Henderson’s dismissive declaration, felt like a betrayal of her father’s memory, a surrender to an injustice.

“No,” she said, her voice quiet but firm. “I cannot. I will not let him simply dismiss this. My father deserved better, and so do I. What would a summons entail, precisely?”

Mr. Hartley nodded, a flicker of approval in his eyes. “It would entail preparing a formal legal document, outlining the specific provisions of the trust and the unresolved questions, and demanding the production of all relevant financial records and correspondence pertaining to the trust. This document would then be officially served upon Mr. Henderson’s firm. Should they fail to comply, or fail to provide satisfactory answers, we would then be in a position to petition the courts for an order compelling their cooperation. This is, of course, a more protracted and potentially costly endeavor.”

“Costly how?” Elara inquired, practical concerns rising even amidst her indignation.

“In terms of legal fees, naturally, but also in terms of reputation, as we have discussed,” Mr. Hartley replied. “Once such a summons is issued, it becomes public record, or at least, knowledge within the legal and financial circles of the town. The whispers will no longer be mere conjecture; they will be rooted in fact, however unpleasant.”

Elara sat for a long moment, considering the stark choices before her. On one side, the comfort of retreat, the avoidance of public scrutiny, the path of least resistance. On the other, the difficult, potentially damaging fight for truth and justice. The decision felt immense, a turning point not just for her inheritance, but for her own character. She knew that once she committed to this path, there would be no turning back without severe damage to her own standing. But the thought of allowing Mr. Henderson to simply walk away, to perhaps profit from what appeared to be a deception, was intolerable.

“Mr. Hartley,” Elara finally said, meeting his gaze squarely, “prepare the summons. I wish to proceed.”

A flicker of surprise, then respect, crossed Mr. Hartley’s face. “Very well, Miss Ashton. I commend your fortitude. This is not a path many would choose.” He began to make notes on a fresh pad of paper. “We will need to be meticulous in our preparation. I will require every scrap of correspondence, every detail you recall your father mentioning about the trust, any bank statements or documents you possess, no matter how insignificant they may seem. The more thoroughly we construct our case, the stronger our position will be.”

Over the next few days, Elara immersed herself in the task, poring over old ledgers, sorting through her father’s desk drawers, and sifting through piles of documents. She found old bank passbooks, cancelled cheques, and correspondence that offered glimpses into her father’s financial dealings. While nothing immediately revealed a smoking gun, the collection of documents deepened her understanding of her father’s methodical nature and confirmed the precise details Mr. Hartley had initially presented. Each document she unearthed, each figure she verified, strengthened her conviction that her father would not have left such a significant sum to ambiguity.

Mrs. Finch, upon learning of Elara’s decision to issue a legal summons, dissolved into a state of profound distress. She spent her days weeping quietly into handkerchiefs, murmuring about the “disgrace” and the “shame” that would surely befall their respectable family. “Your father would never have wanted this, Elara,” she wailed one evening, her voice hoarse from crying. “He was a man of peace, a man who abhorred contention.”

“And a man of integrity, Aunt,” Elara countered gently, but firmly. “He would not have tolerated deception. He trusted Mr. Henderson implicitly. If that trust was misplaced, then it is my duty to ascertain the truth.” Elara understood her aunt’s fear, but it no longer swayed her. The quiet strength she had sought to project to her aunt weeks ago was now truly her own.

The legal process, as Mr. Hartley explained, was not swift. It involved the meticulous drafting of the summons, the careful verification of every detail, and the formal procedures of filing and serving the document. Each step was taken with solemnity, a silent drumbeat marking the progression toward an inevitable confrontation. Elara felt a strange mix of apprehension and exhilaration. The waiting, the passive anxiety, was replaced by a sense of active engagement. She was no longer merely listening for the postman; she was moving forward, taking control.

Mr. Hartley assured her that the serving of a summons often prompted a more direct response, if only to avoid the additional complexities of formal court proceedings. “It forces their hand, Miss Ashton,” he explained. “They can no longer simply ignore you or fob you off. The legal system, slow as it may be, has a way of commanding attention.”

Elara understood. She was, in essence, forcing Mr. Henderson’s hand, compelling him to face the questions he had so arrogantly dismissed. The thought sent a jolt of both fear and satisfaction through her. She was a young woman, relatively inexperienced in the world of finance and legal battles, but she was armed with a sense of righteous purpose and the astute guidance of Mr. Hartley. She knew this battle would not be easy, that it would further solidify her position as a pariah in certain social circles, but the alternative, a silent acceptance of potential deceit, was simply unthinkable. She prepared herself for the storm, drawing strength from the memory of her father and the quiet conviction that she was doing what was right. The town might whisper, her aunt might despair, but Elara Ashton was determined to uncover the truth, whatever the cost.
