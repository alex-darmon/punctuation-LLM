Miss Eleanor sat by the window, a letter from her aunt held delicately in her hand, but her attention was continually drawn away by the incessant observations of Mr. Hemlock. He considered himself a most discerning judge of human nature, particularly that of young ladies, and was rarely silent when one was present. “You find the morning air quite invigorating, do you not, Miss Eleanor?” he began, adjusting his spectacles. “A slight breeze, perhaps; enough to stir the leaves, certainly, but insufficient to cause any serious discomposure. I always say that a gentle breath of nature is beneficial for the nerves, though too much can bring on a cold. Such is often the case, I find.” She smiled faintly, a practiced gesture, without offering a direct reply; she simply wished to finish her reading.

“One must be careful in these changeable seasons,” he continued, undeterred by her silence. “My grandmother, a woman of considerable fortitude, once caught a severe chill from merely stepping outside without her shawl; it laid her low for weeks. Indeed, one never knows when a simple oversight might lead to great suffering. We must ever be vigilant, must we not?” He paused, expecting some acknowledgement of his sagacity. Eleanor, however, had finally found a moment to skim the first paragraph of her letter, her brow slightly furrowed.

“Is that news from your relations in Kent, Miss Eleanor?” he enquired with an air of polite curiosity which barely concealed his deeper desire for information. “I had heard a rumour of some movement amongst them, a potential change of residence; I trust

they are all in good health, and their arrangements proceed without inconvenience?”

Eleanor suppressed a sigh; she wished devoutly to be spared the necessity of answering. “They are, I believe, in their usual state of health, Mr. Hemlock,” she replied, offering a brief glance, her mind already returning to the delicate script before her. “My aunt merely writes of domestic matters, as is her wont.”

“Domestic matters are indeed the very heart of existence, are they not, Miss Eleanor?” he mused, tapping his chin with an air of profound sagacity. “One cannot underestimate the significance of a well-ordered household, especially when contemplating such changes as a removal. A new neighbourhood, new neighbours, new tradesmen; it all requires considerable forethought and management. I recall when the Pembertons moved from their delightful cottage near the common; it was a perfect whirlwind of activity, and Mrs. Pemberton, though an excellent woman, was quite undone by the whole affair. Indeed, one must possess a strong constitution for such upheavals. Is your aunt, pray tell, of a robust disposition?”

“My aunt, sir, is a woman of admirable spirit and excellent judgment,” Eleanor stated, allowing a faint edge of formality to enter her tone, hoping to discourage further intrusion. She then made a point of folding the letter, deliberately, as if to conclude the topic.

“Ah, admirable spirit!” he exclaimed, entirely missing her cue. “A most commendable quality, particularly in times of transition. One always appreciates spirit, though judgment, I confess, is perhaps the more practical attribute for managing such weighty affairs as property and position. Indeed, I have often observed that spirit without sound judgment can lead one into the most lamentable difficulties. My own uncle, a man of profound spirit, invested a considerable sum in a turnpike trust which, alas, never materialised, leaving him somewhat impoverished. A cautionary tale, would you not agree? And property, Miss Eleanor, is a matter of profound concern, especially for ladies. For though a woman’s estate may be secured, its management often falls to others; a sad truth, but a truth nonetheless. Is the Kentish property, perchance, subject to any particularly complex conveyances?”

Eleanor felt a blush rise to her cheeks, partly from vexation, partly from the impropriety of his probing. “My aunt’s affairs are, I am sure, managed with the utmost discretion and propriety, Mr. Hemlock,” she responded, her voice a little firmer now. “I have no doubt of it. And as for the content of her letters, they are, naturally, of a private nature.”

Mr. Hemlock blinked, perhaps registering a hint of reproof, but his self-importance was rarely shaken. “To be sure, to be sure, privacy is a most valuable commodity,” he conceded, adjusting his spectacles once more. “One must respect boundaries, of course. Yet, in matters of such import as residence and estate, a well-meaning friend might often offer invaluable counsel. For instance, I have a cousin, a solicitor of considerable repute in Bristol, who has a particular aptitude for disentangling even the most intricate settlements. A truly remarkable mind, I assure you. Perhaps, if ever your aunt should find herself in need of such guidance, a simple introduction could be arranged. One never knows when such connections might prove advantageous, does one?”

Eleanor’s patience, never inexhaustible, was now quite spent. She could not, however, simply rise and walk away, for such an act would be considered intolerably rude in the circumstances. She looked desperately towards the door, hoping for an interruption, a visitor, a servant with a message, anything to relieve her of Mr. Hemlock’s interminable discourse. Just as she began to contemplate the bold stroke of feigning a sudden indisposition, a light tap sounded at the parlour door, and the servant announced, “Miss Finch, ma’am, and Mr. Reginald Finch.”

Eleanor’s relief was immediate and profound. “Oh, how delightful!” she exclaimed, rising with an animation that quite startled Mr. Hemlock. “Do show them in immediately, John.”

Mr. Hemlock, somewhat put out by the sudden change in atmosphere, merely cleared his throat, gathering his thoughts for a fresh audience. Miss Finch, a bright-eyed young woman of twenty, entered with her brother, a rather quiet gentleman who seemed perpetually surprised to find himself in company. Miss Finch, however, possessed a cheerful volubility that made up for her brother’s reticence.

“Miss Eleanor, my dearest!” cried Miss Finch, advancing with outstretched hands. “What a truly charming morning it is, and how fortunate we are to find you at home! We took the liberty of walking over, for Reginald declared the air quite salubrious, did you not, Reginald?”

Mr. Reginald Finch gave a slight, hesitant nod, and bowed politely to Eleanor and then to Mr. Hemlock, who was now straightening his waistcoat with renewed vigour.

“Mr. Hemlock, you are looking quite in spirits,” observed Miss Finch, with an innocent lack of discernment. “Did you enjoy a particularly fine breakfast this morning? My brother insists that a hearty meal sets one up for the entire day, and indeed, I think he is quite right.”

Mr. Hemlock, ever eager for an opening, smiled benignly. “Indeed, Miss Finch, a breakfast of certain proportions is, without question, most beneficial. But my spirits, I assure you, are always elevated in the company of agreeable young ladies. I was just discussing with Miss Eleanor the inherent complexities of domestic arrangements, particularly those involving changes of residence.”

Eleanor winced, but Miss Finch, oblivious, merely chirped, “Oh, changes of residence! How exciting! Are you contemplating a move, Miss Eleanor? How thrilling that would be! I adore new houses; they always hold such promise, do they not?”

Eleanor quickly interjected, “Not at all, Miss Finch. Mr. Hemlock was merely speculating upon the general topic, inspired by a letter I had just received. There is no question of my moving, I assure you.”

“Ah, a general topic, then!” Miss Finch beamed. “I quite understand. General topics are so very useful for conversation, especially when one has little else to discuss. And what a lovely morning for a walk! We saw Mrs. Bennet’s new carriage on the road; quite a handsome equipage, though perhaps a little too ostentatious for my taste. What do you think, Miss Eleanor? Is it not rather too much gilding for a simple barouche?”

This rapid change of subject was a welcome reprieve for Eleanor, and she gladly engaged in a discussion of carriage aesthetics with Miss Finch, while Mr. Hemlock attempted, with limited success, to draw Mr. Reginald Finch into a more profound conversation about the merits of different coach-builders. Reginald, however, mostly confined himself to monosyllabic agreements or polite nods, his gaze often drifting towards the window.

The morning wore on in this fashion, a delicate dance of social obligations and thinly veiled disinterest. Eleanor, though grateful for the arrival of the Finches, found herself mentally reviewing her aunt’s letter, the contents of which had been so rudely interrupted. Her aunt, Mrs. Daventry, a widow of comfortable means and a sharp wit, had written from her estate in Kent with news that was, to Eleanor, rather perplexing. There was indeed a mention of a 'change', but not of residence; rather, a change in circumstances for her cousin, Miss Clara Daventry.

The letter, once Eleanor had managed to reread it properly after the Finches and Mr. Hemlock had, at long last, taken their leave, revealed the true nature of Mrs. Daventry’s concern. Clara, it seemed, had formed an attachment. Not an attachment to a gentleman of suitable fortune and family, as Mrs. Daventry had fervently hoped, but to a young man, a lieutenant in the militia, with no prospects beyond his commission, and a family, according to Mrs. Daventry, of merely ‘respectable’ rather than ‘distinguished’ origins. The horror! Mrs. Daventry had used no fewer than three exclamation marks to convey her dismay, a punctuation mark she usually reserved for news of exceptional good fortune or genuine calamity.

Eleanor reread the passages again, her brow furrowed with genuine worry for her cousin.

“...my dear Eleanor, I scarce know how to relate the dreadful news! Clara, our sweet Clara, has quite lost her head, and formed an attachment to a Lieutenant Davies! A charming young man, I will grant you, with a fine set of whiskers and a most engaging smile, but quite without fortune, and with no connections to speak of. His family, though respectable, are merely common gentry, with neither consequence nor estate to commend them. I am at my wits’ end, I truly am! He has, it seems, quite captivated her, and she speaks of nothing but his honour and his courage. I have tried reason, I have tried gentle persuasion, I have even, I confess, resorted to a little gentle reproof, but to no avail. She is quite determined! He wishes to declare himself, and she, alas, is only too ready to accept. What am I to do, Eleanor? You, with your calm good sense, must surely see the folly of such a match. A militia officer! With nothing but his pay and his sword! It would be ruin for her, absolute ruin! I cannot bear to think of Clara in a cottage with no proper staff, or worse, following the regiment from town to town like a camp-follower. It is quite beyond anything I had imagined for her. I must beg your counsel, my dear; you always possessed a clear head, even as a child. Can you not come to Kent for a short visit? Perhaps your presence, your gentle influence, might turn her from this disastrous path. I feel so quite alone in this predicament; your Aunt Martha, you know, has always been rather too romantic for her own good, and would likely encourage the poor girl. Do consider it, Eleanor; I truly stand in need of your advice.”

Eleanor folded the letter slowly, her heart heavy. Clara, dear Clara, always so vivacious and headstrong. It was precisely her nature to fall for a dashing young officer, and equally her nature to cling to her affections with a stubbornness that belied her gentle appearance. Eleanor knew her aunt’s distress was genuine, for Mrs. Daventry had always held the highest aspirations for her only daughter, envisioning a match that would secure Clara’s future in the most comfortable and advantageous manner possible. A penniless lieutenant, however charming, was decidedly not part of that vision.

A visit to Kent. The thought brought with it a mixture of apprehension and a strange sense of duty. Eleanor had not visited her aunt and cousin in over a year, and though she loved them dearly, the journey was long, and her own circumstances were, at present, rather uncertain. Her current residence was with her widowed mother, Mrs. Vernon, in a modest house in a quiet country town, a house which, though comfortable, offered little in the way of excitement or prospects. Eleanor, at five and twenty, was still unmarried, a state that, while not uncommon, was beginning to weigh upon her spirits, especially as her younger sisters were already settled. She possessed no fortune of her own, save a small annuity, and depended entirely upon her mother’s generosity, which was, happily, considerable, though not limitless.

To leave her mother, even for a short time, would require explanation and permission. Mrs. Vernon, a woman of generally placid temperament, was prone to anxieties, and any sudden change of plans often threw her into a state of mild agitation. Eleanor knew she would need to tread carefully.

Later that afternoon, as they sat together in the drawing-room, Mrs. Vernon engaged in her needlework, Eleanor chose her moment. “Mama,” she began, “I have received a letter from Aunt Daventry.”

Mrs. Vernon looked up, her spectacles perched on her nose. “Oh, indeed, my dear? And how is dear Maria? I trust her rheumatism is not troubling her this changeable weather. And Clara, our sweet Clara? Is she well?”

“They are well enough, Mama,” Eleanor replied, choosing her words with care. “Though Aunt Daventry is, I apprehend, a little concerned about Clara.”

“Concerned?” repeated Mrs. Vernon, laying down her embroidery. “Whatever can be the matter? Has she caught a chill? Or perhaps her mare has cast a shoe? Maria always worried so about that spirited creature.”

“No, Mama, nothing so trivial,” Eleanor said gently. “It concerns Clara’s affections. She has formed an attachment, it seems, to a young gentleman, a Lieutenant Davies.”

Mrs. Vernon’s eyes widened. “A lieutenant? Oh, dear! Is he a man of fortune, Eleanor? I do hope so, for Clara’s sake. She is such a sweet girl, and deserves only the very best.”

“He is, I gather, without fortune, Mama,” Eleanor admitted, lowering her voice slightly. “And his family, though respectable, are not of any particular consequence. Aunt Daventry is, as you might imagine, quite distressed by the attachment. She fears it will lead to Clara’s ruin.”

Mrs. Vernon wrung her hands. “Oh, my poor Maria! What a predicament! Clara, so headstrong! I always said she had too much spirit for her own good. But ruin, Eleanor? Surely not ruin? A lieutenant, though not ideal, is still a gentleman, is he not?”

“He is indeed a gentleman, Mama, from all accounts,” Eleanor affirmed. “But his prospects are, at best, limited. And Aunt Daventry has always cherished rather grander hopes for Clara. She has written to me, asking for my counsel, and indeed, she has suggested that I pay them a visit in Kent, to perhaps offer some gentle persuasion to Clara.”

Mrs. Vernon paused, considering this. “A visit to Kent? Oh, Eleanor, that is a long journey. And your father’s sister, you know, Mrs. Markham, always finds it so difficult when you are away. She depends upon your company for her afternoon callers, and I am sure she would miss you terribly. Besides, who would read to me in the evenings? My eyes, you know, are not what they once were.”

“I understand your concerns, Mama,” Eleanor said, moving closer and taking her mother’s hand. “But Aunt Daventry is truly in distress. She feels quite alone in this matter, and I believe my presence might indeed be of some small assistance. And as for Aunt Markham, I am sure she would manage perfectly well for a few weeks; perhaps Miss Smith from the village could sit with her occasionally. And I could write to you every day, Mama, and return as soon as I felt I had been of any use.”

Mrs. Vernon sighed, a long, wavering sound. “It is a great imposition, my dear. To travel so far for such an uncertain outcome. For Clara, if she is truly determined, will not easily be swayed. She has always been her father’s daughter in that respect; once her mind is set, it is quite impossible to shift it. I remember when she was but ten, she insisted upon wearing her new bonnet to church, though it was quite unfashionable, and her mother quite forbade it. But Clara wore it, all the same, and declared she would sooner not go to church at all than be seen without it.”

Eleanor smiled faintly, remembering the incident. “She does possess a strong will, Mama, that is true. But perhaps a fresh perspective, a quiet conversation, might prove more effective than Aunt Daventry’s present arguments. My aunt, I apprehend, is rather too passionate in her opposition, and perhaps Clara feels cornered.”

“Perhaps,” Mrs. Vernon conceded, still looking doubtful. “But the expense of the journey, Eleanor. A post-chaise to Kent, and back. It is not a trifling sum, you know. And we must always be mindful of our finances. Your father, bless his soul, always taught me the importance of economy.”

“Aunt Daventry has offered to defray all my travelling expenses, Mama,” Eleanor quickly assured her. “She was quite insistent upon it in her letter. She truly wishes for my presence.”

This seemed to sway Mrs. Vernon more than any other argument. “Well, if Maria is to pay for it, that is a different matter entirely,” she mused. “One must never refuse a generous offer, especially from family. And you are, after all, a sensible girl, Eleanor. You will not allow Clara to make a foolish match, will you?”

“I shall do my utmost, Mama,” Eleanor promised, though with little conviction in her heart regarding the success of her mission. She knew Clara too well.

The decision was thus made, and Eleanor began her preparations for the journey to Kent. The intervening days were filled with a flurry of activity, though mostly of a mundane nature: packing her modest wardrobe, writing letters of apology to Aunt Markham for her temporary absence, and enduring further visits from Mr. Hemlock, who, having heard a whisper of her impending departure, returned with renewed vigour to offer advice on the perils of travel and the proper selection of a reliable post-boy.

“One cannot be too careful on the roads, Miss Eleanor,” he declared one morning, holding forth over a cup of tea. “Highwaymen, you know, are still very much a nuisance, especially in certain stretches of the country. And the inns! One must choose with the utmost discretion. I recall a journey I once undertook to Derbyshire, where the sheets, I assure you, had not been changed for a week, and the landlord was a most villainous fellow, with a squint in one eye and a habit of coughing quite alarmingly. Indeed, one must exercise eternal vigilance. Perhaps you should take a strong masculine escort, Miss Eleanor? My cousin, the solicitor from Bristol, often travels, and I am sure he would be delighted to accompany a lady of your refined sensibilities.”

Eleanor merely smiled, a little wearily, and assured him that her aunt had made all the necessary arrangements for a safe and comfortable journey. The thought of travelling with Mr. Hemlock’s Bristol cousin, however amiable he might be, was enough to make her prefer the risks of highwaymen.

Finally, the day of departure arrived. The hired post-chaise stood at the door, its horses impatiently pawing the ground. Eleanor embraced her mother warmly, assured her again of her speedy return, and promised a diligent correspondence. With a final wave to Mrs. Vernon, who stood at the window dabbing her eyes with a lace handkerchief, and a polite nod to Mr. Hemlock, who had, of course, presented himself to offer a final round of unsolicited advice, Eleanor stepped into the carriage.

The journey proved to be long, as expected, but otherwise uneventful. The roads were in reasonably good repair, the post-boys efficient, and the inns, contrary to Mr. Hemlock’s dire predictions, clean and welcoming enough for a weary traveller. Eleanor found herself able to read, to think, and to gather her thoughts for the delicate task that lay ahead. She thought of Clara, so full of life, and of Lieutenant Davies, whom she had never met, but already felt a certain degree of sympathy for, knowing the formidable opposition he faced from her aunt.

When at last the chaise turned into the long drive leading to Daventry Park, the sun was beginning to set, casting long shadows across the familiar lawns. The house, a handsome Georgian structure, stood silhouetted against the evening sky, a picture of solid respectability and comfortable elegance. As the carriage drew up to the front door, it was opened almost immediately, and Mrs. Daventry herself stood there, a look of profound relief on her face.

“Eleanor, my dearest girl!” she cried, rushing forward to embrace her niece. “Thank heavens you are here at last! I have been counting the minutes, I assure you. Come in, come in! You must be quite exhausted from your journey.”

Eleanor returned her aunt’s embrace warmly. “Indeed, Aunt, I am a little tired, but quite well. It is so good to see you again.”

Mrs. Daventry led her into the well-lit hall, where a maid was already waiting to take Eleanor’s cloak and bonnet. “And how was your journey? Did you encounter any difficulties? The roads, you know, can be quite dreadful this time of year.”

“No difficulties at all, Aunt, I am happy to report,” Eleanor replied, smiling. “The weather was fine, and the post-boys quite capable.”

“Excellent, excellent,” Mrs. Daventry said, still fluttering with an anxious energy. “Come into the drawing-room; Clara is there, and she is quite eager to see you. Though, I must warn you, my dear, she is in a most singular state of mind. But perhaps your arrival will prove a distraction; a fresh face, a new voice, might do wonders.”

Eleanor followed her aunt into the drawing-room, a spacious and elegantly furnished room, where a fire crackled cheerfully in the grate. Seated by the fire, pretending to be engrossed in a book, was her cousin, Clara. At the sound of their entrance, Clara looked up, and her face, though presently clouded with a touch of defiance, brightened considerably upon seeing Eleanor.

“Eleanor! Oh, how wonderful!” Clara exclaimed, rising quickly and rushing to her. She was a beautiful young woman of nineteen, with sparkling dark eyes and a cascade of glossy brown curls. “I had quite given you up for lost! Mama has been fretting over your journey all day.”

“It is a long way, Clara,” Eleanor said, embracing her cousin with genuine affection. “But I am here now, and very glad to be so.”

Mrs. Daventry, having observed this exchange with a slightly hopeful air, cleared her throat. “Now that Eleanor is here, Clara, perhaps we might have a little peace. I have ordered some tea for Eleanor, and she must be quite ready for refreshment.”

Clara gave her mother a rather pointed look, which Eleanor did not miss. “Indeed, Mama,” Clara said, a trace of sarcasm in her tone. “Eleanor will doubtless have much to tell us of the world beyond Daventry Park. Perhaps she has even encountered a dashing young officer upon her travels!”

Mrs. Daventry gasped, clearly taken aback by this remark. “Clara, do not be so impertinent! Eleanor has travelled for a specific purpose, as you well know, and not for idle gossip.”

“Oh, indeed, Mama,” Clara retorted, with a toss of her head. “A specific purpose, to be sure. To rescue me from the clutches of a villain, no doubt. Am I to be locked in my room, Eleanor, until I promise to renounce all connection with Lieutenant Davies, and agree to marry some dull, elderly baronet whom Mama deems acceptable?”

Eleanor felt a wave of weariness wash over her. This was precisely the sort of situation she had feared. Clara was clearly on the defensive, and her aunt was equally determined to assert her authority. It would require considerable diplomacy to navigate these turbulent waters.

“Clara, my dear,” Eleanor began gently, taking her cousin’s hand. “Let us not be hasty in our conclusions. I have only just arrived, and I confess I am quite fatigued. Perhaps we may discuss these matters more calmly after I have had some tea and a moment to collect myself. I am sure there are two sides to every story, and I am very much looking forward to hearing yours.”

Clara looked at Eleanor, a flicker of surprise in her eyes, as if she had expected immediate condemnation. Her expression softened slightly. “Very well, Eleanor,” she said, though still with a hint of petulance. “But you will find that Mama has quite distorted the truth of the matter, as is her custom when she is displeased.”

“Clara!” Mrs. Daventry exclaimed, her face flushing. “That is an outrageous accusation! I have done nothing but state the plain facts of the situation, which are, alas, quite dire.”

Eleanor gently squeezed Clara’s hand, then turned to her aunt. “Aunt Daventry, I am sure Clara does not mean to imply any malice. She is merely distressed, as are you. Perhaps we should all endeavour to speak with a little more temperance.”

A maid entered at that moment with a tea-tray, laden with sandwiches and cakes, offering a welcome respite from the escalating tension. Eleanor gratefully accepted a cup of tea, its warmth a soothing contrast to the chill of the evening air and the heat of the familial dispute. She observed Clara, who had retreated to her seat by the fire, casting defiant glances at her mother, and Mrs. Daventry, who was now engaged in an agitated rustling of her silk dress, her lips pressed into a thin line. It was clear that her mission, if it was to have any hope of success, would require more than just calm good sense; it would demand the most delicate touch, and perhaps a little subterfuge. Eleanor knew that she must first gain Clara’s confidence, and then, perhaps, subtly influence her, rather than openly oppose her.

The evening passed in a somewhat constrained manner, with Mrs. Daventry making repeated, though veiled, references to Clara’s foolishness, and Clara responding with equally veiled, though unmistakable, expressions of resentment. Eleanor did her best to steer the conversation towards more neutral topics, enquiring about local gossip, the new fashions in London, and the progress of Mrs. Daventry’s garden, but the undercurrent of tension was ever present. Lieutenant Davies, though unmentioned directly by name, hung over the room like an unspoken guest.

Finally, to Eleanor’s relief, the hour for retiring arrived. As she was shown to her room, a comfortable chamber overlooking the gardens, Mrs. Daventry lingered for a moment at the door.

“Eleanor, my dear,” she whispered, “I truly hope you can make her see sense. She respects you, I know she does. You were always such a sensible child, and now a sensible woman. This young man, he is quite unsuitable, I assure you. He has absolutely no expectations beyond his pay, and his family, though they live in a decent house in a market town, are quite without distinction. Imagine Clara, married to a man who must live on a lieutenant’s pittance! It is quite unbearable to contemplate. I have always wished for her to marry well, you know, to a man of substance and standing, who could offer her a secure future. A future free from the anxieties of limited means. Do talk to her, Eleanor; appeal to her reason, if she has any left.”

“I will do my best, Aunt,” Eleanor promised, offering a reassuring smile, though inwardly she doubted the efficacy of reason when pitted against strong affection. She knew Clara well enough to understand that emotion, not logic, would be her primary guide.

Once alone, Eleanor unpacked her things, her mind still preoccupied with the delicate situation. She looked out the window at the moonlit gardens, the scent of jasmine drifting faintly on the night air. Clara’s situation resonated with a quiet ache in Eleanor’s own heart. She too had once cherished affections for a gentleman of modest fortune, a curate, whose prospects had been deemed insufficient by her own family. That attachment, though never openly forbidden, had gently, imperceptibly, faded under the quiet pressure of practicality and circumstance. She knew the pain of such a surrender, even if it had been, in her case, a voluntary one. Could she, therefore, with a clear conscience, urge Clara to abandon a love that, however imprudent, brought her joy?

This was the question that troubled Eleanor’s mind as she prepared for bed. Her aunt’s expectations were clear, and socially, entirely justifiable. But Clara’s happiness, if indeed it lay with this Lieutenant Davies, was surely a consideration too. Eleanor sighed, knowing that her task would be far more complicated than merely imparting 'calm good sense'. It would involve navigating the intricate landscape of a young woman's heart, a territory often resistant to the dictates of worldly wisdom. She resolved that her first step must be to listen, truly listen, to Clara’s own perspective, before offering any advice, however well-intentioned. Only then might she hope to be of genuine service to her beloved cousin. She prayed for wisdom, and for the strength to act with both principle and compassion. With these thoughts, Eleanor at last sought the repose of sleep, knowing that the morrow would bring new challenges, and a deeper plunge into the romantic entanglement that had brought her to Daventry Park.

Eleanor, whose internal barometer registered a steady rise in the gentleman’s self-congratulation, managed a delicate cough, a subtle indication of her polite attentiveness, though her thoughts were still firmly tethered to the unread letter. It lay upon her lap, a quiet, rectangular presence, its seal unbroken, its contents a mystery. She wondered idly from whom it might have come, and whether its message would prove to be of consequence, or merely another circular of social triviality. The gentleman, however, seemed to interpret her cough as an invitation, a tacit request for further elaboration, for he promptly cleared his throat and continued with renewed vigour, his gaze fixed upon a distant, perhaps imagined, prospect of perfect domestic rectitude.
