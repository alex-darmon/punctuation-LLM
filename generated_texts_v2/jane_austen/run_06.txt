"No news yet, my dear?" she enquired kindly, as Clara buttered her toast with a rather distracted air.

"Not yet, Aunt," Clara replied, a hint of disappointment in her voice, though she quickly added, "but it is still early, is it not? Gentlemen do not usually send their cards before noon."

Eleanor, reading a volume of essays by a Scottish philosopher, merely offered a quiet, "Indeed, Clara, such matters require a certain decorum. Patience is a virtue, as is often observed."

"Patience is all very well, Eleanor," Clara retorted, "but one cannot help but feel a little impatience on such an occasion! It is not every day that one meets a gentleman so attentive as Mr. Harrington."

Mrs. Selby smiled. "No, indeed, Clara, it is not. But a little suspense, my love, only heightens the pleasure of the eventual arrival. In the meantime, we have our usual duties. Eleanor, perhaps you would assist me in sorting through these accounts this morning? My eyes are not what they once were for such fine print."

Eleanor readily agreed, laying aside her book. She found a quiet satisfaction in the methodical task of balancing figures, a certain orderliness that appealed to her rational mind. While Clara flitted about, attending to her watercolours and needlework with a restless energy, Eleanor diligently applied herself to the household books. It was a useful occupation, and one which she felt provided a more tangible contribution to the family's welfare than merely waiting for a gentleman's card.

The morning passed slowly for Clara, and more steadily for Eleanor. Towards midday, however, a sound from the gravel drive sent Clara flying to the window. "A carriage! Oh, Aunt, it is a carriage!" she exclaimed, her heart fluttering with excitement.

Mrs. Selby, who had been on the point of requesting a new supply of sugar from the village grocer, paused, her attention immediately caught. "Indeed, Clara? Is it a private equipage, or merely a tradesman's cart?"

"It is a private carriage, Aunt! And a very handsome one, too! I believe, I truly believe, it must be Mr. Harrington's!" Clara's voice was filled with a triumphant certainty.

A moment later, the parlour maid, Martha, entered, a silver card tray in her hand. "A card, Madam," she announced, presenting it to Mrs. Selby, "from Mr. Harrington, for yourself and the young ladies. And Mr. Thompson's card, for Miss Eleanor."

Clara gasped with delight. "Mr. Harrington! I knew it! Oh, Aunt, how perfectly delightful!" She clapped her hands together, a vision of unadulterated joy.

Mrs. Selby, with a serene smile, examined the cards. "Very proper, indeed. Mr. Harrington, with his compliments, and enquiring after our health after the ball. And Mr. Thompson, too, Eleanor; that is very pleasing." She handed the cards to the girls.

Clara eagerly took Mr. Harrington's card, holding it as if it were a rare jewel. "He is so very thoughtful, Aunt! He remembers everything! He is truly the most attentive of gentlemen."

Eleanor, meanwhile, took Mr. Thompson's card, a faint, almost imperceptible smile playing on her lips. It was a simple card, neatly engraved, but its arrival held a deeper significance for her than the mere formality of a social call. It was a continuation, a quiet acknowledgement of the conversation they had shared. She felt a pleasant warmth steal over her, a sense of quiet gratification.

"Well, my dears," Mrs. Selby declared, "this calls for a little planning. We must, of course, receive Mr. Harrington, and Mr. Thompson. Martha, tell Mrs. Giles that we shall require tea and some of her best biscuits, and perhaps a small cake. And the drawing-room fire must be properly attended to. Clara, my love, you must put on your prettiest morning dress, and Eleanor, something suitably elegant, but perhaps a little less severe than your usual attire."

Clara was already half-way to the door. "Oh, Aunt, I know just the thing! My new lavender muslin, it will be quite perfect! I must hasten to change!"

Eleanor, more composed, merely said, "As you wish, Aunt. I shall endeavour to select an appropriate garment." She then retired to her room, not with Clara's giddy anticipation, but with a thoughtful composure. Mr. Thompson's visit was not unexpected, given the nature of their conversation, but it was nonetheless a welcome development. She wondered what new intellectual ground they might cover, what further insights into literature or philosophy he might offer. Her aunt's concerns about her social standing, while not entirely dismissed, seemed less pressing in the face of such a promising intellectual exchange.

When the gentlemen arrived, precisely at the appointed hour, Clara was radiant in her lavender muslin, her curls perfectly arranged, and her eyes sparkling with an eagerness that was entirely charming. Mr. Harrington, indeed, was everything she had described: handsome, impeccably dressed, and possessed of a refined air that spoke of good breeding and considerable fortune. He greeted Mrs. Selby with a polite bow, and Clara with a smile that conveyed a flattering degree of particular admiration.

"Mrs. Selby, Miss Clara," he began, his voice smooth and cultivated, "I trust you both recovered well from the fatigues of last night's most delightful assembly? I found myself reflecting upon the pleasantness of our dances, Miss Clara, and felt compelled to enquire after your health this morning."

Clara curtsied gracefully. "Mr. Harrington, you are too kind! I assure you, I am perfectly well, and quite invigorated by the memory of such an agreeable evening. Your attention to the country dances was quite remarkable."

Mr. Thompson, who had entered quietly behind his more dashing friend, now stepped forward to greet Eleanor. He was not as outwardly striking as Mr. Harrington, perhaps, his features being more earnest than classically handsome, but his eyes, behind spectacles, held a warmth and intelligence that Eleanor found immediately appealing. He was dressed neatly, if not with the same fashionable flair as Mr. Harrington, in a dark coat and modest cravat.

"Miss Eleanor," he said, with a slight bow, "I hope I do not intrude upon your morning. I felt it incumbent upon me to express my gratitude for your most engaging conversation last evening. I found our discussion of the Stoic philosophers quite stimulating, and I have since pondered some of your observations with considerable interest."

Eleanor offered him a sincere smile. "Mr. Thompson, you are most welcome. I assure you, the pleasure was entirely mine. It is not often that one encounters a gentleman so well-versed in such matters, and one so willing to engage in a genuine exchange of ideas. I, too, have been reflecting upon our conversation, particularly your insights into the practical application of Seneca's teachings."

Mrs. Selby, observing the two pairs of conversationalists, felt a sense of quiet satisfaction. Clara and Mr. Harrington were engaged in a lively discussion of local gossip and the upcoming season, their interaction sparkling with a conventional charm. Eleanor and Mr. Thompson, though their tones were more subdued, seemed equally engrossed, their conversation taking a more serious, intellectual turn. It was, perhaps, not the most direct path to an advantageous match for Eleanor, as she had often observed, but there was an undeniable compatibility between them, a shared intellectual curiosity that could not be easily dismissed.

Tea was brought in, and Mrs. Giles's biscuits, light and crisp, were much admired. Mr. Harrington, ever the attentive guest, ensured Clara's cup was always full, and complimented her on her drawing-room accomplishments, admiring a small sketch of a cottage which she had recently completed. "Miss Clara, your talent is truly remarkable," he declared, holding the sketch at arm's length. "The delicacy of your hand, and the accuracy of your eye, are quite beyond compare. It quite reminds me of the charming cottages upon my own estate, which, I venture to say, would be greatly enhanced by a similar artistic rendering."

Clara blushed prettily. "You are too kind, Mr. Harrington. It is merely a small amusement, I assure you. But I am delighted that you find it agreeable."

Meanwhile, Mr. Thompson and Eleanor had moved on to the subject of contemporary poets, a topic which allowed Eleanor to express her own considered opinions with a freedom she rarely felt in mixed company. "I confess," she said, her voice thoughtful, "that while I appreciate the elegance of certain modern verses, I often find them lacking the profound philosophical depth of the ancients. There is a certain superficiality, a focus on transient emotions, that does not quite resonate with my own inclinations."

Mr. Thompson nodded enthusiastically. "Precisely, Miss Eleanor! You articulate my own sentiments with remarkable clarity. While a pleasing rhyme and a tender sentiment have their place, the truly great works of literature, I believe, are those which grapple with the enduring questions of humanity, those which offer wisdom rather than mere entertainment. I have often found myself returning to the words of Horace, finding new solace and instruction in his reflections on life's brevity and the pursuit of virtue."

"Indeed," Eleanor replied, her eyes bright with intellectual interest. "There is a timelessness to such works, is there not? A universality of human experience that transcends the vagaries of fashion and social convention. It is a rare privilege to discuss such matters with one who shares such an appreciation."

Mrs. Selby, having settled Clara and Mr. Harrington in a comfortable tête-à-tête, now discreetly observed Eleanor and Mr. Thompson. While she could not follow the intricacies of their philosophical discourse, she could discern the animated expressions on their faces, the evident pleasure they took in each other's company. It was not, perhaps, the boisterous laughter and flirtatious glances she might have hoped for, but there was a quiet intensity, a genuine connection, that spoke volumes. Perhaps, she mused, a gentleman who valued profundity might indeed be a suitable match for Eleanor, despite her initial reservations. It was certainly a more substantial foundation than mere superficial charm, though that, too, had its undoubted advantages.

The visit continued for a considerable time, much to Clara's gratification. Mr. Harrington spoke further of his estate, of improvements he intended to make, and subtly implied a desire for Miss Clara's aesthetic input. He spoke of equestrian pursuits, which Clara, though not herself a rider, listened to with feigned interest, sensing that it was a subject close to his heart. He also recounted a humorous anecdote from a recent London season, which amused Clara greatly and made her feel as if she were already part of his inner circle.

Mr. Thompson, meanwhile, revealed himself to be not only a scholar but also a keen observer of human nature, his comments on the characters in classical literature often extending to perceptive remarks on the foibles and virtues of their contemporary society. Eleanor found herself increasingly drawn to his gentle humour and his thoughtful insights. Their conversation flowed effortlessly from one topic to another, touching upon history, poetry, and even the natural sciences, a breadth of knowledge that Eleanor found refreshingly stimulating.

At length, the gentlemen rose to take their leave. Mr. Harrington, with a deferential bow to Mrs. Selby and a lingering glance at Clara, expressed his profound enjoyment of their visit. "It has been a truly restorative experience, Mrs. Selby, and Miss Clara," he said, his voice full of polite sincerity. "I sincerely hope that we may have the pleasure of calling upon you again very soon. Perhaps, if the weather holds, a drive through the lanes might be a pleasant diversion one afternoon?"

Clara's eyes lit up. "That would be delightful, Mr. Harrington! Most delightful indeed!"

Mr. Thompson, in his turn, addressed Eleanor. "Miss Eleanor, I thank you again for your most edifying conversation. I confess, I leave with several new avenues of thought to explore, and I hope you will permit me the indulgence of continuing our discourse at a future opportunity."

"Indeed, Mr. Thompson," Eleanor replied, a genuine warmth in her tone. "I should be most pleased. It is a rare pleasure to find a mind so congenial."

After the gentlemen had departed, Clara fairly danced about the drawing room. "Oh, Aunt Selby, did you hear? A drive! Mr. Harrington wishes to take me for a drive! Is that not the most charming of invitations? And he admired my sketch, he truly did! He has such excellent taste, and such a discerning eye for beauty. I am sure he meant it as an invitation to his estate, eventually."

Mrs. Selby smiled, a knowing satisfaction on her features. "Indeed, my dear; it is a very promising development. Mr. Harrington's intentions seem quite clear, and very gratifying. You have conducted yourself admirably, Clara." She then turned to Eleanor, whose expression was one of quiet contentment. "And you, Eleanor, seemed equally engaged with Mr. Thompson. He is a very respectable young man, and his admiration for your intellect is, I must confess, a rather unusual, though perhaps not unwelcome, foundation for an acquaintance. Did you find him agreeable?"

"Very agreeable, Aunt," Eleanor confirmed. "His mind is sharp, and his scholarship extensive. I found his observations on the moral dilemmas presented in ancient tragedies particularly insightful."

Mrs. Selby sighed contentedly. "Well, that is excellent, my dear. Perhaps, after all, there is a gentleman who appreciates your unique qualities. One can never quite predict the path of love, or indeed, of suitability, but a shared intellectual pursuit, while not always the first consideration, can certainly form a most enduring bond." She looked from her vivacious niece to her contemplative one, feeling a profound sense of hope for both their futures, despite the very different paths they seemed inclined to follow. The afternoon had certainly been a success, leaving behind a pleasant anticipation of further calls and deeper connections. The air in the drawing-room seemed to hum with the possibilities of impending engagements, of hearts slowly, steadily drawing closer in the intricate dance of genteel society.

Mr. Harrington, on the other hand, has a grace about him that is quite irresistible."

The carriage at last drew up before their own comfortable dwelling, and the sisters, assisted by the coachman, alighted with an agreeable sense of fatigue. The warmth of the fire in the drawing room was a welcome comfort after the chill of the journey, and the familiar surroundings seemed to soothe the last vestiges of the evening’s excitement.

Her thoughts, however, were not of bows or dances, but of Mr. Thompson's earnest, intelligent eyes, and the unexpected pleasure of finding a mind so attuned to her own. It was not, perhaps, the admiration Mrs. Selby sought for her, but it was admiration of a different, and perhaps more profound, sort.

"Very well, my dears," she said, accepting a glass of ratafia from Martha, "we have all had a most excellent evening, in our various ways. Now to rest, for tomorrow will bring its own concerns. I shall look forward to hearing whether Mr. Harrington sends his card, Clara; that will be a most gratifying indication of his intentions."
