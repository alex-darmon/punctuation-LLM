A heavy burden upon your poor father, I should imagine, to provide for so many. And what of yourself, Miss Bennet? Are you to follow your sister’s example and seek a rich man, regardless of his family?”

“I have no such design, madam,” Elizabeth replied, her voice firm, “nor any expectation of a rich husband at all. I am quite content as I am.”

Lady Catherine emitted a sound that was half sniff, half snort. “Content! Young women are rarely content without a proper establishment. It is their duty to marry well, and a duty to their family, for their own comfort, and for the benefit of society. Such sentiments are romantic nonsense, fit only for novels. You have no fortune, I presume?”

“None at all, madam,” Elizabeth confirmed, without a hint of apology.

“And your father’s estate, I am informed, is entailed away from the female line,” Lady Catherine continued, pressing the point, “to Mr. Collins, in fact. Is that not so?”

“It is,” Elizabeth agreed, looking briefly at her cousin, who shifted uncomfortably under her Ladyship’s gaze.

“A most unfortunate arrangement for daughters,” Lady Catherine pronounced, “and one that ought never to have been permitted. Had I been in charge of such matters, I assure you, entails would be managed with far greater discretion. But then, in Hertfordshire, they do things differently, I suppose. And you, Miss Bennet, what are your prospects? You have no expectations beyond your father’s death, after which you will be quite dependent, will you not?”

“I have never allowed myself to dwell upon such disagreeable matters, madam,” Elizabeth said, choosing her words carefully, “and I have every hope that I shall always be able to provide for myself.”

Lady Catherine gave her another long, scrutinising look. “Provide for yourself? What nonsense is this? Young women of good birth do not ‘provide for themselves.’ They marry. Or they live as dependents upon their married relations. Unless, of course, they are so plain or so ill-tempered as to be quite unmarriageable, in which case they may take up some modest employment, perhaps as a governess or a companion. You do not, I trust, contemplate such a degradation?”

“I have contemplated nothing yet, madam,” Elizabeth replied, her patience, though admirable, beginning to fray slightly at the edges. “My future is, as yet, entirely undecided.”

“Undecided!” cried Mr. Collins, aghast. “But my dear cousin, how can you speak so lightly of your future in the presence of her Ladyship, who embodies all the prudence and foresight that a young woman could wish to emulate? Your future, as you well know, is to be settled most advantageously, if only you would be guided by the soundest counsel!” He looked imploringly at Lady Catherine, hoping for a nod of approval.

Lady Catherine ignored him once more. “This independent spirit, Miss Bennet, is not at all becoming in a young woman. It suggests a want of proper modesty, and a dangerous tendency towards unorthodoxy. You must learn to be more pliable, more amenable to the wisdom of your elders. Has your mother not instructed you in these matters? Or your father, perhaps, with his peculiar sense of humour?”

“My parents have always allowed me a certain latitude of thought, madam,” Elizabeth stated, her voice even, “and I value it highly.”

Lady Catherine huffed. “Latitude of thought! That is precisely what leads young women astray. They read novels, they form their own opinions, and they imagine themselves above the common duties of their sex. It is a modern innovation, and a most pernicious one. In my day, young women were taught decorum, silence, and obedience. They learnt to play the pianoforte, certainly, but only to entertain their families, not to gain a reputation for accomplishment, which is, in any case, quite unnecessary for a girl of moderate expectations. Do you spend much time reading, Miss Bennet?”

“A great deal, madam,” Elizabeth answered truthfully, “I am very fond of books.”

“Indeed,” said Lady Catherine, her tone suggesting that this was a confession of some minor vice. “And what sort of books do you read? Not those frivolous romances, I trust, that fill young ladies’ heads with absurd notions of love and elopement? Do you confine yourself to works of instruction, or perhaps sermons?”

Elizabeth smiled faintly. “I confess, madam, that I read all manner of books, according to my humour. Sometimes histories, sometimes essays, and yes, sometimes novels, for a pleasing diversion.”

“A pleasing diversion!” Lady Catherine scoffed. “There are few pleasing diversions for a young lady of sense, save for managing a household, supervising her staff, and ensuring the comfort of her family. I fear, Miss Bennet, that your upbringing has been rather deficient in some essential respects. It is a pity, for with proper guidance, you might have been made into something tolerable. As it is, you appear to have been allowed to run wild, like a country wench.”

Mr. Collins, perceiving Elizabeth’s slight colour, hurried to interject. “My Lady, if I may humbly offer a counterpoint, my cousin Elizabeth, though perhaps possessing a vivacity which some might deem… spirited, is nevertheless a young woman of excellent principles and a most affectionate disposition! Her cheerfulness, I assure you, often enlivens our little parsonage, and she is quite adept at many domestic duties, though she might not always boast of them. Indeed, she has been most kind in arranging the flowers for our supper table this very evening!”

Lady Catherine merely raised an imperious hand, silencing him. “Flowers, Mr. Collins, are a mere trifle. Any housemaid can arrange flowers. I am speaking of more substantial accomplishments, of those qualities that contribute to a young woman’s true worth. But perhaps we have exhausted this topic for the moment. Tell me, Miss Bennet, have you any acquaintances in this neighbourhood apart from ourselves? Do you know Colonel Fitzwilliam well?”

Elizabeth’s heart gave a little flutter at the mention of Darcy’s cousin, but she managed to maintain her outward calm. “I have had the pleasure of conversing with Colonel Fitzwilliam on several occasions, madam,” she replied, “since our arrival at Rosings.”

“He is a most amiable young man,” Lady Catherine declared, “and a great favourite of mine. He has a considerable fortune, you know, being a younger son, though he is destined for a rich wife, as indeed he ought to be. I take a particular interest in his welfare. He is often here, you see, at Rosings, where he is always welcome. And Mr. Darcy, of course, is also a frequent visitor. Do you know Mr. Darcy, Miss Bennet?”

A deeper flush now rose to Elizabeth’s face, betraying her despite her best efforts. “I have indeed had the acquaintance of Mr. Darcy for some months, madam,” she admitted, her voice perhaps a shade less steady than before. “He is a particular friend of Mr. Bingley’s, and we met first in Hertfordshire.”

“Ah, yes, in Hertfordshire,” Lady Catherine mused, her eyes keenly observing Elizabeth’s reactions. “A rather inferior part of the country, I gather, for bringing out young ladies. And you say he is a friend of Mr. Bingley’s. That is unfortunate, for Mr. Bingley is a most unsuitable connection for a man of Mr. Darcy’s standing. However, Mr. Darcy is a man of excellent judgment, and he knows his own consequence. He is, moreover, quite devoted to his family, and to his cousin, my own daughter, Miss de Bourgh. You have met Miss de Bourgh, I suppose?”

“I have had that honour, madam,” Elizabeth answered, thinking of the pale, quiet young woman she had seen earlier.

“She is a remarkably amiable young lady,” Lady Catherine continued, with a proprietorial air, “and of course, she is destined for Mr. Darcy. It has been a cherished wish in both our families for many years. Indeed, it was quite settled between my late sister, Lady Anne, and myself, when they were mere infants. Such connections are of the utmost importance, Miss Bennet, for preserving the integrity of noble families. Do you not agree?”

“I have no experience in such matters, madam,” Elizabeth responded carefully, “and therefore no opinion to offer.”

“No opinion!” Lady Catherine exclaimed, her voice rising slightly. “That is mere evasiveness, girl! Every rational creature must have an opinion on the proper ordering of society and the maintenance of family prestige. It is a fundamental principle. But perhaps you are simply too ignorant to understand such things. Mr. Darcy, I am sure, would be quite shocked to hear of such an indifferent attitude.”

Mr. Collins once again saw an opportunity to chime in. “My Lady, if I may just add, Mr. Darcy, with his profound understanding of such elevated subjects, would, I am certain, agree entirely with your Ladyship’s most eloquent pronouncements! He often speaks of the importance of maintaining proper distinctions and avoiding unsuitable alliances. Indeed, I have heard him say as much on several occasions, though perhaps not in such elegant terms as your Ladyship employs!”

Lady Catherine merely gave a sharp nod, accepting Mr. Collins’s tribute as her due. “Indeed, Mr. Collins, Mr. Darcy is a man of sense, and he appreciates the wisdom of ancient families. He knows his duty. Now, Miss Bennet, I must ask you, for it is a matter of considerable speculation in the neighbourhood, how long do you intend to remain at Rosings? Your visit has already extended somewhat beyond the ordinary bounds of a casual call, has it not? And your continued presence here, I must confess, has given rise to some… conjectures.”

Elizabeth felt a sudden chill, a premonition of some unpleasantness. “I had intended to remain for another week, madam,” she said, trying to keep her tone neutral, “unless my presence here causes any inconvenience.”

“Inconvenience?” Lady Catherine repeated, with a scornful laugh. “It is not a matter of inconvenience, girl, but of propriety. My nephew, Mr. Darcy, is staying here, as you know. It would be highly imprudent, not to say improper, for a young woman of your situation to remain for an undue length of time under the same roof as a gentleman of his consequence, especially when there are, as I have just explained, certain expectations regarding his future. People will talk, Miss Bennet, and scandal, once started, is difficult to suppress. You must consider your reputation, if not his.”

The sheer audacity of Lady Catherine’s implication, the unvarnished condescension, finally stirred Elizabeth’s quiet defiance into something more overtly formidable. Her eyes flashed, though her voice remained steady. “Madam, I assure you, my reputation is entirely safe under my own care, and I have no doubt that Mr. Darcy’s is equally so. I am here by invitation from Mr. Collins, my cousin, whose hospitality I trust is not entirely dependent upon my social standing.”

Mr. Collins looked utterly terrified, his mouth opening and closing like a fish out of water. He glanced frantically from Elizabeth to Lady Catherine, clearly wishing the floor would swallow him whole.

Lady Catherine, for her part, appeared genuinely taken aback by Elizabeth’s spirited reply. Her formidable features hardened, and her eyes glittered with displeasure. “Upon my word, Miss Bennet! Such insolence! Such impertinence! I have never in my life met with such a forward, such an opinionated young woman! Do you know to whom you are speaking? Do you realise the impropriety of your tone?”

“I am perfectly aware, madam, to whom I am speaking,” Elizabeth stated, refusing to back down, “and I trust I am speaking with all due respect for your rank, though perhaps not with the servility you might expect. My opinions, such as they are, I hold freely, and I have never learnt to dissemble them.”

“Dissemble them!” Lady Catherine echoed, aghast. “But it is the duty of a young woman to dissemble her opinions, when those opinions are improper or contrary to the expectations of her superiors! This is a mark of a truly ill-bred mind, Miss Bennet, to imagine that one’s own petty sentiments are worthy of public utterance, especially when contradicted by the wisdom of those who have seen more of the world, and possess a far greater understanding of its intricacies. You seem to forget yourself entirely.”

“I assure you, madam, I do not forget myself,” Elizabeth responded, with a small, ironic smile, “I merely remember that I am a rational creature, and therefore responsible for my own thoughts and words.”

Lady Catherine was, for a moment, speechless. Her face grew crimson, and she drew herself up to her full, imposing height. “This is insufferable! Utterly insufferable! Mr. Collins, why have you brought such a forward, such an objectionable young woman into my presence? She is a disgrace to her sex, and a discredit to your family! I suppose this is the sort of behaviour one might expect from a young lady raised in a remote country parish, without proper guidance or acquaintance with the decorum of true society. She has no notion of what is due to her betters!”

Mr. Collins, finally finding his voice, stammered, “My Lady, I assure you, my cousin Elizabeth is usually most mild and agreeable! I cannot account for this sudden… this unexpected… departure from her customary deference! She has always shown the greatest respect for my patroness, indeed, she has often spoken of your Ladyship’s discernment and generosity!” His voice trailed off into a whimper, as he realised his feeble attempts were only highlighting Elizabeth’s supposed transgressions.

“Silence, Mr. Collins!” Lady Catherine commanded, with a furious glare. “You are as foolish as she is impertinent! But I shall not suffer such behaviour in my house. You may take your leave, Miss Bennet, at your earliest convenience. Indeed, the sooner the better, for your presence here is a positive disturbance to the harmony of my household. I cannot permit my nephew, Mr. Darcy, to be exposed to such impertinent notions.”

“Madam,” Elizabeth said, rising slowly to her feet, her composure now perfectly restored, though her spirit was certainly roused, “I had already informed you of my intention to depart in a week’s time. Your commands, therefore, are quite superfluous, though I assure you, I shall endeavour to hasten my arrangements.” She made a slight, dignified curtsy, and then turned to Mr. Collins, her gaze conveying a silent apology for the awkwardness she had caused him, however justified.

Mr. Collins, who seemed on the verge of apoplexy, could only manage a series of incoherent sounds, fluttering his hands in distress. Lady Catherine, still fuming, merely pointed a rigid finger towards the door.

“Go, girl!” she snapped, “and remember that you have tried my patience to its utmost limit! I have never been so insulted in my own drawing-room!”

Elizabeth, without another word, left the room, her head held high. The door closed quietly behind her, leaving Mr. Collins alone with his formidable patroness, whose temper was undoubtedly still at a fever pitch. Elizabeth walked quickly through the silent hall, her heart still thrumming with a mixture of anger and triumph. She had never before so openly defied a person of such rank, and though she knew her actions would undoubtedly draw the severest censure from Mr. Collins, and likely from the world at large, she could not help but feel a certain satisfaction. To have stood her ground against Lady Catherine de Bourgh, to have asserted her own mind and spirit, was a small victory, but a significant one for her.

She retreated to the small chamber assigned to her, where she sat for some time, reflecting on the extraordinary interview. The very absurdity of Lady Catherine’s pronouncements, her unshakeable conviction in her own superiority, and her profound sense of entitlement, all contributed to a spectacle that might have been amusing, had it not been so intensely personal. Elizabeth considered the implications of Lady Catherine’s words, especially those concerning Mr. Darcy and Miss de Bourgh. The long-standing expectation of their union, the familial desire for such an alliance, was now openly declared. It cast a new light upon Mr. Darcy’s actions, and indeed upon his presence at Rosings. Was he here merely to indulge his aunt, or was he actively pursuing this long-desired arrangement?

The thought brought a pang to her heart, a sensation she quickly sought to dismiss. What could it matter to her? Mr. Darcy was far above her in rank and fortune; any notion of attachment between them had been irrevocably shattered by his odious proposal and her subsequent, vehement refusal. Yet, the memory of his recent visits, his attentiveness, his almost gentle demeanor, still lingered, perplexing her. He had seemed so much improved from their first acquaintance, so much less haughty. Had it all been merely a temporary abatement of his pride, or was there something truly changed within him?

She decided that she would indeed depart from Rosings as soon as was practicable. To remain under Lady Catherine’s roof, especially after such an exchange, would be an unbearable trial. She would write to Jane, explaining her decision, and requesting that she inform their mother of her earlier return. The thought of Jane’s gentle understanding was a comforting one amidst the turmoil of her recent encounter.

The remainder of the evening passed without further incident, at least for Elizabeth. She chose to remain in her room, feigning a slight headache, a polite excuse that would spare her from further company, particularly that of Mr. Collins, whose distress she could well imagine. He would be in an agony of embarrassment, fearing Lady Catherine’s continued displeasure and perhaps even the loss of his living. Such a prospect was indeed dire for him, and Elizabeth felt a fleeting pity for her cousin, even while she silently congratulated herself on her own resolution.

The next morning, Elizabeth found herself avoiding Mr. Collins’s eye at breakfast, though he scarcely dared to look at anyone himself, his countenance betraying a night of profound anxiety. Lady Catherine, surprisingly, was not present at the morning meal, a rare occurrence that spoke volumes of her lingering displeasure. Miss de Bourgh, as usual, sat quietly, consuming her toast with a delicate precision, seemingly oblivious to the undercurrents of tension that pervaded the room. Colonel Fitzwilliam, however, appeared to be in a more contemplative mood than usual, glancing frequently at Elizabeth, as if trying to decipher the cause of the previous evening’s disturbance. He seemed to possess a keen insight, a silent observation that Elizabeth found both unsettling and, in some strange way, comforting.

After breakfast, Elizabeth, determined to make her arrangements, sought out Mr. Collins. She found him pacing nervously in the garden, muttering to himself.

“Cousin,” she began, gently, “I must ask your leave to shorten my visit here. I intend to depart for Hertfordshire in two days’ time.”

Mr. Collins stopped his pacing abruptly, turning to her with a look of frantic appeal. “Two days, Cousin Elizabeth? So soon? But you spoke of a week only last night! And after Lady Catherine’s… her Ladyship’s most recent… observations…” He wrung his hands, quite beside himself.

“Indeed, Mr. Collins,” Elizabeth interrupted him kindly, “it is precisely because of Lady Catherine’s observations that I feel it is most prudent for me to leave. My presence here, as she so forcefully informed me, is giving rise to ‘conjectures,’ and I have no wish to cause further disquiet to her Ladyship, or indeed, to yourself.”

“But my dear cousin!” he cried, quite distressed, “consider the implications! Lady Catherine’s displeasure, once roused, is a most formidable thing! To depart so suddenly, so precipitously, might only confirm her worst suspicions! It might appear as an admission of guilt, a tacit acknowledgement of impropriety!”

Elizabeth could not help but smile faintly at his earnestness. “Mr. Collins, I assure you, my departure will appear to Lady Catherine precisely as she wishes it to appear, no matter the reason I give. She has made it quite clear that she desires my absence, and I merely comply with her unspoken, if not spoken, command. It is better, surely, for all parties concerned, that I remove myself from Rosings.”

Mr. Collins sighed heavily, running a hand through his thinning hair. “Oh, if only you had listened, my dear cousin! If only you had possessed a little more… pliability! A little more… deference! Her Ladyship, for all her august demeanour, is not without her benevolent impulses, and with proper handling, you might have continued to enjoy the unparalleled honour of her patronage for many weeks, perhaps even months!”

“I fear, Mr. Collins,” Elizabeth said, her tone gentle but firm, “that my nature is not entirely suited to the delicate art of ‘proper handling,’ particularly when it involves such pronounced condescension. I regret any distress my frankness may have caused you, but I cannot regret having spoken my mind.”

“Your mind, cousin, is perhaps a little too freely spoken for this society,” Mr. Collins lamented, shaking his head mournfully. “But if you are determined, then I must, of course, arrange for a chaise. Though I dread to inform her Ladyship of your decision. Her temper, I can assure you, has not entirely abated since last night. Indeed, I had the honour of a private audience with her this very morning, and she reiterated, with considerable force, her disapproval of… of certain tendencies.” He looked at Elizabeth pointedly, but she merely met his gaze with calm resignation.

“Then it is settled,” Elizabeth declared, “and I shall make my preparations. Please convey my thanks to Lady Catherine for her hospitality, however briefly it was enjoyed.” She knew the thanks would be entirely wasted, but politeness required the gesture.

She spent the remainder of the day quietly, packing her modest belongings and contemplating the events of her visit. Her time at Rosings had been far more eventful than she could ever have anticipated. She had renewed her acquaintance with Mr. Darcy, albeit under circumstances that had shifted their previous animosity into a more complex, unsettling dynamic. She had faced Lady Catherine’s formidable arrogance and, remarkably, had not entirely succumbed to it. And she had gathered vital information, not only about Mr. Darcy’s proposed match with Miss de Bourgh, but also, in a deeper sense, about the entrenched prejudices and assumptions that governed their elevated world.

The prospect of returning home, though hastened by unpleasantness, was not unwelcome. She longed for the familiarity of Longbourn, for the easy companionship of Jane, and for the simple, unpretentious affections of her family, however trying they might sometimes be. The grand formality of Rosings, with its constant undercurrent of social scrutiny and hierarchical dictates, had begun to weigh heavily upon her spirit.

The next day, as Elizabeth sat in the drawing-room, contemplating her imminent departure, the door opened and Mr. Darcy entered. He looked rather grave, and his eyes immediately sought hers. He approached her chair with a measured tread, and for a moment, neither spoke.

“I understand, Miss Bennet,” he began, his voice low, “that you intend to leave Rosings in two days’ time.”

“Indeed, Mr. Darcy,” Elizabeth replied, a slight tremor in her voice despite her efforts to control it. “My visit has run its course.”

“My aunt, Lady Catherine, informed me of your decision this morning,” he continued, his gaze unwavering, “and she seemed… particularly exercised by it. I trust her observations have not unduly hastened your departure?”

Elizabeth almost laughed, a short, bitter sound that she quickly suppressed. “Mr. Darcy, Lady Catherine’s observations have always been… remarkably direct. And yes, I confess, they have certainly contributed to my conviction that it is time for me to return home.”

He paused, as if considering her words. “I am sorry to hear that my aunt has caused you any distress. She can, I admit, be rather… peremptory in her manner.”

“Peremptory is perhaps an understatement, sir,” Elizabeth countered, a flash of her earlier spirit returning. “She made it quite clear that my continued presence here was both an impropriety and an inconvenience, particularly with regard to your own stay.”

Mr. Darcy’s brow furrowed slightly. “Did she indeed make such remarks? I assure you, Miss Bennet, my aunt’s notions on such matters are often… exaggerated. Your presence here, I must confess, has caused me no inconvenience whatsoever. Quite the contrary, in fact.” His voice had dropped to an even lower tone, and his eyes held a peculiar intensity that unsettled Elizabeth profoundly.

She looked away, her heart quickening its pace. “I am much obliged by your politeness, Mr. Darcy, but I apprehend Lady Catherine’s meaning quite distinctly. She was most insistent upon the propriety of a young lady of my… humble standing, not remaining too long under the same roof as a gentleman of your… elevated expectations.”

“Elevated expectations?” he repeated, a hint of something unreadable in his tone. “And to what expectations do you refer, Miss Bennet?”

Elizabeth met his gaze again, her own eyes challenging him. “To those, sir, which Lady Catherine so plainly articulated last night. To the long-cherished wish of your family, and hers, for your union with Miss de Bourgh. A match, as she explained, settled between your mothers when you were infants, for the preservation of noble families and their consequence.”

Mr. Darcy listened without interruption, his expression unreadable, though a faint colour rose in his cheeks. When she finished, he remained silent for a long moment, his eyes fixed upon her.

“My aunt is a lady of strong opinions, Miss Bennet,” he finally said, his voice carefully neutral, “and she is not always… discreet in expressing them. It is true that such a match has long been desired by both families, and it is equally true that Miss de Bourgh is a lady of amiable character and considerable fortune. But family wishes, however ancient, do not always determine a man’s affections.”

Elizabeth felt a sudden rush of warmth, an unexpected hope that she quickly suppressed. She recalled her fierce refusal of his proposal, her harsh words, her condemnation of his pride and his treatment of Jane and Wickham. Surely, after all that, any thought of affection from him was utterly absurd, a mere fantasy. And yet, his words…

“Perhaps not always, Mr. Darcy,” she said, her voice now barely a whisper, “but they often hold considerable sway, especially in families of your standing. And your own feelings, sir, have been made quite clear to me on a previous occasion, when you felt it necessary to recount all the various objections to a connection with my family, however much you might have been drawn to my ‘liveliness’ and ‘fine eyes.’” A faint blush spread across her cheeks as she spoke, recalling his previous, insulting declaration.

He flinched slightly at her words, a muscle working in his jaw. “Miss Bennet,” he said, his voice now tinged with a solemn earnestness that pierced through her defences, “I acted foolishly, pridefully, and with an unpardonable arrogance on that occasion. My language was unforgivable, and my behaviour even more so. I have reflected deeply upon it since, and I assure you, I have often wished to retract every word, every injurious sentiment. My opinion of you, and of your family, has undergone a most profound alteration.”

Elizabeth stared at him, bewildered by this unexpected admission. Could he truly mean it? Could he, the proud Mr. Darcy, be admitting fault? The memory of his letter, of the truths it contained about Wickham, and of his defence of himself regarding Jane, rushed into her mind. She had come to believe, almost entirely, in his innocence in those matters, and in the justice of his self-vindication. But to hear him express such regret for his original proposal, for his insufferable pride… it was almost too much to comprehend.

“A profound alteration, Mr. Darcy?” she repeated, her voice incredulous. “Indeed, sir, that would be a change of the most extraordinary kind, given the sentiments you expressed so unequivocally at Hunsford. You found my family to be wanting in connections, in fortune, in propriety; and my own person to be tolerable, but certainly not worthy of your consideration, save for a fleeting attraction you found inexplicable.”

He stepped closer, his gaze fixed on hers, an intense earnestness in his eyes. “I spoke with an arrogance and a sense of superiority that shames me now. My objections were founded upon prejudices that I have since come to recognise as unjust and ill-formed. I was blind to your true worth, and to the genuine goodness of your sister, Jane. My judgment was warped by pride of family and consequence, and by a mistaken notion of what constituted true merit. I believed myself to be acting honourably, in separating Bingley from your sister, but I see now that I acted with a presumptuous interference that caused great pain, and for that, I can only beg your forgiveness.”

Elizabeth was rendered speechless. This was a Mr. Darcy she scarcely recognised, a man utterly stripped of his former hauteur, speaking with a sincerity that was impossible to doubt. Her mind reeled with the implications of his confession, of his apology, of his profound self-abasement. The memory of his letter, detailing his genuine belief in Jane’s indifference, and his subsequent regret, now merged with this fresh, overwhelming admission of error. He had indeed changed. He had truly changed.

“You… you beg my forgiveness, Mr. Darcy?” she stammered, hardly able to articulate the words.
