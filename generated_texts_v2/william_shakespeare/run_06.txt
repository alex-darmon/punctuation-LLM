SIR ROGER.
My cloak, good John, my cloak is rumpled so! A monstrous wrinkle, see you not its shame? It doth disgrace me. I cannot stand this insult; who dares touch my finest cloth, I ask you? No man, I swear. Was it the cobbler’s apprentice? His hands are rough. I saw him near my bench. This cannot pass. My reputation, man, it hangs by threads. My honour, too, needs swift mending.

MASTER JOHN.
Sir Roger, pray, calm thy spirit. A mere fold, I tell thee. The air, perchance, caught it so, no malicious hand involved. What cause for such a tumult? Thy brow is furrowed. Thy face is red. This wrinkle, sir, it holds no sword, it plots no treason.

SIR ROGER.
No sword? No treason? Thou art blind, Master John! This wrinkle, sirrah, is a public affront. It speaks of slovenly habits; of a knight unkempt. My enemies will feast upon this sight. Think you the Duke will grant me favour now, with such a garment? Nay, he will scoff. He will laugh. My suit for the mill, it will be lost. This simple wrinkle, it destroys my future. All my hopes, they crumble.

Enter Eleanor.

ELEANOR.
Good sirs, a most curious sight. What weighty matter, pray, engrosses you so? Is it the King’s decree? Or war’s dread news? Or doth some pestilence

afflict our quiet town? Your faces, sirs, are fraught with care. What trouble mars this morn? What ill portends?

SIR ROGER. Pestilence, madam, doth not touch this woe. Nor war, nor king's decree. A deeper wound, it festers here. A wound upon my honour. Look, I pray you. See this fold? This monstrous crease? It shames me. It undoes me quite. My cloak, my finest cloth. It speaks of ruin. It speaks of shame.

MASTER JOHN. Good Eleanor, attend him not. His wits do wander. A simple crease. The tailor’s art may mend it. A moment’s work. No more, I swear. He doth exaggerate. His mood is dark.

ELEANOR. A crease, Master John? Is that the sum of it? This wrinkle, then, excites such passion? Sir Roger, pray, uncloud thy brow. Such trifles pass. The world holds greater pains. Or so I think.

SIR ROGER. Trifles, madam? Nay, you do not grasp it. This is no trifle. This is destiny. My suit before the Duke. My reputation. My very standing, all depend upon it. A knight unkempt. A man of slovenly mien. This is the image they will see. This is the truth.

MASTER JOHN. They will see thy virtue, sir. Thy service, too. Thy loyal heart. A wrinkle, sir, is but a fleeting thing. It comes, it goes. Like shadows on the wall.

SIR ROGER. Shadows, John? These shadows have sharp teeth. They gnaw upon my soul. They bite my future. The Duke, he values order. He loves precision. He hates all disarray. This cloak, it screams disorder. It shouts my failure. My enemies will whisper. They will point.

ELEANOR. But who would mark such a thing? A mere garment. Are men so petty, then? So given to malice? I cannot think it. Most eyes see deeper. They judge the spirit. They weigh the worth.

SIR ROGER. Ah, madam, you live in light. You see but beauty. The world, it is a snake. It hides its venom. Men judge by show. They mock the flawed. They scorn the careless. My mill suit, it is lost. My hopes, they die. A simple wrinkle. Yes. A simple wrinkle brings me down.

MASTER JOHN. Enough, good sir. Thy words are wild. Thy thoughts unsound. This drama, it must cease. Let us sit a while. Let us drink some ale. The world spins on. It cares not for a fold.

SIR ROGER. It cares, John! It cares most terribly! It watches me. It plots my downfall. The Duke, he will receive me. I shall bow. He will look upon my cloak. He will see the blemish. His face will harden. His favour vanish. I know it, John. I feel it in my bones.

ELEANOR. Sir Roger, if this wrinkle holds such sway. Why not remove it? A quick pressing. A gentle hand. It is not past all remedy. Is it? Or is it a stain? A rent, perhaps?

SIR ROGER. A rent? No, madam. Worse than that. A rent may be excused. A tear can be mended. This is a subtle wound. A mark of neglect. It cannot be pressed. Not in this moment. The Duke expects me soon. I must appear. I cannot wait. I cannot hide.

MASTER JOHN. Then go, sir. Go to him. Thy worth shines through. Let the cloak be. Let destiny unfold. Be brave, my friend. Be strong of heart.

SIR ROGER. Brave? Strong? When my own raiment betrays me? No, John. I am undone. I am quite undone. My suit for the mill. That valuable land. It shall be given to some smoother man. Some man whose cloak is perfect. Some man whose life is straight.

ELEANOR. Sir, I do sense a deeper current here. It is not just the cloak. Your heart is troubled. What fear resides within you? Speak it forth. Let us share thy burden. We are thy friends.

SIR ROGER. My burden? It is this world. Its harsh judgments. Its fickle nature. Its sudden turns. My land. My father's mill. It needs new water rights. The Duke, he holds the key. He holds my future. This wrinkle, it’s a sign. A dark omen. It tells me of rejection. It whispers of loss.

MASTER JOHN. And yet, the sun still shines. The birds still sing. Life moves along. Despite thy wrinkle. Come, good sir. Let us seek out Thomas. The tailor, he works near. Perhaps a swift iron. A heated stone. It takes no time at all.

SIR ROGER. No time? You mock me, John. You know I lack it. The Duke's messenger, he did arrive. An hour hence. I must be there. I cannot fail. I cannot delay. This wrinkle, it has sealed my doom.

ELEANOR. (To Master John, aside)
  His mind is quite unhinged. This is not well.
  His fear is monstrous. His despair is deep.
  We must do something. What can be done?
  This cloth, it troubles him. His very soul.

MASTER JOHN. (To Eleanor, aside)
  I know not, madam. He grows more frantic.
  His honour, it consumes him. Day by day.
  He sees great mountains in each tiny molehill.
  His hopes are fragile. His spirit weak.
  He fears the Duke. He fears the world.

SIR ROGER. I hear you whisper. You mock my sorrow. You scorn my plight. Oh, cruel friends! To see me thus. To laugh at my undoing. This cloak, it is my honour. It is my name. It is my hope.

ELEANOR. Nay, Sir Roger, we do not mock. We grieve for thee. We seek a solace. A swift redress. Let me but try. Let me inspect it. Perhaps a smoothing hand. A gentle touch. (She approaches the cloak.)

SIR ROGER. (Shrinking back.)
  No! Touch it not! Thy touch may worsen it.
  Thy gentle hand, it cannot mend this wound.
  It is beyond repair. Beyond all hope.
  This wrinkle. It is fate. It is my doom.

MASTER JOHN. He is obsessed. His reason flees him. What shall we do, good Eleanor? What recourse? He stands on ruin. For a foolish thing.

ELEANOR. We must address his fear. Not just the cloak. Sir Roger, tell me true. What else disturbs you? Is it the Duke's stern eye? Or his harsh temper? Or is it something more? Something deeper?

SIR ROGER. The Duke, he is a hawk. He watches closely. He judges all. He spares no faults. My rival, Master Finch, he seeks the mill. He is a viper. He will use this flaw. He will whisper slanders. He will point and sneer. "Behold Sir Roger. His raiment soiled. His habits poor. His mind unsound."

MASTER JOHN. Finch is a knave. His words are dust. The Duke sees through such trickery. He is a man of justice. And of honour. He will not be swayed. By such small things.

SIR ROGER. You know not Finch! He hath a cunning tongue. He poisons minds. He spins vile webs. This wrinkle is his weapon. It is his chance. My future crumbles. My fortune fades. All for a simple wrinkle. Oh, the shame!

ELEANOR. Then let us meet this Finch. Let us disarm him. If he depends on such a flimsy charge. Then his foundation is most weak. His plot will fail. Let us prepare. Let us stand with thee.

SIR ROGER. Stand with me? You are kind, good madam. But this battle. It is mine alone. My honour, it must bear this weight. My fate, it hangs. Upon this thread. Oh, my poor cloak. My ruined life.

MASTER JOHN. (Sighs deeply.)
  This is a madness. A deep despair.
  He will not listen. He will not see.
  His eyes are blinded. By this trifle.
  What swift cure? What balm for him?

ELEANOR. There is a way. Perhaps. A bold attempt. If the Duke values order. And precision. Then let him see true order. In thy heart. Let thy words be strong. Let thy truth shine. And let this cloak. Be but a challenge. A test of spirit.

SIR ROGER. A challenge? A test? Nay, madam, it is a curse. It binds my hands. It ties my tongue. How can I speak of worth? When my own cloth proclaims my lack? I am a wreck. I am a ruin.

Enter a MESSENGER, hurried and breathless.

MESSENGER. Sir Roger! Sir Roger, I must speak with thee! The Duke, he doth require thy presence now! He bade me hasten. He waits no longer. Time is of the essence. Make no delay.

SIR ROGER. (Gasps, his hand to his heart.)
  Now? Already? So soon, so fast?
  My fate is sealed. My doom is here.
  Oh, cursed wrinkle! Thou hast betrayed me.
  Thou hast undone me. Before my time.

MASTER JOHN. Go, Sir Roger, go! Thy moment comes. Be strong, be bold. Face him with courage. Let thy spirit soar. Beyond this cloth.

ELEANOR. Yes, Sir Roger. Be thyself. Be true. Let honesty be thy best attire. Let wisdom cloak thee. Let truth sustain.

SIR ROGER. (Wringing his hands.)
  But this cloak! This dreadful crease!
  It screams my failure. It shouts my shame.
  How can I face him? How can I speak?
  My voice will falter. My knees will shake.

MESSENGER. Sir, with all due respect. The Duke is stern. He brooks no tardiness. His temper flares. You must come swiftly. Now. This very instant.

SIR ROGER. (Looks at his cloak, then at Eleanor and Master John, then back at the Messenger, a flicker of desperate resolve in his eyes.)
  There is no choice. My hour is come.
  I must endure it. I must confront it.
  This wrinkle. My cross. My heavy burden.
  Wish me well, friends. For I go to my fate.
  (He turns to leave, then pauses, looking back at them.)
  But tell me, truly. Does it look so bad?
  This monstrous wrinkle. Does it condemn me?

MASTER JOHN. It is a wrinkle, sir. Nothing more. Thy worth is greater. Than any cloth.

ELEANOR. Go, Sir Roger. Show thy true self. Let thy integrity shine. Beyond all raiment.

SIR ROGER. (Sighs, a profound, tragic sound.)
  Beyond all raiment. Yes. So be it then.
  (He exits swiftly, following the Messenger.)

MASTER JOHN. The poor man. His mind is quite undone. This is a sorrow. A deep, sad sight.

ELEANOR. Indeed. His fears have grown. They now consume him. A simple crease. A world of woe. This is a lesson. For us all. How small things swell. When fear takes root.

MASTER JOHN. And Master Finch. That venomous snake. He will exploit it. He will feast upon him. We must not let it be. We must intervene.

ELEONOR. But how? He is already gone. To face the Duke. To meet his fate. We cannot enter there. We cannot speak.

MASTER JOHN. Perhaps. But we can hope. We can prepare. If he returns in sorrow. We shall aid him. If he returns in triumph. We shall cheer.

ELEONOR. What if Finch is there? What if he slanders? The Duke, he listens. To all complaints. This is a grave concern. A true peril.

MASTER JOHN. Then let us find out. Let us stand near. We cannot interfere. We can observe. The Duke's court. It is a place of intrigue. A place of peril. A place of hope. Come, Eleanor. Let us go.

ELEONOR. Yes, Master John. My heart aches for him. This is no jest. This is real fear. His mill. His honour. His very life. All on the thread. Of a crumpled cloak.

(They exit. The stage is empty for a moment. Then, enter MASTER FINCH and SIMON, a conniving looking servant.)

MASTER FINCH. The fool is late. I knew he would be. His disarray. His lack of order. The Duke will see it. He will scorn him so.

SIMON. Indeed, Master Finch. His cloak, it was a sight. A rumpled mess. A true disgrace. I saw him fret. I heard him wail. He quite lost his wits. Over a mere fold.

MASTER FINCH. Excellent! Excellent! This plays into my hands. My moment comes. My triumph nears. The mill, good Simon. It shall be mine. Sir Roger’s downfall. My swift ascent.

SIMON. And my reward, good master? My promised gold? For all my tidings. And my swift reports?

MASTER FINCH. Patience, fool. When the prize is won. Thy pockets shall be lined. Thy thirst assuaged. But first, the kill. First, the victory. Sir Roger’s ruin. My glorious day.

SIMON. He was quite distraught. His face was red. He called it treason. He called it doom. A simple crease. A curious thing.

MASTER FINCH. It is not simple, Simon. It is the lever. The fulcrum point. To overturn his world. The Duke, he values polish. He loves appearance. He hates all slovenliness. This is his weakness. This is his flaw. I shall exploit it. I shall prevail.

SIMON. And what of thy suit, Master Finch? Is it well pressed? Is it quite perfect? The Duke, he sees all. He misses naught.

MASTER FINCH. My suit is flawless. My mien composed. My words are honey. My logic keen. I have prepared. I have foreseen this. Sir Roger’s folly. My shrewd design. The mill, Simon. Think of the profits. Think of the wealth. It shall be ours.

SIMON. It is a rich prize. A vast reward. For such a small fault. In another man.

MASTER FINCH. Small? It is immense! It is his character! It shows his lack. Of self-respect. Of discipline. These are the things the Duke esteems. These are the virtues he demands. Sir Roger lacks them. He shall fall.

(Enter the DUKE, LORD GREGORY, and several ATTENDANTS. The DUKE is stern, observant.)

DUKE. Where is Sir Roger? Has he arrived? My patience wears thin. My time is precious.

LORD GREGORY. He is within, Your Grace. He was but moments late. His messenger, he brought him swiftly.

DUKE. Swiftly? I asked for immediate. For promptness. This is a matter of great import. The mill lands. The water rights. Delays. They irk me. They vex my spirit.

MASTER FINCH. (Bowing low, with a slick, practiced grace.)
  Your Grace, forgive me. For my forwardness.
  But perhaps Sir Roger. Is not quite ready.
  For such grave matters. For such great trusts.

DUKE. Indeed, Master Finch? And what gives thee such cause? To speak so freely? To make such claims?

MASTER FINCH. (With feigned reluctance.)
  I mean no malice. Your Grace, I swear.
  But rumour travels. And sight betrays.
  I saw him but this morn. In great distress.
  His raiment quite dishevelled. His cloak quite rumpled.
  A most unseemly sight. For a knight of worth.
  It spoke of haste. Of disarray. Of a troubled mind.

DUKE. Dishevelled? Rumpled? What meanest thou? I prize order. I expect decorum. Are these mere slanders? Or truths unvarnished?

LORD GREGORY. Your Grace, Sir Roger. Is a loyal knight. His heart is true. His service proven. A rumpled cloak. Is but a trifle.

MASTER FINCH. A trifle, my Lord? But trifles oft. Betray the deeper truth. A slovenly habit. A careless mind. These speak of greater flaws. Of things neglected. How can he manage lands? If he cannot manage his own attire? How can he govern streams? If he cannot smooth his own cloth?

DUKE. (Frowning, strokes his beard.)
  There is some truth. In what you say, Finch.
  Appearance speaks. Of inner state.
  Order without. Shows order within.
  I have observed this. In many men.

(Enter SIR ROGER, looking anxious, but trying to maintain a brave face. He bows deeply to the DUKE.)

SIR ROGER. Your Grace. I beg your pardon. For any tardiness. My humble self. Is here to serve.

DUKE. (His eyes scanning Sir Roger, pausing on the cloak.)
  Sir Roger. Thou art here. At last.
  I called thee swiftly. For a serious cause.
  These mill lands. They need clear governance.
  A steady hand. A watchful eye.

SIR ROGER. Your Grace, I am prepared. My suit is ready. I have surveyed the lands. I know their needs. My plans are firm. My purpose clear.

MASTER FINCH. (Coughs politely.)
  Your Grace, forgive me. But I must speak.
  Sir Roger's cloak. It seems to cry out.
  For swift attention. For common care.
  Is this the mien? Of one who seeks command?
  Of one who asks for trust? For great estates?

SIR ROGER. (Flushing crimson, his hand instinctively going to his cloak.)
  Master Finch, you overstep! Your words are cruel!
  My cloak. It is but a garment. A simple thing.
  It does not speak. Of my true worth.

DUKE. (Eyes narrowing at Sir Roger.)
  But it does, Sir Roger. It speaks volumes.
  A man's attire. Is oft his outward soul.
  It shows his care. Or his neglect.
  I see a wrinkle. A rather deep one.
  It suggests haste. Or, perhaps, indifference.

SIR ROGER. Your Grace, I swear! It was an accident!
  A sudden gust. A servant's error.
  I have no indifference. I have great care.
  My heart is loyal. My service true.

MASTER FINCH. (Smiling thinly.)
  A gust, Sir Roger? Or a careless hand?
  Perhaps one too easily ruffled.
  In mind or garment. Which is it, pray?
  The mill lands. They need strong leadership.
  Not one so easily vexed. By trifles.

SIR ROGER. (Anger rising, but trying to control it.)
  Finch, thou art a viper! A malicious tongue!
  You seek to slander! To undermine!
  My suit for the mill. It is just. It is right.

DUKE. (Bangs his hand on a table, a sharp sound.)
  Silence! Both of you! This is my court!
  I hear enough. Of petty squabbles.
  Sir Roger, thy attire. It displeases me.
  It shows a lack. Of due respect.
  For this occasion. For my own presence.

SIR ROGER. (Sinks to one knee, despair in his voice.)
  Your Grace, I beg you. Do not judge me so.
  This wrinkle. It is but a mark. A cruel twist.
  It does not diminish. My loyalty. My worth.
  I have served you well. For many years.
  My father's mill. It needs protection.
  From such as Finch. From men like him.

MASTER FINCH. (Stepping forward, bowing.)
  Your Grace, I come prepared. My plans are clear.
  My documents are ordered. My accounts precise.
  I offer care. I offer diligence.
  My clothes are spotless. My mind is sharp.
  I see no wrinkles. In my purpose.

DUKE. (Looks from Sir Roger's kneeling figure to Finch's smug face.)
  Sir Roger. Rise. This is a difficult choice.
  I value loyalty. I value service.
  But also order. And keen attention.
  This mill. It is vital. To the realm.

LORD GREGORY. Your Grace, consider. Sir Roger’s long years. His honesty. His steady hand. A slight blemish. It means so little.

DUKE. (Turns to Gregory.)
  It means much, Gregory. It speaks of care.
  Or lack of it. In larger things.
  Sir Roger. Tell me. Why this wrinkle?
  Speak honestly. Speak from thy heart.

SIR ROGER. (Stands, slowly, head bowed.)
  Your Grace. I confess. My mind was troubled.
  This very morn. I saw the blemish.
  It filled me with dread. With deep despair.
  I feared your judgment. I feared this moment.
  My anxiety. It made me weak.
  It made me fret. Over this poor cloth.
  I was afraid. Afraid to fail you.

DUKE. (Pauses, observing Sir Roger closely.)
  Afraid to fail? A noble fear.
  But fear. It can consume a man.
  It can distort. His very sight.
  To see a mountain. In a simple crease.

MASTER FINCH. (Interjects swiftly.)
  A mind so troubled. So easily swayed.
  Is this the mind. For such great trusts?
  For mill and land? For vital waters?
  He fears a wrinkle. What will he fear next?
  A cloud? A breeze? A sudden shadow?

SIR ROGER. (Looks at Finch, then back at the Duke with renewed resolve.)
  My fear, Your Grace. It was for you.
  For your esteem. For your good favour.
  But now I see. My folly clear.
  This cloth. It is but cloth. It does not define me.
  My heart. My will. My honest purpose.
  These are the truths. That I present.
  I may have trembled. Over a fold.
  But I shall not tremble. In my duties.
  I shall not waver. In my resolve.
  The mill. It needs a guardian. Not a showman.

DUKE. (His expression unreadable.)
  A guardian. Not a showman. Interesting words.
  Finch. You present a smooth exterior.
  But what lies beneath? What true intent?
  Sir Roger. You show a troubled soul.
  But perhaps an honest one. A candid heart.

MASTER FINCH. Your Grace, my intent. Is pure and true.
  I seek efficiency. I seek success.
  I seek to serve you. With all my might.
  My plans for the mill. Are well conceived.
  They promise profit. They promise growth.

SIR ROGER. And my plans, Your Grace. They promise justice.
  They promise fairness. For all the tenants.
  They promise care. For the land itself.
  Profit is good. But honour is better.
  I serve the people. As well as you.

DUKE. (Sighs, runs a hand through his hair.)
  This is a dilemma. A vexing choice.
  Order versus honesty. Show versus substance.
  I must consider. I must reflect.
  Leave me now. Both of you.
  I shall send my decree. Before the day is done.

MASTER FINCH. (Bows deeply, a confident smile on his face.)
  As Your Grace commands. I wait with hope.
  (He glances triumphantly at Sir Roger as he exits.)

SIR ROGER. (Bows, his face pale, but with a new quiet dignity.)
  Your will be done. Your Grace.
  (He exits, head held higher than before.)

LORD GREGORY. A difficult judgment, Your Grace. Both men have merit.

DUKE. (Stands, walks to a window, looking out.)
  Indeed, Gregory. Indeed they do.
  But one displays. A certain weakness.
  A too-great concern. For others' eyes.
  And the other. A too-great confidence.
  A polished surface. That hides what depths?
  That wrinkle. It revealed a man.
  A man consumed. By fear of judgment.
  Finch’s perfection. It hides his fear.
  A fear of flaws. A fear of truth.

LORD GREGORY. So the wrinkle, Your Grace. Was a test?

DUKE. Perhaps. Or simply fate. Unveiling souls.
  Sir Roger’s anguish. It was quite profound.
  His honesty. It was quite stark.
  He spoke of fear. He spoke of truth.
  Finch. He spoke of profit. And of his own worth.
  The mill needs heart. It needs true care.
  Not just cold numbers. Not just smooth talk.
  It needs a man. Who cares for more.
  Than just his cloak. Or his own gain.

LORD GREGORY. Then your decision. It is made, Your Grace?

DUKE. (Turns from the window, a small, knowing smile on his face.)
  It is, Gregory. It is quite clear.
  A man who sees. His own imperfections.
  And dares to speak. Of his own fears.
  He is the man. I choose for this.
  The crumpled cloak. It proved him more.
  Than any flawless fabric. Could ever show.
  Send word to Sir Roger. The mill is his.
  And to Master Finch. My regrets.

LORD GREGORY. (Bows, a look of relief and satisfaction on his face.)
  As Your Grace commands. A wise decision.
  The people will rejoice. They love Sir Roger.
  His honest spirit. His open heart.

DUKE. (Nods.)
  Yes. And let this be. A lesson learned.
  That outward show. Is not all truth.
  That character. It shines through flaws.
  And courage blooms. In humble hearts.
  A wrinkled cloak. A path to grace.
  Who would have thought it? Who would have known?

(The DUKE exits, followed by LORD GREGORY. The stage is empty. The sound of a distant trumpet plays.)

(Re-enter ELEANOR and MASTER JOHN, looking worried.)

ELEANOR. My heart still beats. With such a dread.
  What news, I wonder? What fate befell him?
  Did the Duke scorn him? Did he condemn?

MASTER JOHN. We must wait. We must be patient.
  His honesty. It may have swayed him.
  Or Finch’s malice. It may have won.

(Enter SIR ROGER, slowly, but with a serene smile on his face. He holds a sealed document.)

ELEANOR. Sir Roger! Speak! What is the news?
  Thy face, it holds. A gentle calm.
  Is it good tidings? Or heavy sorrow?

SIR ROGER. (Smiling broadly, a look of profound relief and joy.)
  Good tidings, friends! Most wondrous news!
  The mill. It is mine! The Duke hath granted it!
  He saw my heart. He saw my truth.
  He chose me, friends! He chose my spirit!

MASTER JOHN. (Embracing Sir Roger heartily.)
  Huzzah! Huzzah! My friend, my brother!
  I knew it! I knew it! Thy worth was seen!
  Thy honour proved! Beyond all cloth!

ELEANOR. (Tears in her eyes.)
  Oh, Sir Roger! This fills my soul!
  This is true justice! This is true grace!
  The crumpled cloak. It did not doom thee!

SIR ROGER. Nay, Eleanor. It saved me, rather!
  The Duke, he saw. My fear, my truth.
  He said my anguish. Over the wrinkle.
  Showed honesty. A tender heart.
  He scorned the smoothness. Of Master Finch.
  He chose my flaws. He chose my soul.

MASTER JOHN. So the wrinkle. Was a blessing! A strange turn!
  A twist of fate! A happy end!
  Who would have guessed? Such power it held!

SIR ROGER. Indeed, John! Indeed, my friend!
  From this day forth. I shall remember.
  That true worth. Lies not in polish.
  But in the spirit. That shines within.
  My cloak. My faithful cloak. It told my tale.
  It spoke my truth. For all to hear.

ELEANOR. And what of Finch? What fate for him?

SIR ROGER. He was dismissed. With words most cold.
  The Duke saw through him. His false facade.
  His shallow heart. His venomous tongue.
  He learned a lesson. A bitter one.
  That showmanship. Is not true character.

MASTER JOHN. This is a day. For great rejoicing!
  Let us to the tavern! Let us drink deep!
  To honesty! To truth! To wrinkles!

SIR ROGER. (Laughing, a genuine, joyful sound.)
  To wrinkles, John! To all our flaws!
  For they reveal us. They make us real.
  They make us human. They make us strong.
  Come, friends. Let us celebrate.
  My heart is light. My spirit free.
  The mill is mine. My future bright.
  And all because. Of one small crease!
  Oh, glorious wrinkle! Thou art my friend!

(They exit, arm in arm, laughing. The stage is left empty, signifying the end of the scene and the resolution of Sir Roger's immediate crisis.)
(Curtain.)

(ACT TWO, SCENE ONE)
(The Mill of Sir Roger. Several weeks have passed. The mill is bustling. SIR ROGER, though happy, shows signs of diligent work. His clothes are still simple, practical. ELEANOR and MASTER JOHN enter, observing the scene. A MILLER'S BOY, young and quick, moves sacks of grain.)

ELEANOR. He labours still. With honest hands. His good fortune. It hath not changed him. His brow is wet. With honest sweat.
MASTER JOHN. Nay, Eleanor. He is the same. A truer soul. You cannot find. The grain flows well. The profits rise. The folk are pleased. They love their miller.
SIR ROGER. (Wiping flour from his brow, smiling warmly.) My friends! My welcome guests! What brings you here? To this dusty realm? I toil, you see. But joyfully.
ELEANOR. We came to check. We came to see. If wealth had altered. Thy honest heart. But no. You stand. As true as ever. A noble man. In miller's garb.
SIR ROGER. The mill, it lives. It breathes, it thrives. A constant hum. A rhythmic beat. It feeds the folk. It fills their bowls. This is my joy. My true reward. (He claps MASTER JOHN on the shoulder.) Come, John. Come, Eleanor. Let us find rest. From this fine dust. My office waits. For friendly chat.
MASTER JOHN. Indeed, Sir Roger. We would hear more. Of thy new life. Of daily tasks. Is it all ease? Or are there thorns?
SIR ROGER. (Chuckling softly.) Thorns, Master John? A few, perhaps. The Duke's own taxmen. They count the coin. And merchants argue. O'er every sack. A miller's life. Is not all sweet. It has its cares. Its heavy loads. But honest toil. It brings true peace. A peace I sought. For many years.
ELEANOR. That peace, it shines. Upon thy face. It glows within. A gentle light.
SIR ROGER. I thank you, Eleanor. For kind words. They warm my soul. Like sun on stone. Come, let us go. (They move towards a small office within the mill. The MILLER'S BOY continues his work, singing a simple tune.)
(They exit into the office. The sound of the mill continues. Enter a STRANGE MAN, cloaked and hooded, who moves stealthily across the stage, observing the mill from the shadows. He glances around, then slips away unseen.)

(ACT TWO, SCENE TWO)
(The Duke's private study in the castle. A large map of the duchy is spread upon a table. THE DUKE stands beside it. LORD GREGORY is seated, listening intently.)

THE DUKE. The reports are dire. My Lord Gregory. From Baron Thorne. His lands are troubled. A lack of grain. A rising disquiet. His tax collections. They fall too short.
LORD GREGORY. A grave concern, Sire. Most surely so. Baron Thorne. He is a weak man. Too easily swayed. By flatterers.
THE DUKE. I need a man. Of keenest sight. Of purest heart. To journey there. To see the truth. To sift the lies. A man not moved. By gold or power.
LORD GREGORY. Such men are rare. In these base times. But one, I know. Whose honour glows. Sir Roger, Sire. The honest miller.
THE DUKE. (Smiling faintly.) Ah, Sir Roger! My wrinkled friend! I had forgotten. His simple worth. He showed such truth. Such honest fear. Over a crease. A single flaw. Yes, he may serve. His eyes are clear. His judgment sound. Unclouded by greed. Or by ambition.
LORD GREGORY. He seeks no gain. Beyond his mill. He loves his folk. He loves his work. His loyalty. Is to his truth. Not to his purse.
THE DUKE. A rare virtue. In these dark days. Send for him, Gregory. At once, dispatch. A royal summons. Bid him attend. My court this eve. Let him prepare. For weighty matters.
LORD GREGORY. It shall be done, Sire. Immediately. He will be here. As you command. (LORD GREGORY rises and bows, then exits.)
THE DUKE. (To himself, tracing a path on the map.) Baron Thorne. A fading name. A tainted name. I need to know. What truly stirs. Within his lands. Sir Roger's soul. It shines like gold. A true pure heart. It is a strength. A mighty shield. For justice true. And honest rule. The common man. Can teach us much. More than these lords. With powdered wigs. And silken tongues. Their false displays. Their empty words. They blind the truth. They hide the facts. I need a man. Who sees with light. Who thinks with sense. Who judges well. Yes, Sir Roger. He will be found. A faithful servant. A loyal friend. His simple cloak. His honest smile. They speak of things. That truly count. He will not fail. My trust in him. He will not break. My simple faith. A miller's wisdom. It oft surpasses. A courtier's wit. A scholar's lore. For it is born. Of daily life. Of earth and grain. Of toil and sweat. A truer school. It cannot be. He knows the needs. Of common folk. He knows their woes. Their daily pleas. He hears their cries. He feels their pain. A simple man. A giant soul. I made a choice. A righteous choice. To give him grace. To grant him peace. And now, in turn. I seek his aid. A vital need. A desperate plea. The realm itself. May hang on this. Upon his truth. His clear insight.

(ACT TWO, SCENE THREE)
(The Duke's grand audience chamber. Courtiers are milling about, whispering. Among them are LORD HAWKWOOD, a proud, sharp-featured man, and LADY MARTHA, a woman of sharp wit and keen observation.)

LORD HAWKWOOD. (To a nearby courtier.) A miller, say you? Summoned here? To this grand hall? What jest is this?
COURTIER 1. So it is told, my Lord. From the Duke's own lips. Sir Roger, the miller. He is to come.
LADY MARTHA. (Interjecting smoothly.) The Duke, my Lord. He sees beyond. Beyond the cloth. Beyond the rank. He judges men. By what they are. Not what they wear.
LORD HAWKWOOD. A dangerous game. My Lady Martha. To elevate. Such simple folk. It breeds unrest. Among the ranks. Among the noble. Our ancient lines. Will soon be mocked. By dusty hands.
LADY MARTHA. Perhaps, my Lord. Their ancient lines. Have grown too thin. Too weak with ease. A fresh new spirit. May serve the realm. Far better, sir. Than fading pride.
(A trumpet sounds. The chattering ceases. THE DUKE enters, accompanied by LORD GREGORY. A moment later, SIR ROGER enters, looking somewhat uncomfortable in his simple miller's clothes amidst the finery, but holding his head high.)

THE DUKE. (Addressing the court.) My Lords! My Ladies! Give welcome now. To Sir Roger. A man of worth. A man of truth.
(A few polite claps, mostly from those loyal to the Duke. LORD HAWKWOOD sneers subtly.)

SIR ROGER. (Bowing deeply.) Your Grace. I stand before you. As you bade. My simple self. At your command.
THE DUKE. Rise, Sir Roger. There is no need. For such deep bows. From honest men. I have a task. A weighty task. A difficult one. For one whose heart. Is truly pure. Whose mind is sharp. Whose judgment clear. I speak of Baron Thorne. His lands, you know. They border ours. A vital link. A troubled place. Rumours abound. Of scarcity. Of famine's sting. And heavy hand. I need you, Roger. To journey there. To ascertain. The very truth. Of Thorne's affairs. His steward's rule. His people's plight.
LORD HAWKWOOD. (Stepping forward, his voice dripping with scorn.) Your Grace, forgive me. But a miller? To judge a Baron? To weigh his worth? What knowledge has he? Of statecraft's arts? Of noble law? Of complex trade? His hand, it guides. A grinding stone. Not ducal policy. Not royal writ.
THE DUKE. (His voice firm, unyielding.) My Lord Hawkwood. Your counsel's heard. But I have chosen. My own path here. Sir Roger's hands. They guide the mill. They feed the folk. He knows their pain. He knows their wants. Far better, sir. Than many lords. Whose cushioned lives. Keep them aloof. From common need.
SIR ROGER. Your Grace, I thank you. For your trust. But I am simple. My skills are few. I know the grain. I know its worth. But matters grave. Of state and rule. They vex my mind. They vex my soul.
THE DUKE. Your honesty. It is your strength. Your common sense. It is your guide. You ask no gain. No rich reward. Save truth itself. And justice served. That is the skill. That I require. You will observe. You will converse. With common folk. With honest traders. With lesser lords. You will report. To me alone. What you discover. In Thorne's domain. Will you accept? This sacred trust?
SIR ROGER. (After a moment's hesitation, looking at the Duke's earnest face.) I will, Your Grace. With all my heart. I will endeavour. To serve you well. My humble best. Shall be your guide.
THE DUKE. Excellent, Roger! A coach awaits. Tomorrow morn. For your swift journey. Lord Gregory. Will brief you well. On all the points. That you must seek. And for your stay. In Thorne's cold halls. A modest sum. For your expense. No lavish goods. No grand display. Just your true self. Your watchful eye.
LORD GREGORY. (To Sir Roger.) Come, Sir Roger. Let us withdraw. To a quiet place. We shall discuss. The journey's scope. The matters grave.
(SIR ROGER bows to THE DUKE, then follows LORD GREGORY out of the chamber. The courtiers whisper amongst themselves. LORD HAWKWOOD shakes his head in disbelief.)

LADY MARTHA. (To Lord Hawkwood, with a knowing smile.) The Duke, my Lord. He plays a game. A subtle one. You fail to grasp. He tests us all. He probes our hearts. He shows the court. What true worth means.
LORD HAWKWOOD. A dangerous test. A foolish whim. To place such trust. In common hands. It smacks of folly. It reeks of chaos. This simple miller. He will be lost. In Thorne's dark court.
(A low, insidious laugh is heard from a shadow near a tapestry. A brief glimpse of a figure, quickly disappearing. No one else seems to notice.)

(ACT TWO, SCENE FOUR)
(A secluded tavern in the capital, later that night. MASTER JOHN and ELEANOR are seated at a table, looking concerned. The tavern is noisy, but they are in a quiet corner.)

MASTER JOHN. Such news! Such news! He is dispatched. To Baron Thorne. A fearful journey. A dangerous quest.
ELEANOR. My heart is heavy. For Sir Roger. To leave his mill. To leave his home. And face such perils. In Thorne's grim lands. He is so kind. So good and true. The wolves will circle. The foxes scheme.
MASTER JOHN. Indeed, my dear. These courtly matters. Are not his field. His honest nature. It may be bruised. By false reports. By cunning traps. By subtle lies.
ELEANOR. I fear for him. I truly do. His simple cloak. His candid face. They hold no shield. Against deceit.
MASTER JOHN. The Duke, he trusts him. That is his strength. That is his hope. But Finch, I saw. I saw him earlier. Lurking still. Around the castle. His eyes held malice. His face was dark. He plots, I swear. He plots again.
ELEANOR. Finch! That viper! He was dismissed. He has no power.
MASTER JOHN. Power, no. But venom, yes. A whispered word. A planted seed. A rumour spread. Can fell a tree. As strong as oaks. A good man's name. Can be destroyed. By evil tongues. By jealous hearts.
ELEANOR. We must warn him! We must try! Before he leaves! Before he rides!
MASTER JOHN. It is too late. He sleeps now, sure. Preparing well. For tomorrow's journey. But we must watch. We must be vigilant. For our dear friend. For Sir Roger's sake. His truth and honour. They are our pride. They are our light. In these dark days.
ELEANOR. What can we do? We are so small. So powerless. Against such foes.
MASTER JOHN. We pray, Eleanor. We hope, we wait. We trust in God. And in his grace. Sir Roger's heart. It is his guide. It is his strength. It is his shield. His simple faith. His honest soul. Will see him through. Will bring him home. Let us drink now. To Sir Roger. To his safe return. To truth's triumph. (He raises a tankard. ELEANOR raises hers, her eyes still worried.)
ELEANOR. To Sir Roger. May he prevail. May truth prevail. Against all odds. Against all lies.
MASTER JOHN. He will, my dear. He always will. His worth is pure. His spirit strong. No courtly wiles. No Baron's tricks. Can break his will. Or dim his light. He carries truth. Within his soul. A beacon bright. A guiding star. For all to see. For all to learn.
ELEANOR. He must return. He must be safe. The mill awaits him. His friends await. We need his smile. We need his wit. We need his truth. To grace our lives. May God protect him. May angels guard. His every step. His every thought. His every deed. From harm and ill.
MASTER JOHN. He will return. He will, I know. His honest way. It conquers all. It breaks the chains. Of false pretence. It shines a light. On darkest deeds. He is a man. Of true renown. A simple soul. A hero true.
(They drink, then sit in pensive silence, worry etched on their faces as the tavern noise fades.)

(ACT THREE, SCENE ONE)
(The roads leading to Baron Thorne's lands. SIR ROGER is riding a sturdy horse, dressed in practical travelling clothes, still simple, but now bearing the crest of the Duke's messenger. LORD GREGORY rides beside him. They are followed by a small escort of two guards.)

LORD GREGORY. The path is long. The way is hard. But courage, Roger. You ride for truth.
SIR ROGER. My Lord Gregory. My heart is firm. But my mind ponders. This weighty task. To look with care. At Thorne's domain. To speak with folk. Of need and want. It is a work. I take most seriously.
LORD GREGORY. The Duke's trust. It honours you. It honours all. Who value worth. Beyond the show. The Baron Thorne. He is a man. Of fickle temper. And sudden rage. His coffers, too. They seem quite bare. While his own lands. Are rich and vast. It is a puzzle. A mystery.
SIR ROGER. I shall observe. I shall inquire. With gentle voice. With open mind. The common man. He knows the truth. He feels the sting. Of every wrong. I shall listen close. To what they say. To what they sigh. To what they whisper.
LORD GREGORY. Indeed, Sir Roger. That is your gift. Your gentle way. It wins all hearts. Unlike some men. Who shout and bluster. And blind themselves. With their own noise. We near the border. Of Thorne's domain. The air grows cold. The shadows long.
(They ride on. A lone figure is seen watching them from a thicket by the roadside. It is MASTER FINCH, his face grim and vengeful. He holds a small scroll.)

MASTER FINCH. (To himself, with a hiss.) So, the miller rides. A ducal envoy! What arrogance! What foolish pride! The Duke, he errs. He makes a fool. Of noble blood. By raising dirt. But I will watch. I will ensnare. This honest fool. In webs of lies. His simple heart. His trusting mind. Will be undone. By clever schemes. I have my plan. A subtle plan. To taint his name. To crush his fame. To prove him false. To prove him weak. The Duke will see. His great mistake. (He smiles a cruel smile, clutches the scroll, and melts back into the shadows.)

(ACT THREE, SCENE TWO)
(Baron Thorne's main village. It is quiet, unusually so. The houses look rundown. Few people are about. Those seen look gaunt and weary. SIR ROGER and LORD GREGORY dismount. Their guards stay with the horses.)

SIR ROGER. This silence here. It chills my bones. A heavy cloak. Upon this place. Where are the children? Where are the voices?
LORD GREGORY. A bad sign, Roger. A very bad sign. The fields seem fallow. The gates stand shut. This is not famine. Of nature's hand. This is misrule. This is neglect.
(An old woman, bent and frail, emerges from a cottage. She carries a small, empty basket.)

OLD WOMAN. Good sirs. What brings you? To our sad village? We have no coin. We have no food. To offer guests.
SIR ROGER. (Approaching her gently.) Good mother. We mean no harm. We come from the Duke. To learn your plight. To hear your woes. Tell me, old dame. What troubles you? Why are your fields. So bare and poor?
OLD WOMAN. The Baron's men. They take our grain. Each harvest time. They seize our stores. They leave us naught. But bitter straw. For beast and man. We starve slowly. We waste away. Our children cry. Our futures fade.
LORD GREGORY. But the Duke's own records. They show good yield. From Thorne's rich lands. What do you mean? They seize your stores?
OLD WOMAN. The Baron's steward. Master Alaric. He boasts of plenty. He sends false counts. To our good Duke. But here, alas. The truth is bare. Our barns are empty. Our stomachs too. He sells our grain. In distant lands. For his own profit. His own vile purse.
SIR ROGER. (His face clouding with concern.) This is grave news. Most truly grave. Does the Baron know? Of this harsh deed?
OLD WOMAN. The Baron, sir? He feasts and drinks. He hunts his deer. He sees no truth. He trusts his men. He trusts Alaric. And cares for naught. But his own ease. His lavish life. He lets them bleed us. He lets us die. For lack of care. For lack of food.
SIR ROGER. (Taking a small piece of bread from his pouch and offering it to her.) Here, good mother. A little comfort. May it sustain you. For a short while.
OLD WOMAN. (Her eyes widening, she takes the bread with trembling hands.) Oh, noble sir! A true kind heart! Such goodness found! In these dark days! May God reward you! May Heaven bless! Your generous hand. Your gentle soul. (She begins to eat, tears running down her wrinkled cheeks.)
LORD GREGORY. This steward, Alaric. He bears watching. He bears swift judgment. If this be true.
SIR ROGER. It must be true. Her eyes speak truth. Her hunger speaks. A louder truth. We must proceed. With utmost care. And gather proof. Before we act. Let us find lodging. And then observe. This Alaric. This cruel man.
(They leave the old woman, who continues to eat the bread, and move through the village, their expressions stern. SIR ROGER looks around, seeing the signs of neglect and suffering everywhere.)

(ACT THREE, SCENE THREE)
(Later that day, in a dingy inn in Baron Thorne's village. SIR ROGER and LORD GREGORY are seated, disguised somewhat, wearing plainer cloaks. The two guards are outside, keeping watch. SIR ROGER is writing in a small notebook.)

SIR ROGER. The tale is grim. From every lip. The people suffer. The land is bled. Alaric, the steward. He holds the power. He takes the grain. He cheats the Duke. He starves the folk.
LORD GREGORY. A rogue, indeed! A treacherous dog! We must expose him. Without delay. The Duke will send. His full might here. To bring him low. To right these wrongs.
SIR ROGER. Not yet, my Lord. We need more facts. We need hard proof. A villain's word. Is easily turned. His lies are smooth. His plots are deep. We need to catch him. In his own snare.
LORD GREGORY. What proof do you seek? What evidence? We have the words. Of starving folk.
SIR ROGER. We need the books. The ledgers kept. The records forged. The stolen sums. I heard a whisper. In the village. Of a secret vault. Beneath the manor. Where Alaric hides. His ill-gotten gains. His true accounts.
LORD GREGORY. A bold suggestion! A dangerous plan! To seek such things. Within the manor!
SIR ROGER. Danger it may be. But truth demands it. Without these proofs. Our case is weak. Alaric will deny. The Baron will rage. The Duke will doubt. We must be sure.
LORD GREGORY. How would you enter? The manor's walls? They are well-guarded. At every turn.
SIR ROGER. I have a thought. A simple one. Tomorrow morn. I will approach. The manor gates. As a humble miller. Seeking trade. For special flour. For finest bread. For the Baron's table.
LORD GREGORY. (Surprised.) A miller? A miller's guise? But your ducal crest? Your messenger's badge?
SIR ROGER. I shall remove them. For the nonce. I will be Sir Roger. The honest miller. Once again. My crumpled cloak. It served me well. It spoke my truth. It saved my life. Perhaps again. It can reveal. The hidden lies. The cruel deceit. I know the ways. Of millers true. I know their speech. I know their lore. I can blend in. I can observe. Unseen, unheard. Within their halls.
LORD GREGORY. (Shaking his head in wonder.) Your courage, Roger. It is immense. Your simple wisdom. It far surpasses. Our courtly craft. Our guarded speech. But be most careful. For Alaric's wrath. It knows no bounds. If he suspects.
SIR ROGER. I shall be wary. I shall be swift. My mission clear. My purpose true. To find the vault. To find the proof. To set these folk. From their dark chains. This is my charge. This is my duty.
(Outside the inn, in the darkness, a shadowy figure is seen listening intently near a window, then melting away.)

(ACT THREE, SCENE FOUR)
(The next morning. Baron Thorne's manor gates. A GUARD stands watch. SIR ROGER approaches, dressed in his old, simple miller's clothes, carrying a small, empty sack.)

SIR ROGER. Good sir! A word, if you please.
GUARD. State your business! Who are you?
SIR ROGER. A humble miller. From the east. I bring fine flour. Of special blend. For the Baron's table. To bake his bread. The freshest kind. The whitest grain. I seek the steward. Master Alaric. To offer trade. To offer goods.
GUARD. (Scoffing.) Flour, you say? The Baron has plenty! We are well-stocked! Our coffers full! Be gone, old man! Disturb us not!
SIR ROGER. But this is special! A secret blend! It makes the bread. So light, so sweet! The Baron loves. The finest things. He will be pleased. To taste such bread. I only seek. To see the steward. To make my plea. My simple offer.
(Master Alaric emerges from the gatehouse, a sneering, avaricious man.)

ALARIC. What is this commotion? Who troubles my gate?
GUARD. A miller, sir. With idle tales. Of special flour. And grand designs.
ALARIC. (Looking Sir Roger up and down with disdain.) A miller, eh? And what have you? That our own lands. Cannot provide? We have our mills. We have our grain.

ALARIC. (A flicker of interest in his eye, perhaps thinking of a way to profit.) A rare delight? And what price, miller? Do you demand? For this fine dust?
