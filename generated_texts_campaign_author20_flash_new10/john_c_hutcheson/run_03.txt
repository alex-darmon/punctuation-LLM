"Aye, lad, Mr. Thorne's a man of rules, and no mistake," rumbled Old Silas, his voice like stones grinding, "and if you don't step mighty carefully round his pet charts, he'll have your hide nailed to the mast for a new wind vane." I tried to appear unconcerned, for I saw the old bosun was earnest enough, and only speaking from a kindly caution, born of long years at sea. "I'll do my work with diligence, Silas," I replied, perhaps with more pride than prudence, "and he'll find no fault in a job well done, I trust." "Ah, but 'tis not always the *doing* but the *how* of the doing that nettles a man like Thorne; for he's one of them precise fellows, and if you don't bow and scrape to his antiquated methods, he'll be after you like a shark on a fresh catch." "I am not one to abandon scientific principle for a man's ego, Bosun Blackwood!" I declared, drawing myself up, "I am a graduate of the Royal Academy of Surveying, and Captain Merrick himself assigned me this post aboard the *Stargazer*."

"Now, young Mister Ashton, don't you be letting your books get the better of your common sense, mind you!" Silas chuckled, a dry sound like canvas flapping, though his eyes held a glint of approval at my spirit. "A young 'un with too much schooling, as we calls him, soon finds himself adrift if he can't weather the squalls of an officer's temper! You needn't go for to truly scorn Mr. Thorne's old ways; I only meant you to nod and smile like as how you thinks him a very Solomon of the seas, and then you'll keep your berth without a ripple in the calm--Mr. Henderson, the navigator, is a sensible sort, and you'll find him easy enough to work alongside." "Very well," I said in answer, as if consenting to his experienced counsel; but I resolved in my own heart that I would not bend to mere prejudice should Mr. Thorne attempt to obstruct my duties. He would find no weak-kneed compliance in me, I swore! The rest of my shipmates, Silas then proceeded to inform me, were a sturdy and diligent company, forming as fine a crew as he'd ever sailed with--fit for any task, and all proper seamen "of the old school." Fincher, the carpenter, he said, was a quiet, practical, and unassuming fellow, who kept to his tools and his work; while Old Tom, the sail-master, whom I should have listed in his rightful place among the petty officers, was a jovial soul, who could spin a yarn and sing a hearty shanty on a calm evening in the forecastle; but, the very spirit of the crew, Silas added, was Barney O'Malley, the cook, an Irishman, as his cheerful grin would attest. He was always ready to tell a tall tale and "raise a bit of a ruckus" when spirits needed lifting, besides always willing to lend a hand in any sudden emergency. Silas advised me "to be sure and make friends with Barney," if only for the sake of the unexpected hot mug of cocoa he might dispense on a frigid dawn watch. "Indeed, you've seen a few tides, Bosun Blackwood!" I observed, when he shared this valuable piece of counsel with me, as one of the essential secrets of maintaining good cheer at sea. "Ay, Mister Ashton," he replied, with a knowing wink; "I've not been sailing these forty years for nothing, I can tell you." The ship's clerk--to finish his account of the main figures on board--was a fussy, timid sort of man from Bristol, who had a peculiar way of counting every biscuit; and, as he was forever trying to curry favour with the chief cartographer, I need hardly add he was no favourite either of my friend Silas, or with the crew generally--everyone believing that he hoarded supplies for his own comfort, out of deference to Mr. Thorne's general air of entitlement! The *Stargazer*, then, carried thirty-two souls in all of living human cargo, including the Captain and my eager self, besides her weighty instruments and provisions; we on board representing a small, self-contained world, with our fates intertwined so long as our expedition should continue. While Silas and I were conversing by the mainmast, I noticed Mr. Thorne--who had the forenoon watch, and was supervising the work on deck--approach close to the chart room door, and pause in his pacing once or twice, as if he were about to call out and demand to know the subject of our discussion; but something seemed to divert his attention, so he merely scowled in our direction, as I fully expected, although he glowered at Silas and me, with a sour look on his pale, precise face, "as if he could measure us for a coffin right then and there," as the bosun later quipped, when I mentioned the cartographer's observation. I believe Mr. Thorne took an immediate dislike to me from the very start; and the Captain's subsequent favour only served to deepen his animosity, as I would discover. That very afternoon, shortly before the midday observation, when Captain Merrick emerged from his cabin with his grand sextant, and found me already prepared with my chronometers, in readiness for his instructions, I heard Mr. Thorne utter a dismissive cough behind the Captain's back concerning me. "Hmph, Captain," he muttered aside to Mr. Henderson, the navigator--"Old Steady" as the men affectionately called him, for his unwavering good nature--"the Captain's taking on the role of a schoolmaster to teach that greenhorn how to take a proper sun-line, you see; did you ever witness such a thing? These are peculiar times when college boys sail to sea for their lessons, and ship captains must stoop to instructing them!" Henderson offered no reply to this except a noncommittal hum, which could have signified anything, but I was quite certain Thorne was attempting to mock my inexperience. Captain Merrick, however, did not appear to catch the remark; and proceeded to test my accuracy with the chronometers, asking me to log the precise time of the sun's meridian passage and the bearings of a distant uncharted island. He was evidently pleased when, a little later, I had completed my calculations, based on the observed altitude and time, and, having applied the necessary corrections for drift and current, found out that our true position on the charts was very nearly 17 degrees 23 minutes 15 seconds South and 145 degrees 10 minutes 30 seconds East, placing us precisely at the mouth of the newly discovered reef passage. It was a labour of both skill and theoretical understanding, however, for the passage was indeed narrow, and we had only recently taken our "point of departure" from the last known landmass; while soundings were becoming increasingly critical, requiring constant vigilance; so, there was considerable importance in our taking such a precise observation early in the voyage. The Captain had done it not merely to test my knowledge, but also to confirm the *Stargazer*'s exact location, and he was thoroughly satisfied with the result, it seemed. "Why, Thorne," he said to the chief cartographer, who stood by with an air of barely concealed skepticism on his face, hoping to hear of my computational errors, "this young man has charted us with more precision than any I've seen in years!" This, you may be assured, did not sit well with the cartographer, who grumbled something about it's "all being by pure chance and newfangled gadgets." But the Captain would not tolerate such dismissal. "No, no, Thorne," he retorted, sharply, "it is all rigorous calculation and careful observation, such as I could not have expected from one so young;" then, turning to me, he added, with a warm smile, "you continue with this dedication, young Ashton, and you'll be mapping new worlds before we reach the Southern Ice!" The Captain desired me, furthermore, to assist in the daily charting and to maintain a separate log of all astronomical data, the same as the chief cartographer had to do, which that individual interpreted as a direct slight and a check upon his authority, and consequently grew to despise me all the more. As I subsequently discovered, he was quite resistant to modern methods of hydrographic survey, and wary of any innovation that might expose his reliance on older, less precise techniques, therefore, he feared his occasional inaccuracies might be too easily revealed if his log were compared daily with mine. Unaware of the true depth of these hostile sentiments towards me, Captain Merrick sealed Mr. Thorne's boiling resentment by inviting me to join him in his cabin for dinner that day at the first bell of the second dog watch, instead of awaiting the conclusion of Mr. Henderson's duty, and partaking of the "second mess," as was the customary practice in scientific expeditions. Thorne almost sputtered with indignation when he overheard the Captain's invitation. His precise, pale face turned a mottled crimson, like a overripe plum! "Indeed, indeed, Captain Merrick," said he, with a mock deference that scarcely masked his fury: "but I had not understood that I shipped aboard this vessel to dine with mere apprentices." The Captain's eyes flashed with a sudden, icy glint, a fire not easily extinguished. "And I would have you understand, Mr. Thorne," he countered, swiftly, enunciating every word with deliberate force, "that Mr. Ashton is not a 'mere apprentice,' but a highly valued scientific officer, whose contributions are precisely why we are making such excellent progress; and his company at my table is entirely at my discretion, without need for your approval!"

The Captain's words, delivered with such cold, measured force, seemed to hang in the crisp sea air, stripping Thorne of any remaining veneer of civility. The cartographer’s mottled face, having momentarily frozen in a grotesque rictus of indignation, now slackened, his thin lips working soundlessly for a moment as if attempting to formulate a retort that simply would not come. He knew, as did every man aboard the *Stargazer*, that to challenge Captain Merrick on the quarterdeck, especially after such a direct and public reprimand, would be an act of professional suicide; therefore, he merely inclined his head stiffly, a movement more akin to a rigid mechanism grinding into place than a genuine bow of respect, and then, without so much as a glance in my direction, pivoted sharply on his heel and strode away, his retreating figure radiating a palpable aura of thwarted malice. His resentment, I understood then, was not merely superficial, a passing pique, but a deep seated, festering wound that had now been pricked by the Captain's precise and unsparing language, and I could feel its unseen tendrils reaching out, already seeking to ensnare me.

For my own part, I stood somewhat transfixed, a peculiar mixture of relief and unease washing over me. Relief, naturally, that the Captain had so unequivocally championed my cause and rebuked Thorne’s insolence; but unease, too, for I was not so naive as to believe that such a public humiliation would temper Thorne’s animosity, rather, I suspected it would only serve to harden his resolve to see me fail, to prove my youth and inexperience a fatal flaw, even if it meant jeopardizing the expedition itself. The Captain, however, seemed entirely unconcerned by the lingering fallout of the exchange. He turned to me, his stern features relaxing once more into that familiar, albeit rare, warm smile. "Do not let such small mindedness trouble you, young Ashton," he said, his voice now modulated to a gentler tone, "for you have proven your worth, and that is what matters aboard this vessel. Go now, prepare yourself for dinner. Seven bells, Captain's cabin." With a curt nod of dismissal, he then turned to address the first officer, leaving me to grapple with the emotional wake of the confrontation.

I made my way below decks, the planks beneath my feet feeling less stable than usual, and the familiar scent of tar and salt and damp wood seeming imbued with a new tension. My cabin, a compact space I shared with a junior medical officer who was presently on duty, offered little sanctuary from my swirling thoughts. I sat on the edge of my bunk, running a hand through my hair, a nervous tremor still coursing through my veins. The implications of the Captain's invitation were profound. To dine in the Captain's cabin was not merely a privilege; it was a mark of extraordinary favor, an elevation that bypassed rank and custom, signaling a direct personal trust. It set me apart, not only from Mr. Thorne, but from almost every other officer on board, and while it brought with it an undeniable surge of pride, it also brought a chilling realization of the isolation it might engender. How many more would view me with Thorne’s same jealousy, albeit perhaps less overtly expressed? I had never sought such distinction; my only desire was to serve with diligence and to apply my knowledge to the best of my abilities. Now, however, I was inextricably linked to the Captain’s authority, a pawn, however valued, in the intricate social chess game of naval life.

I spent the next hour meticulously preparing myself. My uniform, though not new, was brushed clean, my brass buttons polished to a gleam, my hair neatly combed. I wanted to present myself with the utmost respect for the occasion, to demonstrate that I understood the gravity of the Captain's invitation. As the seven bells sounded, its clear chime echoing through the narrow passages of the ship, I made my way to the Captain's cabin. The door, usually kept slightly ajar during daylight hours, was now firmly closed. I hesitated for a moment, took a deep breath, and rapped softly. A moment later, I heard a curt "Enter," and I pushed the door open.

The Captain’s cabin was, as I had always imagined, a study in purposeful austerity. It was larger than any other private quarters on the ship, yet it contained nothing superfluous. A sturdy desk, laden with charts and navigational instruments, dominated one side, while a simple bunk, neatly made, occupied another. A small, well stocked library of books, their spines worn with use, filled a set of shelves affixed securely to the bulkhead. A single, oil lamp, swung in gimbals to counteract the ship’s motion, cast a warm, steady glow, illuminating the space with an intimate, somewhat scholarly air. The air itself, though confined, was surprisingly fresh, a testament to the Captain’s meticulous standards for cleanliness and ventilation. Captain Merrick stood by the desk, his back to me, examining a chart with an intense, almost proprietary air. He turned as I entered, a faint smile touching his lips. "Ah, Ashton, do come in. Make yourself comfortable." He gestured towards a small, round table set for two, near the stern window, which was currently shuttered against the encroaching evening and the rising chill.

I saluted, then quickly moved to the table, taking the seat he indicated, a surprisingly comfortable, albeit fixed, chair upholstered in dark leather. The table itself was simple, made of polished mahogany, and already bore two plates, two glasses, and a bottle of what appeared to be claret. A ship’s steward, a quiet, efficient man named Jenkins, entered almost immediately, bearing a tureen of steaming soup, which he served with practiced ease, his movements fluid despite the gentle roll of the ship. "Thank you, Jenkins," the Captain said, his voice even and polite. "That will be all for now." Jenkins bowed slightly and withdrew, leaving us in a comfortable silence that was broken only by the creak of the ship’s timbers and the distant rush of water against the hull.

The soup was a rich, hearty broth, undoubtedly beef, brimming with finely chopped vegetables. It was a far cry from the often bland and repetitive fare served in the mess, and I savored each spoonful. The Captain, observing my appreciation, chuckled softly. "A small luxury, I admit," he said, "but a man must have his comforts, even at sea. Especially when heading into the desolate reaches of the Southern Ocean." He then paused, his gaze thoughtful. "Tell me, Ashton, what are your thoughts on our progress thus far? Do you still find your instruments behaving as expected?"

I put down my spoon, considering my words carefully. "Captain, the chronometers maintain their rate with remarkable consistency, and my sextant is proving an invaluable companion. The conditions, though already growing colder, have been favorable for observations, and the *Stargazer* is proving herself a stable platform. I have no doubt that our position, as determined today, was accurate to within the finest margins of modern capability." I spoke with genuine conviction, for I truly believed it.

The Captain nodded, a flicker of satisfaction in his eyes. "Precisely. And that, my young friend, is why you are here. This expedition requires the utmost precision, the most rigorous application of scientific method. We are not merely sailing; we are charting, observing, discovering. Every league we traverse must be recorded with unimpeachable accuracy, for we venture into waters where none have gone before, at least not with the instruments available to us now." He took a sip of his claret, his gaze distant, as if already peering into the uncharted vastness ahead. "I have no patience for those who cling to outdated notions, who resist the advance of knowledge for the sake of comfort or, worse, professional jealousy. The sea, Ashton, cares nothing for one’s pride or one’s antiquated methods. It demands respect, and it punishes carelessness with an unforgiving hand."

It was clear he was referring to Thorne, though he did not speak his name. The Captain's unspoken warning hung in the air, reminding me that my elevation came with its own set of dangers. "I understand, Captain," I replied, my voice a little lower than before. "I shall endeavor to uphold the highest standards of accuracy in all my work."

"I have no doubt that you shall," he said, a reassuring firmness in his tone. "Indeed, your contributions are already proving vital. The separate log I asked you to maintain, it is progressing well?"

"Yes, Captain. It is meticulously kept, with all astronomical observations, chronometer readings, and my own calculations recorded in duplicate, as you instructed."

"Excellent. It is a necessary safeguard." He paused, his expression growing more serious. "This voyage, Ashton, is not merely about finding a new continent, should one exist. It is about pushing the boundaries of human understanding, about demonstrating that science, when applied with dedication and intellect, can conquer the most formidable challenges. There will be hardships. There will be dangers. And there will be those who, from fear or ignorance, will seek to impede our progress. But we must not falter. Our mission is too important." He looked at me then, his gaze piercing. "Are you prepared for such a journey, young man? For the isolation, the cold, the relentless demands?"

"I am, Captain," I said, meeting his gaze squarely, a surge of resolve hardening my own spirit. "I am prepared for whatever challenges lie ahead. My commitment to this expedition is absolute."

He held my gaze for another moment, then a slow smile spread across his face. "Good. That is what I wished to hear. Now, let us have some more of this excellent beef, for we have many leagues yet to cover before we see the Southern Ice."

The rest of the dinner passed in a more convivial manner. We spoke of my studies, of the advances in navigation, of the history of exploration. The Captain, I discovered, possessed a surprisingly detailed knowledge of astronomical theory and the mechanics of chronometers, questioning me on the intricacies of their construction and the various methods for compensating for temperature fluctuations. It was a rigorous examination, but one I welcomed, for it allowed me to demonstrate my understanding beyond mere rote calculation. He spoke, too, of the *Stargazer* herself, of her sturdy construction, her powerful engines, and her specialized equipment for scientific observation, such as the deep sea sounding apparatus and the improved dredging gear. He explained the ship's purpose with a quiet passion, describing the potential for discovering new species, new geological formations, and, perhaps, even new patterns in the Earth's magnetic field in these uncharted territories. By the time I took my leave, well after the second dog watch had concluded, I felt invigorated, inspired, and more deeply committed to the expedition than ever before. The Captain’s trust was a heavy mantle, but one I was eager to bear.

The days that followed settled into a rhythm dictated by the ship and the sea. The *Stargazer* pressed steadily southward, her engines a constant thrum beneath our feet, the bow cutting a crisp furrow through the increasingly grey and turbulent waters. The wind, hitherto a mere breeze, now began to gather strength, carrying with it the unmistakable bite of the polar regions. The air grew perceptibly colder each day, and the crew, once accustomed to lighter attire, now bundled themselves in thick woolens and oilskins. I, too, found myself donning additional layers, marveling at the gradual transformation of our surroundings from the familiar blue of temperate seas to a palette of steel grey and churning white.

My duties were constant and demanding. Every morning, weather permitting, I took a round of sights with my sextant, verifying the ship's position against the rising sun. Throughout the day, I meticulously recorded chronometer readings, barometric pressure, air and sea temperatures, and the ship's speed and course. The evenings, when the stars emerged from the deepening twilight, were often spent on the quarterdeck, the biting wind whipping at my exposed face, as I sought to fix our position by stellar observation, cross referencing my findings with the Captain’s navigator, Lieutenant Davies, a quiet, competent man who, unlike Thorne, seemed to appreciate the benefits of modern instruments, even if he did not fully understand their intricacies. My separate log, a heavy tome bound in leather, became my constant companion, filled with precise figures, careful diagrams, and my own observations on the reliability of the instruments and the vagaries of the weather.

Thorne, meanwhile, remained a constant, albeit often silent, shadow. He made no further overt challenges to my authority or my presence at the Captain’s table. Instead, his animosity manifested itself in more insidious ways. He would scrutinize my calculations with an almost obsessive zeal, his pale eyes narrowing as he ran his finger down my columns of figures, as if hoping to find some glaring error. On more than one occasion, I would find a piece of my equipment subtly displaced in the chartroom, a pencil moved from its usual position, a ruler laid askew, minor things, certainly, but enough to convey a sense of purposeful disruption, a passive aggressive assertion of his displeasure. He would offer unsolicited "advice" in a saccharine tone, suggesting "older, more reliable" methods that would invariably lead to less precise results, or make dismissive remarks about "the impracticality of youth and its fascination with complicated mechanisms" within earshot of junior officers, attempting to sow seeds of doubt about my competence.

One afternoon, as we approached a region where the charts indicated a complex series of submerged shoals, Thorne approached me in the chartroom. "Mr. Ashton," he began, his voice surprisingly bland, "I observe you are preparing to take a sounding with your… electronic device." He gestured with a dismissive flick of his wrist towards my recently acquired sonic depth finder, a marvel of modern engineering that used sound waves to measure the ocean floor, replacing the laborious and often inaccurate method of lead and line. "A curious contraption, to be sure. But I find the old ways best in these tricky waters. One can never trust these newfangled gadgets. Give me a good, heavy lead and an experienced hand any day." He smiled, a thin, humourless stretching of his lips.

I maintained my composure, despite the prickle of annoyance his words invariably produced. "With all due respect, Mr. Thorne, the sonic depth finder provides continuous readings with far greater accuracy and speed than a lead line, especially in deep water or when the ship is making way. It allows for a comprehensive profile of the seabed, which is invaluable in uncharted or poorly charted areas such as this."

"Ah, accuracy, yes, always accuracy," he drawled, his voice dripping with condescension. "But what of reliability? What if your little box should cease to function? The sea is a harsh mistress, Mr. Ashton, and she cares naught for your delicate electronics. Trust me, a man of my experience knows the dangers of over reliance on untested technology." He then turned to the helmsman, who stood nearby awaiting orders, and offered a pointed warning. "Be careful, lad, with these new instruments. The Captain is fond of them, but old sailors know that innovation sometimes sails a perilous course."

Such incidents, though outwardly minor, chipped away at the camaraderie that usually prevailed amongst officers on such voyages. Thorne’s constant, understated campaign of obstruction was a subtle poison, designed to isolate me, to undermine my credibility with those below decks, and to plant seeds of doubt in the minds of anyone who might observe our interactions. I, however, refused to be drawn into open argument. I continued my work with unwavering diligence, allowing my precise observations and the clear, undeniable data to speak for themselves. The Captain, it seemed, was well aware of Thorne’s tactics. He never directly intervened in these minor skirmishes, but he continued to seek my counsel on navigational matters, often asking for my readings first, or subtly referencing my separate log during his discussions with Lieutenant Davies, thereby reaffirming his trust in my methods.

As weeks turned into months, the character of the voyage began to change dramatically. The vibrant blues of the open ocean gave way to a somber, brooding grey, often shrouded in a heavy mist that condensed on every surface, lending the ship a perpetually damp and melancholic air. The first icebergs appeared, ghostly white behemoths drifting silently through the fog, their vast, submerged bulk a constant, terrifying reminder of the hidden dangers that luroked beneath the surface. We had entered the realm of the Southern Ice, and the *Stargazer*, though stoutly built and reinforced for polar waters, moved with a newfound caution, her powerful engines often throttled back to a slow, methodical churn. The creaking of the ship’s timbers, once a reassuring lullaby, now seemed to carry a note of strain, a protest against the immense forces of nature that surrounded us.

Navigational challenges multiplied exponentially. The magnetic compass became increasingly unreliable as we approached the South Magnetic Pole, requiring constant corrections and relying more heavily on the gyroscopic compass, a relatively new invention Thorne viewed with profound suspicion. The sun, when it appeared at all through the thick clouds, hung low in the sky, a pale, watery disc offering scant warmth and often obscured for days on end, making accurate celestial observations difficult. The sonic depth finder, however, became an indispensable tool, its continuous readings painting a vivid, albeit abstract, picture of the underwater landscape, allowing us to weave a safe path through uncharted shallows and submerged ridges that would have been utterly imperceptible to the traditional lead line.

It was during one particularly dense and protracted period of fog, thick as a shroud, that Thorne’s animosity, combined with his reliance on outdated methods, finally precipitated a crisis. For three days, visibility had been reduced to mere ship lengths, the world outside the ship’s rails a featureless grey void. Icebergs, once majestic and distant, now loomed suddenly out of the mist, their cold, white faces appearing with startling abruptness, necessitating frantic rudder adjustments and engine maneuvers. The Captain had ordered a reduced speed, and the incessant clang of the ice bell from the crow’s nest became a constant, nerve wracking accompaniment to our progress.

On the morning of the fourth day, as I stood my watch in the chartroom, carefully monitoring the sonic depth finder's display and plotting its readings, Thorne entered, his face drawn and pale, his usual sneer replaced by an expression of anxiety. "Mr. Ashton," he began, his voice unusually strained, "the Captain requires our utmost attention. My calculations, based upon our dead reckoning and a brief glimpse of the sun two days past, indicate we are approaching a region of potentially dangerous, uncharted shoals, far more extensive than previously believed. I am concerned that your electronic device may be giving erroneous readings due to the density of the fog, perhaps refracting the sound waves in an unpredictable manner."

I looked up from my chart, surprised by his sudden, if begrudging, acknowledgement of my instrument. "Mr. Thorne, the sonic depth finder is designed to operate independently of atmospheric conditions. Its readings are clear and consistent. My log, compiled from continuous data over the past seventy two hours, shows a gradual deepening of the seabed, trending away from any immediate danger from shoals, though I agree we must remain vigilant for isolated pinnacles of rock."

Thorne scoffed, a desperate, almost fearful sound. "Continuous data, yes, but what of its *veracity*? My charts, based on the accumulated wisdom of generations of navigators, show a different story! I have cross referenced my calculations with Lieutenant Davies's log, and we are in agreement: a dangerous bank lies directly ahead, perhaps within the hour, if we continue on this course!" He pointed a trembling finger at a hastily sketched mark on his own, heavily annotated chart.

A cold dread coiled in my stomach. To dismiss Thorne entirely would be reckless, yet my instruments, precise and unyielding, contradicted his assertion. His mention of Lieutenant Davies, however, lent his claim a degree of urgency. I knew Davies was a good officer, though perhaps too easily swayed by Thorne’s seniority. "Mr. Thorne," I said, my voice firm despite my internal apprehension, "I shall recheck my instruments, and double verify my chronometer rates. But the sonic depth finder has proven itself reliable throughout this voyage."

Before I could complete my reexamination, the Captain himself entered the chartroom, his presence immediately dominating the confined space. His face, usually impassive, was now etched with concern. "Report, gentlemen. Lieutenant Davies has relayed Mr. Thorne’s concerns. Are we indeed in such immediate peril, Thorne?"

Thorne stepped forward, his eyes darting from the Captain to me. "Captain, with all due respect to Mr. Ashton's modern instruments, my calculations, corroborated by Lieutenant Davies's own dead reckoning, suggest we are on a collision course with a dangerous, uncharted bank. The fog makes visual navigation impossible, and I fear these new instruments may be misleading us. I urge you, sir, to alter course immediately, perhaps twenty points to port, and reduce speed further."

The Captain turned to me, his gaze sharp and unwavering. "Ashton? Your assessment?"

"Captain," I began, my voice clear and steady, despite the pressure, "my sonic depth finder has been operating flawlessly for the past three days. The continuous profile of the seabed indicates a gradual increase in depth, showing no sudden shoaling ahead of our current position. While I cannot discount the possibility of an isolated hazard that may have escaped detection, the overall trend is clear. Furthermore, my celestial observations from the morning, though taken through intermittent breaks in the fog, place us approximately five miles to the east of Mr. Thorne’s calculated position, a difference that would entirely negate the immediate danger of his reported bank."

Thorne let out a gasp of indignation. "Five miles! Impossible! Your chronometers must have drifted, young man! Or your observations were flawed! The magnetic compass, too, is erratic in these latitudes, everyone knows that!"

"My chronometers are rated daily, Mr. Thorne, and their rates remain remarkably consistent," I retorted, a flash of anger, momentarily eclipsing my deference, "and I utilize the gyroscopic compass for all course corrections in these magnetic anomalies, as per standing orders, Captain." I turned back to Merrick. "Captain, I recommend we maintain our current course for another half hour, while increasing the frequency of sonic soundings to maximum. Should any shoaling be detected, we can then take immediate evasive action. Altering course based solely on a dead reckoning that contradicts instrument readings of this fidelity would, I submit, introduce an unnecessary element of risk, potentially steering us into *unknown* dangers that my instruments have not yet surveyed."

The Captain stood silently for a moment, his eyes moving from Thorne’s agitated face to my own resolute one, then to the various charts spread before us. The air in the chartroom was thick with tension, the fate of the *Stargazer* and all aboard resting on his decision, and by extension, on whose assessment he chose to trust. His expression was unreadable, but I sensed the immense weight of responsibility that bore down upon him. He knew Thorne's limitations, his inherent distrust of new technology, but he also knew the inherent unpredictability of the sea, especially in such poorly charted waters.

After what seemed an eternity, he finally spoke, his voice calm, measured, and decisive. "Mr. Thorne, your concerns are noted, and I appreciate your vigilance. However, Mr. Ashton's instruments provide continuous, real time data that, in conditions such as these, offer a far more immediate and verifiable picture of the seabed than dead reckoning alone. We shall maintain our current course, as Mr. Ashton suggests, but at a further reduced speed. Mr. Ashton, continue with your intensified soundings. Report any changes, however slight, immediately. Lieutenant Davies will stand by the helm, ready for any maneuver."

Thorne’s face crumpled, a mask of defeat and resentment. He opened his mouth as if to protest, but the Captain raised a hand, silencing him. "That is my order, Mr. Thorne. I will have no further debate on the matter."

The next thirty minutes were agonizing. The only sounds in the chartroom were the quiet hum of my sonic depth finder, the rhythmic clicking of my pen as I recorded the readings, and the muffled thrum of the ship’s engines. I watched the display with an intensity that burned my eyes, my breath held tight in my chest. Each reading confirmed the last: the seabed continued its gentle slope downward, offering no indication of the treacherous bank Thorne had so confidently predicted.

Then, just as the half hour was drawing to a close, a shout from the crow’s nest pierced the dense fog. "Iceberg dead ahead! Fine on the starboard bow!"

My head snapped up, and even Thorne, who had been glowering silently in a corner, flinched. The Captain, however, remained outwardly calm. "Helm, hard a port! Engines, full astern!" he bellowed, his voice cutting through the tension.

The ship shuddered violently as the engines reversed, and the helm swung hard over. A monstrous, ghostly shape began to materialize out of the swirling grey, a colossal mass of ancient ice, its jagged peaks shrouded in mist, its sheer face appearing impossibly close. It was a close call, a terrifying dance with destruction, but the *Stargazer*, responding to the Captain’s expert command, swung wide, the great iceberg sliding past our starboard side with only yards to spare, its cold breath sweeping across the deck.

"Mr. Ashton, your unwavering trust in your instruments, and your precise application of modern scientific methods, have averted a potentially catastrophic incident.
