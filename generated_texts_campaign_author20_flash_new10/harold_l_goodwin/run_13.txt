The sun dipped below the jagged peaks, painting the desert sky in shades of bruised purple and orange. Maya shivered. “Are you sure about this, Leo? It’ll be dark soon.”

Leo grinned, his backpack clanking with unseen contents. “Don’t tell me you’re scared. It’s just an old building. Everyone says it’s empty.” He pushed aside a tangle of thorny brush, revealing a cracked concrete path. Rusting pipes snaked along the ground.

“That’s what everyone says about ghost towns too,” Maya muttered, but she followed him. The air grew cooler, carrying a metallic tang she couldn’t quite place. They moved cautiously along the perimeter fence, looking for a way in. A section of chain-link had been torn away, leaving a gaping hole.

“Jackpot,” Leo whispered. He squeezed through, holding the wire apart for Maya. She slipped through, snagging her jacket on a loose barb. They were inside.

The main entrance was a collapsed mess of steel and broken glass. Leo led the way to a smaller service door, half-buried in sand. He put his shoulder against it. It groaned, then swung inward with a shriek of tortured hinges. Darkness engulfed them.

“Flashlight,” Maya whispered, pulling hers from her pocket. The beam cut through the oppressive black, revealing a narrow, dusty corridor. Cobwebs hung like tattered curtains. “Hold on.”

A low hum, almost imperceptible, vibrated through the floor. Leo stopped, head cocked. “Did you hear that?”

“I felt it,” Maya replied, her voice barely audible. “It sounds like… power.”

Their footsteps echoed eerily as they advanced. The hum grew steadily louder. They turned a corner, and a faint light pulsed from under a door ahead. Leo instinctively flattened himself against the wall. Maya crouched beside him, her heart beginning to pound a frantic rhythm. This was not what they expected. This place was supposed to be dead.

A murmur of voices reached them, muffled but distinct. They were speaking in low tones, too low to make out words. Leo glanced at Maya, his eyes wide with a mixture of fear and excitement. She knew what he was thinking. They had to get closer.

They crept forward, moving with agonizing slowness. The door was slightly ajar. Leo peered through the crack, then pulled back quickly. He turned to Maya, his face pale. “There are people in there. Lots of equipment. And… something else.”

“What ‘something else’?” Maya whispered.

Before he could answer, a loud clang echoed from deeper within the facility, followed by a shouted command. Footsteps approached, rapid and heavy. Leo shoved Maya into a recessed alcove, pulling himself in after her. They held their breath as two figures strode past, illuminated briefly by a harsh, flickering overhead light. They wore dark uniforms.

“They’re not supposed to be here yet,” one of them said. His voice was rough.

“No matter. We’ll secure the area,” the other replied.

Maya felt a surge of cold dread. This was no abandoned building. They had stumbled into something serious. Leo’s hand found hers, squeezing tightly. They waited until the footsteps faded, then he nudged her. “We have to go. Now.”

They backed out of the alcove, moving swiftly and silently down the corridor. The hum seemed to throb with menace now. They reached the service door. Leo pushed it, but it wouldn't budge. He pushed harder, grunting with effort. The door was jammed.

“It’s no good,” he whispered, his voice tight with panic. “It’s stuck.”

Suddenly, the overhead light in their corridor flickered on, blazing with blinding intensity. A voice boomed from behind them. “Well, well, what have we here?”

A heavy hand clamped down on Leo’s shoulder. Another grabbed Maya’s arm, twisting it painfully. She gasped, dropping her flashlight. It clattered to the floor, plunging them into sudden shadow as the light bulb above them exploded with a sharp pop.

“Don’t move, kids,” a gruff voice commanded. Strong hands searched them quickly, expertly removing Leo’s pocketknife and Maya’s small multi-tool.

“Just a couple of snoopers,” another voice said, sounding bored. “What do we do with them?”

“Lock them up. The holding cell in Sub-Level B should do,” the gruff voice decided. “We’re on a tight schedule.”

They were roughly marched through a maze of corridors, past doors marked with strange symbols. Maya tried to memorize the path, counting turns, but it was useless in the dim, chaotic light of their captors’ flashlights. Down a flight of grimy metal stairs they went, then through another heavy door.

“In here,” a guard ordered, pushing them forward. They stumbled into a small, windowless room. The air was stale and metallic. There was a single steel cot bolted to one wall.

“Tie them to the cot,” the bored voice said. “Make it quick.”

Rough ropes, stiff with age, were quickly secured around their wrists and ankles, binding them tightly to the cold metal frame of the cot. Maya struggled, but it was useless. The knots were professional, unyielding.

“They won’t be going anywhere,” the gruff voice said with a chuckle. “We’ll be gone long before they can wiggle out of that.”

The door clanged shut, the sound echoing ominously in the small space. Darkness enveloped them once more, broken only by the faint glow of an emergency light strip near the ceiling.

“Well,” Leo whispered, his voice surprisingly calm. “That went well.”

Maya let out a shaky breath. “Very funny. Got any bright ideas, smart guy?”

“Only one,” Leo said, a hint of his old mischievous self returning. “I think I have a loose tooth.”

Maya strained against her bonds, feeling the rough hemp chafing her skin. “A loose tooth? What good is that going to do?”

“Not for me,” Leo clarified. “For the rope. I think I can reach it.” He began to shift, slowly, deliberately, his body twisting against the restraints. Maya watched, hope flickering within her. The hum of the facility thrummed around them, a constant reminder of their predicament. She tried to move her own hands, testing the ropes, searching for any weakness. Her fingers brushed against a small, sharp splinter on the cot frame. It pricked her skin.

“Found something,” she whispered. “A splinter.”

“Good,” Leo grunted, still struggling. “Keep working on it. We’re getting out of here.”

“Good,” Leo grunted, still struggling. “Keep working on it. We’re getting out of here.”

Maya adjusted her position, wincing as the rough hemp abraded her wrists. The small, sharp splinter she had found was embedded in the cold steel frame of the cot, just beneath her right hand. It was barely visible, a tiny sliver of metal, but its edge felt surprisingly keen against her fingertip. She twisted her wrist, trying to bring the rope into contact with the splinter. It was awkward, painful. The rope was thick, made of coarse, dried fibers that resisted her efforts. Each small movement sent a jolt of discomfort up her arm, and the persistent pressure of the binding made her fingers tingle. She could feel the blood flow constricting, her skin growing cold.

“Anything yet?” Leo’s voice was strained, muffled. He was still contorting his body, his teeth presumably gnawing at the ropes around his own wrists. Maya imagined the feel of the rough fibers against his gums, the aching jaw, the metallic taste of his own efforts. He sounded determined, though.

“Just trying to get a purchase,” Maya whispered back, her voice tight. She focused on the task, blocking out the thrumming hum of the facility, the chill in the air, the uncertainty of their situation. Her world shrunk to the narrow space between her wrist and the steel cot, the point where the rope met the almost imperceptible sharpness of the splinter. She scraped, pulled, twisted. A single fiber snapped, a faint, almost inaudible pop. Maya felt a surge of triumph, minuscule though it was. “One down,” she breathed. “This is going to take a while.”

“Tell me about it,” Leo muttered. “My molars are going to be blunt instruments by the time we’re done. And the taste of this rope, ugh. I think it’s been dipped in old engine oil.” He paused, then grunted again. “Any luck with your… splinter?”

“It’s slow,” Maya admitted. “But it’s doing something. I think I’m fraying it. The fibers are old. That might help. Or it might just mean they’re too tough.” She continued to work, focusing all her dwindling energy. The splinter was small, and she had to be careful not to break it, or worse, dislodge it from the frame. If it broke, she would be back to square one. Her wrists were already raw, the skin chafed red and angry. A small bead of blood welled up where the rope had rubbed particularly fiercely. She ignored it, her gaze fixed on the task. The rhythmic scraping of the rope against the splinter became a meditative drone, a counterpoint to the distant, unidentifiable sounds of the facility.

Minutes stretched into what felt like hours. Each successful fraying of a rope fiber was a small victory, a glimmer of hope against the overwhelming odds. Leo continued his work in silence, punctuated by occasional grunts and heavy sighs. Maya pictured him, head bent, teeth tearing at the unyielding material. It was a brutal, desperate method, but in their situation, any method was worth trying. Her fingers were growing numb, her arms aching from the constant, unnatural tension.

“I think I’m making progress,” Leo said finally, his voice hoarse but with a hint of renewed energy. “It’s starting to give. Slowly. But it’s giving.”

“Me too,” Maya confirmed. “I can see a thin strand now. It’s not just fraying, it’s actually starting to thin out in one spot.” She shifted again, trying to get a better angle, a stronger leverage. The cot felt colder than ever, its unyielding surface pressing into her back and legs. The ropes were like iron bands, each knot a solid, impassable barrier. But the splinter was a tiny weapon, and she was wielding it with a desperate precision born of necessity.

More time passed, marked only by the growing ache in their bodies and the increasing piles of discarded rope fibers on the cot and the floor beneath them. Maya’s splinter had managed to cut through several outer layers of the hemp rope, exposing the tightly woven core beneath. It was tougher than the outer layers, more resistant. She had to apply more pressure, twist harder, her breath coming in short, sharp gasps. Sweat beaded on her forehead despite the cool air of the cell. Her arms trembled from exertion.

“Almost there,” Leo whispered, his voice sounding closer now, as if his efforts were also bringing him marginally freer movement. “I think I can feel the knot loosening.”

Maya redoubled her efforts. The splinter was still holding, though she could feel its sharp edge dulling slightly. The rope, however, was clearly weakening. A significant portion of the fibers had been severed, and the remaining ones stretched taut, groaning under the strain. She could almost feel the tension in the material, the point where it would finally give way. She pushed harder, one last, desperate effort.

With a sudden, muffled snap, the last few strands of rope around Maya’s right wrist gave way. Her hand, previously bound tightly against the cot frame, sprang free. A wave of intense pain shot through her wrist as blood rushed back into the constricted capillaries, followed quickly by a tingling numbness. She gasped, letting out a small cry of relief and pain.

“Leo!” she whispered, her voice trembling. “I’m free! My right hand is free!”

“You are?” Leo replied, his voice filled with surprise and renewed hope. “That’s fantastic! Now help me.”

Maya quickly assessed her situation. Her right hand was free, but her left wrist and both ankles were still tightly bound. The immediate priority was to free her other hand. It was much easier now. With her right hand, though still sore and stiff, she could reach the knot on her left wrist. The splinter was still in the cot frame, but she no longer needed it. She worked quickly, her fingers fumbling at the tightly drawn knots. The guards had been professional, but they hadn’t anticipated a free hand. The rough hemp chafed against her raw skin, but she ignored it, driven by urgency.

It took a few agonizing minutes, but eventually, the knot on her left wrist loosened and then untied. Both her hands were free. She rubbed her wrists, trying to restore circulation, flexing her fingers. They felt clumsy, uncooperative.

“Okay, I’m free,” she whispered. “Now what about your ankles?”

“Forget my ankles for a second,” Leo said, his voice closer now. “My right wrist is almost done. Just a few more strands. If you could just… pull on it, maybe?”

Maya twisted her body, trying to reach him. Her ankles were still tied, but with her hands free, she had more leverage. She struggled, straining against the ankle ropes until she was able to lean far enough to touch Leo’s arm. His wrist was indeed mangled, the rope mostly shredded. A thin, dark line of blood was visible where the rope had rubbed his skin raw. He was still straining, his teeth gritted.

“Hold still,” Maya commanded softly. She grabbed the mangled rope around his right wrist, digging her fingers into the frayed fibers. She pulled, hard, grunting with effort. The remaining strands, weakened by Leo’s persistent gnawing, snapped with a series of sharp pops. Leo cried out, not in pain, but in a mixture of relief and surprise, as his right hand shot free.

“Yes!” he breathed, rubbing his wrist. “Thank you, thank you, thank you!” He sounded exhausted, but invigorated. “My tooth… it’s definitely loose now. Maybe even missing.” He didn’t elaborate, but Maya could imagine the state of his mouth.

“Don’t worry about your tooth,” Maya said. “Let’s get your other hand, then our ankles.”

With one hand free, Leo quickly untied his left wrist. The guards hadn’t bothered with complex knots for the secondary bindings, assuming the primary ones would hold. Within another minute, both of Leo’s hands were free. They worked together now, their movements more coordinated, their urgency palpable. Leo’s fingers, though sore, were strong. He worked on Maya’s ankles, while she, less constrained now, started on his. The process was much faster with four free hands. The coarse ropes fell away, one by one.

Finally, they were free.

Maya pushed herself up, her muscles screaming in protest. Her legs felt like jelly, pins and needles shooting through them as circulation returned. She stumbled, catching herself on the cold steel cot. Leo was already testing his own legs, swaying slightly, but managing to stand upright. They both stretched, massaging their wrists and ankles, trying to work out the cramps and stiffness.

“Well,” Leo said, a grimace on his face as he rotated his shoulder. “Phase one complete. We’re off the menu, at least for now.” He reached up and touched his mouth. “Definitely a loose tooth. Maybe two. Ugh.”

Maya ignored him, her eyes scanning the small, windowless cell. The single emergency light strip near the ceiling cast a dim, sickly yellow glow over the bare concrete walls. The air was still stale and metallic, tinged now with the faint, sour smell of their own fear and exertion. There was nothing else in the room, no loose bricks, no hidden panels, no vents. Just the steel cot, bolted firmly to the wall, and the heavy, steel door.

“The door,” Maya stated, pointing. “That’s next. Any ideas?”

Leo walked over to the door, pressing his ear against it. He heard nothing but the distant, rhythmic hum of the facility. He felt along the edges, testing for weaknesses, a crack, anything. The door was solid, seamless. There was no visible handle on their side, just a small, flush metal plate where a lock mechanism would be.

“Looks like a magnetic lock, or maybe a high security bolt system,” Leo whispered, stepping back. “No keyhole, no visible latch. Probably controlled from the outside. Or with a keycard.” He ran a hand through his hair, looking frustrated. “And we don’t have a keycard. Or a lockpick kit. Or anything, for that matter. They took everything.”

Maya walked to the far corner of the room, running her hand along the grimy concrete. “There has to be something. A vent. A panel. Anything. They can’t just put people in a box with no way out.”

“They just did, Maya,” Leo reminded her, his voice devoid of humor. “They probably don’t intend for us to get out until they’re good and ready. And ‘ready’ usually means ‘disposed of’.” He kicked lightly at the bottom of the door. It made a dull, solid thud.

They spent the next ten minutes systematically searching every inch of the cell. They ran their hands over the walls, feeling for imperfections, listening for hollow spots. They checked under the cot, though it was bolted to the wall and barely off the ground. They even examined the emergency light strip, hoping it might conceal something, but it was just a sealed fixture.

Nothing. The cell was exactly what it appeared to be: a bare, inescapable box.

Maya slumped against the wall, her initial euphoria draining away, replaced by a fresh wave of despair. “So, what now?” she asked, her voice barely above a whisper. “We’re free of the ropes, but we’re still trapped. It’s worse now, in a way. We had hope, and now…”

Leo sat down on the cot, resting his elbows on his knees, his chin in his hands. He was thinking, his brow furrowed in concentration. “They said Sub-Level B. And they said they were on a tight schedule. They’re not going to be gone that quickly if they’re just leaving us here to rot. They must have a plan for us.”

“A plan that probably doesn’t involve us walking out the front door,” Maya finished for him. She looked at the door again, her eyes narrowing. “What if… what if it’s not meant to be opened from this side at all? What if it’s an automated system?”

“Automated systems still need a trigger,” Leo pointed out. “A sensor. A timer. Or someone on the outside pressing a button.” He looked around the room again. “Maybe there’s a way to signal. To make noise.”

They tried that. They pounded on the door, shouted, kicked at the walls. The only sound that came back was a faint, mocking echo. The cell was clearly soundproofed, or at least heavily insulated. Their efforts were futile.

“Wait,” Leo said, his eyes suddenly lighting up. He stood and walked back to the door. He pressed his ear against it again, then ran his hand along the bottom edge, where the door met the floor. “There’s a small gap here. Barely a hair’s breadth, but it’s there.” He knelt, peering closely. “And I can feel a faint draft. Very faint.”

Maya knelt beside him. Indeed, there was a minuscule gap, just enough for a sheet of paper, perhaps, but certainly not enough to see through or manipulate anything.

“What good is a draft?” Maya asked, puzzled.

“It means the air is circulating,” Leo explained. “Which means there has to be a ventilation system. And if there’s a ventilation system, there’s usually a way to affect it. Or to use it.” He stood up, looking at the ceiling. “It’s probably in the ceiling. But we can’t reach it.”

They looked up. The ceiling was concrete, seamless, just like the walls. The emergency light strip was recessed, flush with the surface. There was no visible vent.

“There must be a main vent for the sub-level that this room connects to,” Leo mused, walking back to the cot. “And if they want us to stay in here, they probably wouldn’t put a vent that could be easily accessed.” He sat down again, thoughtful.

Maya’s gaze fell back to the splinter in the cot frame. It was still there, tiny, metallic, a symbol of their hard-won freedom from the ropes. She reached out and touched it. It was still sharp. A thought began to form.

“Leo,” she said slowly. “That splinter. It’s still stuck in the cot. It’s small, but it’s sharp. What if we could use it to… to pick something?”

Leo looked at her, then back at the splinter. “Pick what? There’s no lock to pick. And it’s stuck in the frame. We can’t get it out.”

“Maybe we don’t need to get it out,” Maya countered. “Maybe we can use it to… carve something. Or chip away at something.” She looked at the small, flush metal plate on the door. It was solid, but it wasn’t completely flat. There was a tiny seam where the plate met the main door frame. “If we could somehow get a small tool, a lever, we could try to pry it open. Or at least expose whatever mechanism is behind it.”

Leo stood up again, his eyes following her gaze to the door. “You mean, try to chisel away at the concrete around it?”

“No, not the concrete. The plate itself,” Maya clarified. “It has to be fastened somehow. Screws, bolts, something. Even if it’s just adhesive, there’s a seam. If we could scrape at it, maybe find a purchase, we could work it loose. It’s our only lead.”

It was a desperate idea, born of desperation. The splinter was tiny, barely a millimeter wide. But it was all they had.

“Okay,” Leo said, his voice determined. “Let’s try it. We need something to help us. We need to get the splinter out of the cot, or find something else that’s small and rigid.” He looked around the cell again, his gaze more intense this time. He knelt and began to meticulously search the floor around the cot, feeling with his fingertips. The floor was rough, unyielding concrete, but years of wear and neglect might have left something behind.

Maya joined him, her eyes sweeping over the floor, then to the cot. The small metal splinter gleamed faintly in the dim light. She reached out, trying to wiggle it, but it was firmly embedded. “It’s not budging. It’s like it’s fused with the metal.”

“Then we need to find something else,” Leo said, his voice low. He kept searching, his fingers brushing over every imperfection, every grain of dust. “They took our tools. My knife, your multi-tool. They didn’t leave anything behind. These guys were thorough.”

As he spoke, his fingers brushed against something cold and smooth. It was small, no bigger than his thumbnail. He picked it up. It was a small, flat metal button, dark grey, with a tiny symbol etched into its surface. It was the kind of button you’d find on an old uniform, or perhaps a piece of equipment. It was made of solid, relatively hard metal.

“Look what I found,” Leo said, holding it up for Maya to see. “It’s just a button, but it’s solid. And it has a thin edge.”

Maya took it, turning it over in her fingers. It wasn’t razor-sharp, but it was firm and had a slightly beveled edge. It was certainly better than nothing. “It’s not much, but it’s something. Let’s try it on the door plate.”

They moved back to the door, their hopes cautiously renewed. Leo took the button, and with Maya holding the emergency light to provide a better, though still dim, view, he began to work on the small metal plate that concealed the lock mechanism. He started by trying to insert the edge of the button into the minuscule seam between the plate and the door. It was a painstaking process. The button was slightly too thick for the tightest parts of the seam, but in other places, it managed to wedge itself in.

He scraped, pushed, and twisted, exerting as much pressure as he dared without breaking the fragile button. He worked slowly, meticulously, trying to widen the seam, to find a weakness in the plate’s attachment. After several minutes of frustrating effort, a faint, metallic groan emanated from the plate. A tiny sliver of metal, no bigger than a grain of sand, flaked off the edge of the plate.

“Something’s happening!” Maya whispered, her eyes wide. “Keep going!”

Leo continued, his concentration absolute. The edge of the button was beginning to dull, but it was still functional. He worked his way around the plate, trying to identify how it was secured. There were no visible screws, so it was likely either glued or held by hidden clips. If it was glued, he might be able to slowly pry it off. If it was clips, he would need to find the right leverage point.

After a long, tense period of scraping and prying, a small section of the plate, near the bottom, began to lift ever so slightly. It was barely perceptible, but it was enough. Leo gritted his teeth, applying more pressure. A faint popping sound, like a tiny adhesive seal breaking, echoed in the small room.

“I think it’s glued,” Leo said, his voice tight with effort. “Or held by very small clips.” He continued to work, carefully, methodically. He used the button to create a small gap, then widened it. His fingers, despite their soreness, were nimble.

Finally, after another eternity, a corner of the metal plate pried away from the door. It made a louder popping sound this time, and then slowly, agonizingly, peeled back. It was indeed held by a strong adhesive, which was now giving way. Leo continued his work, slowly pulling the plate away from the door, revealing the inner workings of the lock.

It wasn’t a complex magnetic lock as he’d initially feared. Instead, it was a heavy-duty, mechanical bolt system, operated by a small, exposed circuit board and a series of interlocking gears. A thin bundle of wires connected the board to the outside, presumably to a control panel or a keycard reader. There was no keyhole, but there was a small, recessed button on the circuit board itself, marked with a tiny, faded symbol that looked like an open padlock.

“Bingo,” Leo breathed, his eyes fixed on the small button. “A manual override. Or a local control. Whatever it is, it’s a button.”

“Do you think it will open the door?” Maya asked, her heart pounding with a mixture of fear and excitement.

“Only one way to find out,” Leo said, his voice firm. He reached out a finger, hesitated for a fraction of a second, then pressed the small button.

A soft click echoed from within the door. Then, with a low, hydraulic hiss, the heavy door slowly, silently, began to slide open, revealing a dimly lit corridor beyond.

They froze, holding their breath, listening intently. The corridor was empty. The rhythmic hum of the facility was still present, a constant, pervasive drone, but there were no footsteps, no voices, no signs of guards.

“Okay,” Leo whispered, his hand still on the open door. “Be careful. We don’t know what’s out there. Or where ‘out there’ even is.”

Maya nodded, her eyes darting quickly from side to side, trying to take in as much of the corridor as possible. It was a concrete tunnel, just like their cell, but wider, with pipes running along the ceiling and walls. The lighting was even dimmer here, provided by scattered emergency lights, casting long, unsettling shadows. It felt colder too, a damp, metallic chill that seeped into their bones.

“We need to be silent,” Maya instructed. “And fast. Do you remember any of the turns from when they brought us down here?”

Leo shook his head grimly. “It was too chaotic. Flashlights in our eyes, they were pushing us. I was just trying to keep my balance. You tried to count turns, didn’t you?”

“I did,” Maya confirmed, her brow furrowed in concentration. “But it was useless. They went down a flight of stairs, then through another heavy door. I remember that much. But the path through the corridors, before that… it was a maze.”

“Okay, so we’re essentially blind,” Leo summarized, a hint of frustration in his voice. “Great. Let’s stick to the walls. And listen for everything.”

He peeked his head out first, then stepped fully into the corridor, Maya right behind him. The door remained ajar. They couldn’t risk closing it and having it relock automatically. They would have to leave it open, a stark sign of their escape. But there was no time to think about that now.

They moved slowly, cautiously, their bare feet silent on the concrete floor. Every creak of the pipes overhead, every distant clatter, sent a jolt of adrenaline through them. The corridor stretched out in both directions, seemingly identical.

“Which way?” Maya whispered, pointing left, then right.

Leo looked around, his instincts kicking in. “Left feels… less traveled. Right feels like it might lead to a more active area. Let’s try less traveled first. We need to find an exit, not more guards.”

They turned left, pressing themselves against the cold, damp wall. The corridor curved gently, leading them deeper into the labyrinthine sub-level. The air grew heavier, carrying a faint, acrid scent that Maya couldn’t quite place. It was chemical, industrial, and faintly metallic.

They passed several more identical, heavy steel doors, presumably more holding cells, though they didn’t hear any sounds from within. Then they came to a junction. Three paths diverged. One continued straight, another veered sharply left, and the third, a narrow, darker passage, branched off to the right.

“This is where it gets tricky,” Leo whispered. “Any gut feelings?”

Maya closed her eyes for a moment, trying to recall any detail from their forced march. “I remember a flight of stairs down, then a door,” she repeated. “And we were already deep. These corridors are flat. We haven’t gone up or down any stairs since we were pushed into the cell.”

“So we need to find stairs up,” Leo deduced. “Or an elevator. Or a service shaft.” He pointed to the narrow passage on the right. “That one looks like a service tunnel. Might lead to maintenance shafts or vents.”

“Or a dead end,” Maya countered, looking at the straight path. “This way looks like it could be a main artery. Maybe it leads to a stairwell.”

They deliberated for a few agonizing seconds. The constant hum of the facility seemed to grow louder, more oppressive. They were playing a dangerous game of chance.

“Okay,” Leo decided, pointing straight ahead. “Main artery it is. If it’s too active, we backtrack. But we need to make progress.”

They continued down the main corridor. The air grew slightly warmer here, and the acrid smell intensified. They began to hear more distinct sounds now: the distant clatter of machinery, the faint rumble of what sounded like an exhaust fan, and occasionally, muffled voices.

“Voices,” Maya mouthed, her eyes wide with alarm. She pulled Leo back into the shadows of a recessed alcove, a small space where some pipes disappeared into the wall.

They flattened themselves against the cold concrete, barely breathing. The voices grew louder, two distinct male voices, seemingly approaching from the direction they were heading. They were speaking in low tones, but the acoustics of the corridor carried their words surprisingly well.

“…schedule is tight, I tell you,” one voice grumbled. “The new shipment needs to be processed by morning. And that’s before the Director arrives.”

“Don’t remind me,” the second voice sighed. “Always a rush. I swear, one day this whole place is just going to explode from the pressure.”

They were getting closer. Leo and Maya exchanged terrified glances. These sounded like the guards or facility workers. They would be discovered.

“What do we do?” Maya whispered, her heart hammering against her ribs.

Leo looked around wildly. The alcove was too small to hide completely if they were searched. There were no doors nearby. The only option was to run, but in which direction? Back towards their cell, risking being seen in the open, or further down the corridor, towards the approaching voices?

Just then, the voices rounded a bend in the corridor, still a good distance away, but now visible. Two men in dark grey uniforms, carrying flashlights, were walking towards them, seemingly unaware of their presence. They weren’t the gruff or bored voices from before, these were different, younger, perhaps. But they were still guards.

“There!” Maya whispered, pointing to a small, almost imperceptible gap in the wall behind them. It was a narrow, dark crevice, barely wide enough for a person to squeeze through, covered by a hanging length of flexible, dark piping. It was obviously some kind of service access or forgotten opening.

“No time to question it,” Leo breathed. “Go!”

Maya didn’t hesitate. She squeezed herself into the narrow gap, pushing the flexible piping aside. It was tight, rough, and smelled of stale dust and ozone. She felt concrete scraping against her sides, tearing at her clothes. Leo followed quickly, grimacing as he squeezed through, pulling the piping back into place just as the guards’ voices grew louder, now almost directly opposite their hiding place.

They held their breath, pressing themselves against the rough, inner surface of the hidden passage. They could hear the guards’ footsteps now, heavy and deliberate, passing right by their narrow hiding spot. They stopped.

“Did you hear that?” one guard asked.

Leo and Maya froze.

“Hear what?” the other guard replied, his voice closer, now. “Just the usual hum. Your nerves are shot, Jenkins.”

“No, I swear, I heard something,” the first guard insisted. “Like… a scraping. Or a whisper.”

A flashlight beam danced across the flexible piping covering their hiding spot, a mere inch from Leo’s face. He could feel the heat of the bulb, smell the dust burning on its surface.
