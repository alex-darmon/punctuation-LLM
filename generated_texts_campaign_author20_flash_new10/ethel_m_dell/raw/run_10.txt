Elara stared at him, the raw emotion in his words striking her with a sudden, poignant force. The anger began to recede, replaced by a confused understanding. Gareth shook his head slowly. "No, Elara. Not cross." He reached out a hand, hesitating, then gently touched her arm, a gesture both tentative and firm. "You feel things deeply. It is one of your many strengths, though you sometimes think it a weakness. Come, the air grows chill. We should go."

His words struck a chord with Elara. Protecting what remained. It was what she had sought to do for herself when she fled to this remote place. To protect her own fragmented spirit, to find healing in the wild, untamed beauty of the moor. As they neared the first cottage, a faint scent of woodsmoke mingled with the damp earth, a comforting, domestic smell that contrasted sharply with the wildness they had just left behind. Elara hugged her cloak tighter around her. The air was indeed growing sharp.

Just as they were about to step onto the cobbled path leading to their own cottage, Gareth stopped, his head tilted slightly, listening. Bran, too, had come to a standstill, a low, almost imperceptible growl rumbling deep in his chest.

He looked at her, his gaze direct and assessing. For a moment, she thought he would refuse, retreat into his accustomed silence. But then, he sighed, a sound that seemed to carry the weariness of the moor itself. "Perhaps it is, Elara. Perhaps it is."

My father, prepared, had produced a pair of crudely fashioned goggles from his pack. They were made from dark leather with narrow slits, not unlike those worn by Arctic explorers in old books I had seen. He handed them to me with a curt instruction: "Wear these. Snow blindness will finish us faster than the cold." I fumbled with the straps, my fingers numb and clumsy, finally securing them around my head. The world instantly dimmed, the glare softened, and the sharp edges of the landscape became less assaulting. It was a small comfort, a fleeting moment of relief in an otherwise unrelenting ordeal.

As we continued our slow, arduous ascent, the wind, which had merely been a whisper before, began to gather strength. It whistled through the narrow passes and across the exposed ridges, a mournful, hungry sound that seemed to strip away any remaining warmth from my exposed skin. It carried fine particles of snow, stinging my face and collecting in the folds of my clothing. I pulled my scarf higher, burying my nose and mouth in the rough wool, trying to breathe the slightly warmer air trapped within. The wind was no longer a background element; it was an active participant in our struggle, pushing against us, threatening to steal our footing, chilling us to the bone with every gust. It felt like the mountain itself was trying to shrug us off, an irritating inconvenience upon its vast, frozen shoulders.

My father paused, briefly, at a point where the ridge narrowed considerably. On one side, the snow fell away sharply into a deep, shrouded ravine; on the other, a sheer rock face, sparsely dusted with snow, rose like a giant's wall. The wind howled here with renewed ferocity, seeming to funnel through the constriction, making conversation impossible. He turned his head slightly, his gaze sweeping the precarious path ahead. His brow was furrowed, a faint line of concern etching itself across his face. This was a rare sight. Usually, his expression was one of grim determination, unreadable to my young eyes. Now, there was a flicker of something else, a calculating caution that sent a cold knot of apprehension coiling in my stomach, independent of the external chill. He secured Brynn's sling more tightly, readjusting her weight. Then, with an almost imperceptible nod, he started forward again, placing each boot with deliberate, precise care, testing the snow before committing his full weight. I mimicked his movements as best I could, my eyes glued to his feet, trying to replicate the measured cadence of his steps. Each footfall was critical here. A slip, a loss of balance, and the consequences would be catastrophic. The drop into the ravine seemed impossibly far, a dark maw waiting to consume any misstep. My heart hammered against my ribs, not from exertion this time, but from a cold, creeping fear.

The sheep, sensing the increased danger, bunched even closer, their collective body heat and the rustle of their wool providing a slight, ephemeral comfort. Their anxious bleats were drowned out by the wind, but their nervous energy was palpable. They moved with a newfound urgency, their instinct for self preservation overriding their usual skittishness. They trusted my father implicitly, their every movement a testament to his leadership. He was their shepherd, their protector, and they clung to his presence with an almost desperate loyalty. One particularly large ram, its horns curving majestically, pressed against my leg, providing a warm, woolly barrier against the wind on one side. I found myself grateful for its proximity, for the simple, unthinking animal presence that offered a momentary distraction from the stark realities of our situation.

The next hour stretched into an eternity. The ridge continued its relentless ascent, punctuated by short, treacherous descents and narrow, wind swept traverses. My lungs ached with a deep, pervasive burn. My muscles screamed with a dull, persistent pain. My mind, usually prone to youthful wanderings, was focused solely on the next step, on keeping pace with my father, on not falling behind. I stumbled frequently, but each time I caught myself, driven by an almost primal fear of being left behind, of facing the vast, indifferent mountain alone. My father, hearing a particularly loud stumble, finally glanced back, his eyes, narrowed behind the slits of his goggles, held a flicker of something I couldn't quite decipher. Not anger, not impatience, but perhaps a shared understanding of the monumental effort required. He didn't speak, but his gaze was enough. It was an unspoken command to push on, to find reserves I didn't know I possessed.

My thirst became a gnawing torment. The cold air, constantly drawn into my mouth, dried my throat to a parched desert. I longed for a sip of water, a moment of respite, but I knew such luxuries were out of the question. We were moving, and movement was survival. To stop was to invite the cold to claim us. The snow, though abundant, was not a source of immediate relief. Eating it would only lower our core body temperature, hastening the onset of hypothermia. I learned these lessons early, lessons etched into my memory by the harsh realities of mountain life. Survival was a meticulous balance of effort and conservation, of endurance and caution.

After what felt like days, though the sun had barely moved from its mid morning position, the ridge began to broaden slightly. The wind, while still strong, seemed to lose some of its focused intensity, spreading out over a wider expanse. My father slowed his pace, still deliberate, but no longer quite so urgent. He paused, finally, at the base of a towering, snow dusted rock formation that offered a small degree of shelter from the relentless wind. "We rest here," he said, his voice raspy, the words sounding alien after hours of silence. "Five minutes. No more." His eyes, normally piercing, were now shadowed with fatigue, but his resolve remained unbroken.

I collapsed gratefully onto a relatively clear patch of rock, shivering violently despite the exertion. My muscles seized up almost immediately, protesting the abrupt cessation of movement. Brynn, stirring on his back, let out a soft whimper. My father gently lowered her to the ground, positioning her so her back was against the rock, shielding her from the wind. Her small face was pale, almost translucent, her lips a faint blue. He unwrapped a small square of dried meat from a pouch inside his coat and offered it to her. She nibbled at it weakly, her eyes half closed. He then offered me a similar piece. It was tough, salty, and revived a flicker of warmth in my belly. We ate in silence, the only

consumption of the tough, stringy meat a stark reminder of our precarious existence. My father’s gaze swept the horizon, his head slowly turning, assessing the subtle shifts in wind and light, reading the mountain like a familiar, formidable book. His silence was not empty. It was filled with the weight of decisions, the calculations of risk and reward that defined every step in this unforgiving landscape. He never spoke unnecessarily. Every word was a tool, precisely wielded.

Brynn, nestled against the rock, closed her eyes completely, her breathing shallow and uneven. Her small hand, still clutching the dried meat, relaxed slightly. A wave of cold dread washed over me, a more potent chill than the relentless wind. I wanted to reach out, to touch her, to offer some comfort, but my father’s presence, his absolute focus, held me immobile. There was no room for sentimental gestures, only for practical action. My own limbs ached with a deep, throbbing pain. The muscles in my calves felt like tight knots of rope, and my shoulders screamed from the constant strain of my pack. My thirst had not abated, but the piece of meat had at least given my stomach something to work on, dulling the sharper pangs of hunger.

“Listen,” my father said, his voice low, almost a whisper against the howl of the wind. He wasn’t speaking to me directly, but to the air, to the mountain itself. His eyes were fixed on a distant point, a jagged peak shrouded in swirling snow. “The wind is turning. It will be harder soon.”

My heart sank. Harder? It already felt like we were pushing the limits of human endurance. The brief respite had only served to highlight the profound fatigue that had settled deep within my bones. I looked at Brynn again, her fragile form a stark contrast to the raw power of the mountain around us. How much more could she endure? How much more could I?

Five minutes stretched into what felt like an eternity and then, abruptly, was over. My father stirred, his movements economical despite the obvious strain. He gathered Brynn gently, positioning her on his back once more, securing her with practiced hands. She offered no resistance, no complaint, merely slumped against him, a silent, precious burden.

“Time to move,” he stated, his voice firm, leaving no room for argument or delay. He didn’t wait for my acknowledgment, simply pushed himself up, his eyes already scanning the next section of the path. I struggled to my feet, my muscles screaming in protest at the sudden demand. Each joint felt stiff, each movement a conscious effort. The cold seemed to penetrate deeper now that my body had cooled slightly. I shivered violently, trying to re ignite the internal furnace that movement provided.

We began again, the broad expanse of the ridge offering little in the way of visual cues, only a relentless, upward slope into the swirling white. The wind, true to my father’s prediction, had indeed shifted. It no longer came directly from the west, but seemed to swirl and eddy, buffeting us from all sides, carrying fine particles of snow that stung my exposed skin like a thousand tiny needles. My breath plumed out in thick white clouds, instantly whisked away by the gale. The rhythmic crunch of our boots on the compacted snow was the only sound, a monotonous beat against the crescendo of the wind.

My father, though still moving with his characteristic determination, seemed to hunch a little more, his head bowed deeper into his shoulders. The air was growing thinner, each gasp for breath becoming more labored, more desperate. My lungs burned with a constant, fiery ache, a sensation that had become a familiar companion on this journey. I focused on his back, on the steady swing of his ice axe, on the rhythm of his steps. One foot in front of the other. That was all that mattered.

The terrain changed subtly. The snow became deeper in places, forcing us to high step, expending precious energy. In other sections, the underlying rock was exposed, offering a firmer footing but requiring careful placement of our boots to avoid slipping on the icy surfaces. My father navigated these treacherous sections with an intuitive grace, his body seemingly molded to the mountain’s whims. I tried to emulate his movements, placing my feet precisely where he had, using my own ice axe for balance, but I lacked his innate connection to the unforgiving ground.

The visibility, already poor, deteriorated further. The swirling snow intensified, blurring the line between earth and sky, creating a disorienting whiteout. The vastness of the mountain, once a distant threat, now pressed in on us, an opaque, suffocating presence. I lost all sense of direction, relying entirely on my father’s unwavering compass. He seemed to know, instinctively, which way was up, which way was safe.

Time became meaningless. It was merely a continuous present, an endless cycle of movement, pain, and cold. My mind, stripped of all extraneous thought, was reduced to a primal awareness of my body, of the immediate surroundings, of the necessity of keeping pace. I tasted blood in my mouth, the result of chapped lips and dry, cracked skin. My fingers, even inside my thick gloves, felt numb, a dull ache radiating from the tips. I tried to flex them, to bring some warmth back, but it was a futile gesture.

Then, a new sound cut through the roar of the wind. A low, continuous rumble, barely perceptible at first, then growing louder, more distinct. My father stopped dead, his body tensing. He turned his head slightly, peering into the swirling white. His silence was more unsettling than any shout. The rumble intensified, a deep, guttural growl that vibrated through the very ground beneath our feet. My breath caught in my throat. I knew that sound. It was the mountain speaking, and its voice was terrifying.

An avalanche.

My father’s head snapped towards me, his eyes wide with a sudden, urgent alarm. “Run!” he shouted, his voice a raw, desperate rasp against the wind. “To the left! Get to the rocks!”

My legs, leaden moments before, found a sudden, desperate burst of energy. I stumbled, half falling, half scrambling towards a cluster of jagged rocks that appeared vaguely through the driving snow. The sound behind us was no longer a rumble, but a deafening roar, a hungry beast devouring everything in its path. The ground shook violently. I risked a glance over my shoulder. Through the swirling white, I saw it. A towering wall of snow and ice, surging down the slope, a silent, deadly wave of destruction. It was moving impossibly fast.

“Faster!” my father screamed, his voice strained with effort. He was just behind me, Brynn a silent bundle on his back. He pushed me forward with a hand on my pack, propelling me towards the sparse shelter. I reached the rocks, diving behind the largest one, pressing myself against its rough, icy surface. The roar was deafening now, an all consuming sound that vibrated through my entire being. I squeezed my eyes shut, bracing for impact.

A split second later, my father slammed into the rock beside me, sheltering Brynn against his chest. He threw an arm around me, pulling me closer, his body a shield. The world exploded in a white fury. Snow and ice, carried by an unimaginable force, crashed over us, burying us in a cold, suffocating embrace. I felt the impact, a tremendous pressure, and then everything was darkness and cold. I couldn’t breathe. My chest tightened, my lungs screaming for air. Panic, cold and sharp, threatened to overwhelm me.

Then, just as quickly as it had come, the main surge of the avalanche passed. The roaring subsided, replaced by a dull, muffled hiss, the sound of countless tons of snow settling. I was buried, completely encased in snow. The pressure on my body was immense, restricting my every movement. I tried to move my arms, to clear the snow from my face, but I couldn’t. My father was still there, pressed against me, his arm still tight around my shoulders. I could feel his warmth, a faint but reassuring presence in the oppressive darkness.

“Breathe,” his voice was muffled, strained, but clear enough to penetrate the thick blanket of snow. “Small breaths. Don’t panic.”

His words, simple and direct, were a lifeline. I focused on them, forcing myself to take shallow, measured breaths. The air was thin, icy, but it was there. My father began to move, slowly, deliberately. I felt a slight shift in the snow around me, a subtle pressure release. He was digging. He was always digging. His immense strength, even when exhausted, was a force to behold.

It felt like an eternity, trapped beneath the snow, the cold seeping into my bones, the darkness absolute. My mind, usually prone to wild imaginings, was eerily calm, focused solely on the sounds of my father’s struggle, on the faint scraping and pushing, on the almost imperceptible movement of the snow.

Then, a sliver of grey light appeared above me. It widened slowly, painfully. I gasped, sucking in a lungful of frigid air, coughing violently as my lungs reacted to the sudden influx. My father’s hand, gloved and powerful, appeared above the snow, grabbing my collar and pulling me upwards. I emerged, gasping, into a landscape utterly transformed.

The avalanche had scoured the slope clean, leaving behind a chaotic mess of snow, ice, and debris. Where there had been a continuous, unbroken incline, there was now a jagged, uneven surface, a testament to the mountain’s brutal power. The air was still thick with fine snow particles, but the immediate danger had passed.

My father was already mostly free, having created a small pocket for Brynn and himself. He had managed to keep her face clear of the snow, a feat that seemed impossible given the ferocity of the avalanche. She lay limp against his chest, her eyes closed, her face even paler than before.

He looked at me, his face streaked with snow, his eyes hollow with fatigue but blazing with an intense resolve. “Are you hurt?” he asked, his voice still hoarse.

I shook my head, still gasping for air. “No. Just cold.”

He nodded, a brief, sharp movement. “Good. We need to move. This slope is unstable now.”

The thought of moving again, of exerting myself after such a terrifying ordeal, was almost unbearable. My legs felt like jelly, my body weak and trembling from the shock. But his words were not a request. They were a command. And I knew, deep down, that he was right. The mountain, having shown its teeth, could easily snap them again.

We began to dig ourselves out completely, using our gloved hands and the blunt ends of our ice axes. The snow was dense, compacted by the force of the avalanche, and every scoop was an effort. My father worked tirelessly, never pausing, his movements efficient and powerful. I tried to match his pace, pushing through the residual fear and exhaustion.

Once we were both free, our packs still strapped to our backs, though partially buried, my father carefully checked Brynn. He unwrapped her face from his coat, brushing away lingering snow. Her lips were blue, her skin almost translucent. She shivered violently, a tiny, fragile tremor that tore at my heart.

“She’s colder now,” he said, his voice flat, devoid of emotion, yet I heard the worry underlying it. “We need warmth. And shelter. Soon.”

He scanned the shattered landscape. The path we had been following was obliterated, buried under tons of snow. The world had been reset, and our way forward was now a blank canvas of unknown dangers.

“We go down,” he finally declared, pointing towards a steep, treacherous descent that led into a narrow, shadowed gully. “There might be an overhang. Some shelter.”

My stomach churned at the sight. Going down in deep snow, especially after an avalanche, was often more dangerous than going up. The risk of sinking, of hidden crevasses, of further slides, was immense. But the alternative, staying exposed on the open slope, was clearly worse.

My father started descending, carefully testing each step with his ice axe, kicking steps into the steep incline. I followed, my heart pounding in my chest. Each downward step was jarring, a controlled fall. The snow was soft in places, threatening to give way, and hard in others, making a solid footing difficult. My fear of heights, usually suppressed by the immediate urgency of the climb, resurfaced with a vengeance as I looked down into the shadowy depths of the gully.

We moved slowly, painstakingly, the descent more arduous than any ascent. The wind, trapped within the gully, seemed to intensify, howling around us with renewed fury. The light began to fade, the grey sky deepening to a bruised purple. Dusk was falling, and with it, the temperature would plummet further. The thought of spending a night exposed in this unforgiving place was a terrifying prospect.

Brynn, on my father’s back, began to whimper, a faint, weak sound that was almost lost in the wind. My father adjusted her, trying to provide more warmth, but his face was grim. Her condition was deteriorating rapidly. We needed shelter. We needed warmth.

The gully widened slightly as we descended, the walls becoming steeper, more imposing. The sound of the wind, echoing off the rock faces, was deafening. My legs burned, my knees aching from the constant strain of bracing against the downward slope. My entire body felt like a lead weight, dragged down by gravity and exhaustion.

Then, my father stopped. He pointed with his ice axe towards a dark patch in the rock face, barely visible in the deepening gloom. It was a shallow cave, little more than an overhang, but it offered a promise of respite from the direct assault of the wind and snow.

“There,” he rasped, his voice barely audible. “We make camp.”

Relief, sharp and sudden, washed over me, almost causing my knees to buckle. We had made it. For now. This was not the end of the journey, but it was a temporary reprieve, a chance to gather strength, to survive another few hours.

The final few yards to the cave entrance were the hardest. My body felt utterly spent, my mind screaming for surrender. But the thought of Brynn, of her fragile life depending on us, spurred me on. I pushed through the pain, through the overwhelming fatigue, dragging one leaden foot after the other.

We reached the small opening, a dark maw in the rock face. My father went in first, carefully, checking for any hidden dangers. I followed, shedding my pack with a groan of relief as I stepped onto the relatively flat, snow dusted floor of the cave. The air inside was still cold, but it was miraculously calm, shielded from the relentless wind. It was a sanctuary, however temporary.

My father gently lowered Brynn from his back, laying her on a relatively dry patch of rock. She was barely conscious, her breath a shallow, stuttering whisper. Her lips were almost black, her skin icy to the touch. He immediately began to rummage in his pack, pulling out a small, waterproof bag. From it, he extracted a foil survival blanket, crinkly and metallic. He carefully unfolded it and wrapped Brynn in it, tucking the edges tightly around her small form.

“We need heat,” he said, looking at me, his eyes now shadowed with a deep, consuming worry. The stoicism, the unyielding resolve that had defined him throughout the day, seemed to waver, just for a moment, revealing the raw fear beneath. “We need to make a fire.”

My heart sank again. A fire. In this environment, with the little fuel we carried, it was a daunting task. The small pack of kindling we carried was precious, reserved for emergencies. This, clearly, was an emergency. My hands, still numb despite the relative calm, felt utterly useless.

He pulled out a small, worn pouch from his inner coat pocket. It contained a small bundle of dry tinder he had meticulously collected from a lower elevation, along with a flint and steel. He began to gather what little dry material he could find within the cave, small flakes of rock, a few brittle twigs that had somehow blown in and settled. It was a meager collection, barely enough to sustain a flame.

I watched him, my body aching, my mind still reeling from the day’s traumas. The fear for Brynn, for all of us, was a cold knot in my stomach. The silence in the cave, broken only by the faint whimper of Brynn and the scratch of my father’s flint, was heavy with unspoken dread.

He struck the flint, a shower of sparks briefly illuminating his grim, determined face. The sparks fell onto the tiny pile of tinder, but nothing caught. He tried again, and again, his movements precise, his focus absolute. My own hands felt too clumsy, too cold to be of any help. I could only watch, and pray.

Finally, after what felt like an age, a tiny wisp of smoke curled upwards, followed by a faint, orange glow. A spark had caught. My father carefully nurtured it, blowing gently, coaxing the tiny flame to life. He added the brittle twigs, one by one, carefully building the miniature blaze. The fire, small as it was, cast flickering shadows on the cave walls, chasing away some of the encroaching darkness. It was a fragile beacon of hope in the vast, indifferent wilderness.

The heat, though minimal, was a profound comfort. I instinctively moved closer, holding my numb hands towards the tiny flames. My father, however, remained focused on Brynn. He moved the small fire as close to her as he dared, shielding it from any stray drafts. He then began to rub her cold limbs, trying to stimulate circulation, to bring some warmth back into her fragile body.

He took out another square of dried meat, tearing off a tiny piece and trying to get her to swallow it. She barely responded, her head lolling weakly. He pressed it against her lips, urging her softly, “Eat, little one. You need strength.”

He then rummaged further in his pack, pulling out a small, insulated flask. He poured a tiny amount of liquid into its cap and offered it to Brynn. “Warm broth,” he explained to me, his voice a strained whisper. “Keeps the cold out.”

He carefully spooned a few drops into her mouth. She swallowed weakly, a faint cough escaping her lips. He repeated the process, patiently, persistently. Each swallow, however small, was a victory. Each flicker of a response, a sign that life still clung to her.

I sat there, watching them, feeling utterly helpless. My father, despite his exhaustion, was a whirlwind of focused action, driven by an unwavering love and an unshakeable will to survive. He was a force of nature in himself, as formidable as the mountain, yet capable of such tender care.

He glanced at me then, his eyes, still shadowed with fatigue, held a look of profound, shared understanding. “Get some rest,” he commanded, his voice softer than usual. “I will keep watch. We need you strong for tomorrow.”

I wanted to protest, to offer to take a turn, but the weariness that had settled deep within my bones was overwhelming. My eyelids felt heavy, my thoughts sluggish. I nodded, acknowledging his command, and slumped against the cold rock wall, pulling my pack closer for what little warmth it offered. The flickering firelight danced before my eyes, painting patterns on the darkness. The warmth, the relative safety of the cave, the rhythmic crackle of the fire, all conspired to pull me down into the depths of sleep.

My last conscious thought was of Brynn, her pale face illuminated by the firelight, and of my father, a silent sentinel, watching over us both, his silhouette etched against the cave’s rough walls, an unyielding guardian in the heart of the indifferent mountain. The wind still howled outside, a constant, mournful cry, but within the small sanctuary, for now, we were safe. Tomorrow, the mountain would demand its due again. “Breathe,” his voice was muffled, strained, but clear enough to penetrate the thick blanket of snow. “Small breaths. Don’t panic.”

His words, simple and direct, were a lifeline. I focused on them, forcing myself to take shallow, measured breaths. The air was thin, icy, but it was there. My father began to move, slowly, deliberately. I felt a slight shift in the snow around me, a subtle pressure release. He was digging. He was always digging. His immense strength, even when exhausted, was a force to behold.

It felt like an eternity, trapped beneath the snow, the cold seeping into my bones, the darkness absolute. My mind, usually prone to wild imaginings, was eerily calm, focused solely on the sounds of my father’s struggle, on the faint scraping and pushing, on the almost imperceptible movement of the snow.

Then, a sliver of grey light appeared above me. It widened slowly, painfully. I gasped, sucking in a lungful of frigid air, coughing violently as my lungs reacted to the sudden influx. My father’s hand, gloved and powerful, appeared above the snow, grabbing my collar and pulling me upwards. I emerged, gasping, into a landscape utterly transformed.

The avalanche had scoured the slope clean, leaving behind a chaotic mess of snow, ice, and debris. Where there had been a continuous, unbroken incline, there was now a jagged, uneven surface, a testament to the mountain’s brutal power. The air was still thick with fine snow particles, but the immediate danger had passed.

My father was already mostly free, having created a small pocket for Brynn and himself. He had managed to keep her face clear of the snow, a feat that seemed impossible given the ferocity of the avalanche. She lay limp against his chest, her eyes closed, her face even paler than before.

He looked at me, his face streaked with snow, his eyes hollow with fatigue but blazing with an intense resolve. “Are you hurt?” he asked, his voice still hoarse.

I shook my head, still gasping for air. “No. Just cold.”

He nodded, a brief, sharp movement. “Good. We need to move. This slope is unstable now.”

The thought of moving again, of exerting myself after such a terrifying ordeal, was almost unbearable. My legs felt like jelly, my body weak and trembling from the shock. But his words were not a request. They were a command. And I knew, deep down, that he was right. The mountain, having shown its teeth, could easily snap them again.

We began to dig ourselves out completely, using our gloved hands and the blunt ends of our ice axes. The snow was dense, compacted by the force of the avalanche, and every scoop was an effort. My father worked tirelessly, never pausing, his movements efficient and powerful. I tried to match his pace, pushing through the residual fear and exhaustion.

Once we were both free, our packs still strapped to our backs, though partially buried, my father carefully checked Brynn. He unwrapped her face from his coat, brushing away lingering snow. Her lips were blue, her skin almost translucent. She shivered violently, a tiny, fragile tremor that tore at my heart.

“She’s colder now,” he said, his voice flat, devoid of emotion, yet I heard the worry underlying it. “We need warmth. And shelter. Soon.”

He scanned the shattered landscape. The path we had been following was obliterated, buried under tons of snow. The world had been reset, and our way forward was now a blank canvas of unknown dangers.

“We go down,” he finally declared, pointing towards a steep, treacherous descent that led into a narrow, shadowed gully. “There might be an overhang. Some shelter.”

My stomach churned at the sight. Going down in deep snow, especially after an avalanche, was often more dangerous than going up. The risk of sinking, of hidden crevasses, of further slides, was immense. But the alternative, staying exposed on the open slope, was clearly worse.

My father started descending, carefully testing each step with his ice axe, kicking steps into the steep incline. I followed, my heart pounding in my chest. Each downward step was jarring, a controlled fall. The snow was soft in places, threatening to give way, and hard in others, making a solid footing difficult. My fear of heights, usually suppressed by the immediate urgency of the climb, resurfaced with a vengeance as I looked down into the shadowy depths of the gully.

We moved slowly, painstakingly, the descent more arduous than any ascent. The wind, trapped within the gully, seemed to intensify, howling around us with renewed fury. The light began to fade, the grey sky deepening to a bruised purple. Dusk was falling, and with it, the temperature would plummet further. The thought of spending a night exposed in this unforgiving place was a terrifying prospect.

Brynn, on my father’s back, began to whimper, a faint, weak sound that was almost lost in the wind. My father adjusted her, trying to provide more warmth, but his face was grim. Her condition was deteriorating rapidly. We needed shelter. We needed warmth.

The gully widened slightly as we descended, the walls becoming steeper, more imposing. The sound of the wind, echoing off the rock faces, was deafening. My legs burned, my knees aching from the constant strain of bracing against the downward slope. My entire body felt like a lead weight, dragged down by gravity and exhaustion.

Then, my father stopped. He pointed with his ice axe towards a dark patch in the rock face, barely visible in the deepening gloom. It was a shallow cave, little more than an overhang, but it offered a promise of respite from the direct assault of the wind and snow.

“There,” he rasped, his voice barely audible. “We make camp.”
