A wave of exhaustion, mingled with a profound sense of achievement, washed over Elara. They had made it. They had survived the storm, navigated the devastation, and reached their next waypoint. The journey was far from over, but this was a victory, a hard won triumph against the odds.

As they drew closer, the signs of human habitation became more apparent. The trees thinned out, giving way to more open ground. The faint outline of a path, albeit a heavily overgrown one, reappeared. The roar of the river softened into a steady murmur as it spread out over the wide, shallow crossing.

They walked onto the ford itself, the water barely reaching their ankles. The stones underfoot were smooth and worn, testimony to countless crossings over generations. On the other side, a clear, if muddy, track led away into a sparse stand of oak trees. It was undeniably a road, even if it was little more than a cart track.

Gareth stopped in the middle of the ford, turning to look back at the treacherous forest they had just emerged from. His face was grim, his eyes still watchful. “We are on the road now,” he said, his voice flat. “But the dangers are not over. They never are.”

Elara looked back too, at the dark, dense, silent wall of trees. The forest, still dripping, still bearing the scars of the storm, seemed to watch them, a vast, indifferent entity. She shivered, but this time it was not from cold. It was from the lingering echo of fear, and the unsettling realization that they had truly been at the mercy of something far greater than themselves.

But as she turned to face the road ahead, a new resolve settled within her. She was no longer the naive, frightened woman who had entered the forest. She had endured. She had survived. And with Gareth by her side, she felt a flicker of courage she hadn’t known she possessed. The road ahead was uncertain, but for the first time since their journey began, she felt a fragile sense of hope. They had faced the wrath of the wild, and they had emerged, battered but unbroken. The path to their destination, whatever secrets it held, now seemed a little less daunting. They took their first steps onto the ancient track, leaving the tumultuous river and the scarred forest behind them, venturing into the next leg of their perilous journey. The sun, now bright and warm, cast long shadows before them, guiding them forward into the unknown.

The cart track was little more than two parallel ruts worn deep into the earth, occasionally bridged by hardy tufts of grass or patches of moss. It wound its way through the sparse stand of oak trees, their leaves still bearing the recent memory of the storm’s fury in torn edges and scattered debris. The ground was firmer here, a welcome change from the sodden, uneven terrain of the forest. Each step felt less like a struggle for purchase and more like a deliberate, if weary, advance.

Elara found her gaze drawn to the tracks themselves, a silent testament to others who had passed this way, perhaps generations ago. Who were they? What had they carried? Where had they gone? The questions floated through her mind, a quiet counterpoint to the rhythmic crunch of their boots on the gravelly path. Her shoulders still ached, and her legs felt heavy, but the nagging fear that had been a constant companion in the depths of the wild had receded, replaced by a dull throb of physical exhaustion and a cautious optimism.

Gareth walked slightly ahead, his back a steady, reassuring presence. He moved with the same economical grace she had observed in the forest, his senses still attuned to the environment, even on this seemingly safer path. He glanced from side to side, occasionally stopping to examine a bent twig or a faint impression in the mud. He was not just walking; he was reading the road, interpreting its subtle cues. Elara watched him, absorbing his meticulous observation, trying to emulate his silent vigilance.

The sun climbed higher, warming the air, drying their still damp clothes. The world seemed to breathe again after the storm’s suffocating grip. Birds, emboldened by the return of fair weather, sang hesitant melodies from the branches, their calls a stark contrast to the oppressive silence of the storm ravaged woods. A gentle breeze rustled the leaves, carrying the faint scent of damp earth and distant pine. It was a day of fragile peace, a reprieve from the terror.

After what felt like another endless hour, the oaks gave way to a more open landscape. Rolling fields, long untended, stretched out on either side of the track. Here and there, skeletal remains of old fence posts stood like forgotten sentinels, testament to a time when this land had been cultivated. Now, wildflowers bloomed in riotous profusion, their vibrant colors a defiant splash against the muted greens and browns of the overgrown fields. The track itself widened slightly, becoming a more discernible path, though still rough and uneven.

“This way,” Gareth murmured, pointing towards a faint rise in the distance. “That’s the high ground. We can see further from there.”

Elara nodded, her throat too dry for words. The prospect of gaining a vantage point was appealing, even if it meant another uphill climb. She focused on putting one foot in front of the other, her gaze fixed on Gareth’s retreating form. Her mind, no longer consumed by immediate danger, began to drift, replaying snippets of their journey. The terrifying flash flood, the biting cold, the dark, suffocating fear. She had been so helpless, so dependent. Yet, she had pushed through. She had learned. The memory of Gareth’s steady hand, his calm voice, his unwavering resolve, brought a strange warmth to her chest. He had seen her at her weakest, and he had not faltered.

As they ascended the gentle slope, the ground became rockier, the vegetation sparser. The wind, unhindered by trees, picked up, whipping at their hair and clothes. When they reached the summit, a wide panorama unfolded before them. To the west, the dark line of the forest they had just left stretched to the horizon, a formidable, brooding presence. To the east, the landscape opened up further, a patchwork of fallow fields and distant copses of trees, all beneath a vast, indifferent sky.

In the far distance, barely visible through the shimmering heat haze, Elara thought she could discern a faint column of smoke rising. It was thin, almost transparent, easily mistaken for a cloud. She squinted, trying to confirm her observation.

Gareth, however, had already seen it. His eyes, keen and experienced, had fixed on the distant wisp the moment they crested the rise. His expression tightened. “Smoke,” he stated, his voice devoid of emotion. “Could be anything. A farmhouse, a traveler’s fire, or something worse.” He did not elaborate on what ‘worse’ might entail, but Elara understood the unspoken implications. In these uncertain times, any sign of human activity could be a beacon of hope or a harbinger of danger.

They stood for a long moment, scanning the horizon, absorbing the details of the landscape. The silence here was different from the forest’s. It was an expansive silence, broken only by the whisper of the wind and the occasional distant bird call. There was a sense of scale, of distance, that the claustrophobic confines of the woods had not allowed.

“We follow the track,” Gareth eventually decided, pointing eastward. “It seems to lead in that direction. We will keep to the edges of the fields, away from the open ground where possible.” He began to descend the slope, his pace slightly quickened, as if the sight of the distant smoke had injected a new urgency into their progress. Elara followed, her hope now tempered by the renewed sense of caution Gareth exuded.

The track, though more defined, remained rough. They passed the ruins of what might have been a small stone cottage, its roof long collapsed, its walls crumbling. Wild vines climbed over the stones, reclaiming the structure for nature. It was a poignant sight, a silent reminder of lives once lived, now forgotten. Elara wondered about the people who had called this place home. What had become of them? Had they fled? Had they perished? The desolation was palpable, a stark contrast to the resilient beauty of the wild.

The sun began its slow descent, casting long, golden shadows across the fields. The air grew cooler, and a distinct chill began to creep into their bones. The persistent ache in Elara’s limbs intensified, a deep-seated weariness that no amount of resolve could fully banish. Her stomach rumbled, a sharp reminder of their meager rations. They had conserved their food well in the forest, but the physical exertion of the last two days had taken its toll.

“We need to find shelter before nightfall,” Elara said, her voice a little hoarse from disuse. “And water.”

Gareth grunted in agreement. “There should be a stream further on, judging by the contours of the land. We will aim for that.” He peered ahead, his eyes constantly searching. “And a place to make a fire, if possible, without drawing unwanted attention.”

The mention of fire sparked a flicker of longing in Elara. The thought of warmth, of a hot meal, seemed like an impossible luxury. They had spent the last night huddled against the elements, soaked and shivering. A fire, however small, would be a comfort beyond measure. But Gareth’s words, ‘unwanted attention,’ resonated with the warning he had given earlier. The dangers on the road were different from the dangers of the wild, perhaps more insidious.

As the light began to fade, the colors of the landscape deepened. The greens became richer, the blues of the sky more profound, streaked with vibrant oranges and purples from the setting sun. It was a beautiful, melancholic sight. The beauty, however, did little to soothe Elara’s growing apprehension. The open fields, which had seemed a relief from the oppressive forest, now felt exposed, vulnerable.

Just as twilight began to blur the edges of the distant horizon, Gareth stopped abruptly. He pointed to a small cluster of trees off to their left, slightly downhill from the track. “There. And a dip in the land. Looks like the stream bed.”

Relief washed over Elara, loosening the tension in her shoulders. Her legs, which had threatened to give out, found new reserves of strength. They moved towards the trees, their steps quickening with the promise of rest and water.

The small copse was indeed situated around a shallow depression, and within it, a narrow, clear stream trickled over smooth stones. The water looked inviting, clean and cold. Gareth immediately knelt, cupping his hands and drinking deeply, then refilling his water skin. Elara did the same, letting the cool liquid soothe her parched throat. It was the purest water she had tasted in days, a small luxury in their harsh existence.

After satisfying their thirst, Gareth began to scout the area. He moved quietly through the undergrowth, his eyes scanning for suitable shelter. Elara watched him, leaning against the sturdy trunk of an old oak, her body craving rest. Her mind, however, remained active, observing and learning. She noticed how he avoided breaking twigs, how he tested the ground with his foot before putting his full weight on it. He moved like a shadow, a creature of the wild himself.

He soon returned, gesturing towards a thicket of gnarled bushes and fallen branches deeper within the copse. “Not much,” he admitted, his voice low, “but it will provide some cover. We can clear a small space.”

They worked together, Gareth using his knife to cut away some of the denser undergrowth, Elara gathering fallen leaves and softer branches to create a makeshift bed. The effort was taxing, but the thought of a relatively dry and concealed spot spurred them on. They cleared a space barely large enough for them to lie down side by side, nestled deep within the natural cover.

“Fire?” Elara whispered, the word a question, a plea.

Gareth hesitated, looking up at the darkening sky. A few stars were beginning to prick through the deepening blue. “Small one,” he decided. “And we will keep it well hidden. No smoke tonight, just embers for warmth.”

He rummaged in his pack, producing a small flint and steel. He gathered a handful of dry tinder, meticulously arranging it. Elara watched, fascinated, as he struck the flint, sending sparks flying. It took several attempts, but finally, a wisp of smoke curled upwards, followed by a tiny, flickering flame. Gareth carefully nurtured it, adding small, dry twigs, gradually coaxing it into a small, contained fire. It glowed with a comforting warmth, chasing away the chill and casting dancing shadows on the surrounding foliage.

They ate their evening meal in silence, a few dried berries and a sliver of preserved meat. It was meager, but the warmth of the fire and the knowledge that they were relatively safe, for now, made it seem more palatable. The crackling of the fire and the distant murmur of the stream were the only sounds, a welcome change from the storm’s roar and the oppressive silence of the forest.

Elara felt the day’s journey settling into her bones. Her muscles screamed in protest, and her eyelids felt heavy. She leaned back against the rough bark of the oak, letting the fire’s warmth permeate her weary body. She looked at Gareth, illuminated by the flickering light. His face was etched with fatigue, but his eyes remained alert, constantly sweeping their surroundings. He seemed less a man and more a force of nature himself, molded by the wild and its relentless demands.

“Thank you,” she said, her voice soft, almost lost in the crackling of the fire. “For everything. For getting us through.”

Gareth turned his gaze to her, his expression unreadable in the dim light. “We are not through yet, Elara,” he replied, his voice low and steady. “But we are here. And we continue.” He paused, then added, “You did well today. You did not complain, and you kept moving.”

The small compliment, rare and unexpected, warmed Elara more than the fire. She felt a surge of pride, a quiet satisfaction. She truly had changed. The city dwelling woman, accustomed to comforts and security, had been stripped bare, forced to confront her own vulnerabilities, and had found a resilience she never knew she possessed. She thought of her former life, the carefully curated existence, the trivial concerns that had once occupied her mind. They seemed impossibly distant now, almost alien. This new reality, though brutal, felt more immediate, more real.

Sleep began to pull at her, a heavy, irresistible current. Gareth, ever watchful, stood up and carefully banked the fire, ensuring it would not flare up or send too much smoke into the night air. He then took his position, sitting with his back against a tree, his sword across his lap, his eyes scanning the darkness beyond their small refuge. He would stand watch for the first half of the night, as he always did.

Elara lay down on the makeshift bed of leaves, curling into a tight ball, seeking warmth. The ground was hard, but it was dry, and the cover of the bushes felt like a small sanctuary. She closed her eyes, but sleep did not come easily. The sounds of the night pressed in on her. The chirping of crickets, the rustle of leaves, the distant hoot of an owl. Each sound, innocuous in itself, held a hint of uncertainty, a potential threat. Her senses, sharpened by the forest, remained on high alert.

She thought about the distant smoke they had seen. Who were they? Friend or foe? Or just another lonely traveler, like themselves, seeking refuge from a world that had become increasingly unforgiving? The road ahead, Gareth had said, held its own dangers. She understood now that those dangers were often more complex, more insidious, than the raw, elemental fury of the storm. They were the dangers of other people, of desperation, of the unknown motives that drove humanity in fractured times.

Despite the lingering unease, a fragile hope persisted. They were moving forward. They were surviving. And with Gareth, she felt a strength, a quiet determination, that she could not have mustered alone. He was her guide, her protector, and, in a strange, unspoken way, her anchor in this turbulent new world.

Hours later, deep in the night, Elara was jolted awake by a faint, unfamiliar sound. It was a rhythmic crunching, subtle but distinct, coming from the direction of the track. Her heart hammered against her ribs. She held her breath, straining her ears.

Gareth, who had been sitting motionless, was already tense. His hand rested on the hilt of his sword. He turned his head slowly, his eyes narrowed, listening. He did not move, did not even breathe audibly.

The sound grew slightly louder, confirming her fear. It was the sound of footsteps, several of them, approaching their position on the track. They were not heavy, thudding footsteps, but lighter, more deliberate, as if someone was trying to be quiet.

Elara’s breath hitched in her throat. Fear, raw and primal, coiled in her stomach. She tried to make herself as small as possible, pressing deeper into the leaves, praying they were unseen, unheard.

Gareth slowly, meticulously, drew his sword, the soft scrape of metal against leather barely audible. He held it ready, his body coiled, alert for any movement. He whispered, his voice a mere breath against the night air, “Stay still. Do not make a sound.”

The footsteps paused, directly opposite their hidden copse. Elara squeezed her eyes shut, imagining their forms, their faces, their intentions. Were they bandits? Lost travelers? Soldiers? The possibilities swirled in her mind, each one more terrifying than the last. She could feel Gareth’s presence beside her, a rock in the churning sea of her terror. His stillness was absolute, his focus unwavering.

A soft cough broke the silence, followed by a hushed murmur of voices. They were indistinct, too low to make out words, but the cadence was clear. There were at least two, perhaps three. They were talking.

Then, to Elara’s immense relief, the footsteps resumed, moving past their hiding spot, slowly receding down the track. They faded into the distance, growing fainter and fainter until they were gone, swallowed by the vast silence of the night.

Gareth remained motionless for a long time, his sword still drawn, listening intently. Only after several minutes of profound silence did he finally relax, letting out a slow, controlled breath. He sheathed his sword with the slightest click.

“Travelers,” he whispered, his voice still taut with lingering caution. “Moving fast. Didn’t stop.”

Elara let out a shaky breath she hadn’t realized she was holding. Her entire body trembled with the adrenaline surge. “Who were they?” she managed to ask, her voice a thin, reedy sound.

“Impossible to say,” Gareth replied, shaking his head. “But they kept to the track. And they didn’t seem to be searching.” He paused, then added, “Could be refugees. Or scouts. Or something else entirely.” The ambiguity was unsettling.

The close call reinforced Gareth’s earlier warning. The road was not a sanctuary. It was merely a different battleground, with different rules and different adversaries. The forest had been a physical threat; the road presented a human one.

Sleep, for Elara, was now an impossibility. Her mind raced, replaying the near encounter. She remained awake, listening, every rustle of leaves, every distant sound, magnified by her heightened senses. Gareth, despite the disruption, seemed to have resumed his silent vigil, his gaze fixed on the darkness.

As the first faint streaks of dawn began to appear on the eastern horizon, painting the sky in soft hues of grey and rose, Elara felt a renewed sense of weariness, deeper and more profound than before. The night had been restless, punctuated by fear. Yet, the sunlight brought with it a different feeling. A feeling of having survived another night, another peril.

Gareth finally stirred, stretching his stiff limbs with a quiet groan. He looked at Elara, his eyes showing the strain of a sleepless watch. “Dawn,” he stated simply. “Time to move.”

They extinguished the small embers of their fire, carefully covering them with earth and leaves to leave no trace. They packed their meager belongings, the simple act now a well practiced routine. Their muscles ached, their bodies were stiff, but the sun, now a brilliant orb in the sky, promised a new day.

As they emerged from the copse and stepped back onto the cart track, Elara cast one last look at their temporary refuge. It was a small, hidden space, easily missed, yet it had provided them with safety through a perilous night. The thought of it gave her a strange comfort.

The track ahead wound its way through a series of low hills, offering glimpses of more open country beyond. The distant smoke they had seen yesterday was no longer visible, either dissipated or hidden by the undulating landscape. The air was crisp and cool, carrying the scent of damp earth and blooming wildflowers.

Gareth set a steady pace, his strides long and measured. Elara matched him as best she could, her body slowly adjusting to the rhythm of the journey. The early morning light cast long, shifting shadows before them, guiding them forward into the unknown. Each step was an act of faith, a commitment to survival, a defiance against the odds.

The road was long, and their destination remained a distant, uncertain hope. But they were moving. They were enduring. And in the quiet resolve that had settled deep within Elara, she knew that they would continue, one weary step after another, until they reached whatever lay at the end of this long, arduous path. The fear was still there, a constant companion, but it was no longer paralyzing. It was a part of the journey, just like the hunger, the cold, the exhaustion. And alongside it, a fragile, persistent hope, burning bright in the face of an uncertain dawn. They were on the road, and they would see where it led. Their story, battered and bruised, was far from over.

Elara, still raw from the night’s near miss, focused on matching his rhythm, one footfall after another. The crunch of dried leaves and scattered pebbles beneath their worn boots was the loudest sound, a steady, hypnotic counterpoint to the rush of blood in her ears. The air was cool against her skin, carrying the fresh scent of pine needles and damp earth, a stark contrast to the cloying fear that had lingered through the dark hours.

As the sun climbed higher, casting longer, sharper shadows, the low hills they had navigated began to give way to broader, more open fields. Remnants of old cultivation were visible in the gentle undulations of the land, faint furrows suggesting a time when this ground was tended, not merely traversed by desperate souls. Now, wild grasses grew tall, swaying like a silent, green sea in the morning breeze. Patches of wildflowers a brilliant splash of yellow and purple dotted the landscape, their vibrant hues a stark, almost cruel, reminder of life’s enduring beauty amidst their struggle.

Elara’s gaze swept the horizon, a constant, involuntary search for movement, for danger. The world felt vast and indifferent, yet every rustle in the undergrowth, every distant bird call, held a potential significance. Her senses, honed by constant vigilance, filtered the myriad sounds of the wilderness into a finely tuned assessment of threat. The fear was still a cold knot in her stomach, but it was no longer paralyzing. It was a fuel, a sharpening agent, keeping her alert, keeping her alive. Thirst became a persistent companion, a dry rasp at the back of Elara’s throat. Her muscles, stiff from the night’s interrupted rest, protested with a dull ache, but she pushed through it, refusing to acknowledge the discomfort. Survival was a grim marathon, not a sprint. The fire was an essential comfort, a source of warmth and a deterrent to some of the night's creatures, but it also represented a risk. Elara lay down, wrapped in her thin blanket, but sleep was a distant prospect. She found herself imagining the lives of the people who had once lived in the abandoned farmstead, their routines, their simple joys, their eventual terror and flight. And Mara, despite her gentle voice, had a hardness in her eyes that spoke of profound loss and deep resilience.

They were survivors, just like Elara and Gareth. Their faces were etched with the same weariness, their eyes held the same cautious suspicion. Perhaps, Elara thought, they were not so different after all. Perhaps, for tonight, they could find common ground in the shared struggle for warmth and a moment of peace. The fire crackled, casting long, shifting shadows that danced around them, momentarily pushing back the oppressive darkness of the world beyond the clearing. For a little while, at least, they were safe. The night was still long, and danger was never far, but for this brief moment, the smoke had delivered them not to a trap, but to a fragile, temporary sanctuary.

The silence, which had momentarily offered a fragile truce, deepened as the fire settled into a calmer, more consistent crackle. Its light flickered, painting the faces gathered around it in hues of orange and deep shadow. Elara watched the others, her mind still turning over Mara's words about the fishing village. South along the great river. A distant hope, but a hope nonetheless. The scent of woodsmoke mingled with the damp earth and the subtle, indefinable smell of the deep forest at night, a mix of pine needles, decaying leaves, and something wild and untamed.

Gareth, ever watchful, broke the quiet again. "This river you speak of," he began, his voice low, "how far south is it? And what kind of river? Is it navigable, or too treacherous?"

Kael grunted, shifting his weight. "It's the Old Serpent. Runs from the highlands all the way to the coast, or what's left of it. Used to be a major trade route, before. Wide in some parts, narrows to rapids in others. Dangerous, even in the old times. Now? Who knows what it carries. Debris, disease, maybe things that drift down from the blighted lands." He paused, a faraway look in his eyes. "We don't go near the big rivers anymore. Too open. Too many eyes watching. And too many things waiting in the water itself."

Mara sighed, a soft, weary sound. "It was decades ago, Gareth. My grandmother spoke of it. She said it was a place where people tried to live by the old ways, catching fish, farming the floodplains. They built high walls, she said, and had lookouts posted always. But that was before the blight really took hold in the south. Before the sickness spread so wide." Her voice trailed off, lost in old memories.

Elara felt a cold knot tighten in her stomach. Decades ago. The blight. The sickness. So many things could have happened to that village in the intervening years. It could be a ruin, a graveyard, or worse, a place infested by something unspeakable. Yet, it was the only lead they had.

Finn, who had been silent for so long, fiddling with his wooden bird, suddenly looked up, his eyes wide and earnest. "My dad said the river monsters were gone," he whispered, his voice barely audible above the fire. "He said the big ones died first, when the air turned bad."

Kael snorted again, a humorless sound. "Your dad was probably trying to make you feel safe, boy. There's always something in the water. Always. Just because you don't see it doesn't mean it isn't there. The land changed, the creatures changed with it. Some things got smaller, yes, but some things got... different." He looked at Finn with a gaze that was not unkind, but heavy with the weight of experience. "You learn to trust your gut, Finn. If it feels wrong, it probably is."

Gareth nodded slowly. "Survival is in caution, yes. But also in seeking out what is necessary. We need a place. A stable place, for the boy and for ourselves. The roads are no longer safe for long term travel. We need a base."

"And you think a ghost village on a poisoned river is your answer?" Kael challenged, his tone sharper now. "You're dreaming. Most of these 'settlements' are nothing more than short lived pockets of desperation. They get raided, or they starve out, or they succumb to the creeping rot. We've seen it time and again. We've seen too much of it."

Mara flinched at his directness. Her eyes, which had softened momentarily, hardened again. "We survive," she said, her voice quiet but firm. "We know the land. We know where to find roots, where to set snares, even if the pickings are lean. It is not an easy life, but it is our life. We have learned to be invisible when we need to be. A village, a fixed point, that is a target."

"A target, yes," Elara spoke up, surprising herself with the strength in her voice. "But also a community. Protection in numbers. Shared labor, shared burdens. A place to teach Finn. A place to rebuild some small part of what we lost." She looked at Mara, trying to convey understanding, not judgment. "We are all tired of running."

Kael studied Elara, a flicker of something unreadable in his eyes. He seemed to assess her, measuring her resolve. "Running is what keeps you alive out here, girl. The minute you stop, you become prey. The minute you build walls, you're just showing them where to attack."

Kael gave a dry, mirthless chuckle. "Who do you think? The desperate. The vicious. The ones who lost their minds and their humanity. The ones who cling to the shadows and prey on those with light. And the ones who aren't human anymore, not entirely. The ones the blight twisted."

A shiver ran down Elara's spine. Kael's eyes narrowed. "Depends. Some are just madmen, raiders driven to savagery by hunger and sickness. They'll take anything. Your food, your gear, your hope. Others... others are different. He paused, then added, his voice dropping almost to a whisper, "We saw a few, north of here, near the old iron mines. Not close, but close enough to know to run. They hunt in packs, like wolves, but smarter. More patient."

A heavy silence descended again, broken only by the persistent crackle of the fire. The cheerful warmth it provided felt suddenly inadequate against the chilling mental image Kael had painted.

Gareth took a deep breath. "This fishing village," he said, turning the conversation back to their original objective, "do you know anything more concrete about its location? Mara hesitated, then looked at Kael. He gave a slight nod, a concession. "My grandmother spoke of a high bluff," Mara began, her voice softer now, almost a storytelling tone. Elara mentally pictured it. A natural bluff, a deep channel, a stone watchtower. These were details, specific enough to search for, even if they were based on tales from decades ago. It gave the vague hope a tangible form.

"And how far is 'south'?" Gareth pressed gently.

"If we could find a boat," Elara mused aloud, "it might be faster."

Kael snorted. "A boat? You think you'll just find one floating down the river, intact and seaworthy? Any boat that survived would be guarded fiercely, or long since rotten. And as I said, the river itself is a dangerous place. More dangerous than the forest, sometimes."

"Perhaps," Gareth acknowledged, "but it is a possibility worth considering. We will need to decide in the morning whether to pursue this lead. We have exhausted our supplies and our hope for a northern settlement. South it is, then, if this is truly our best option."

Mara suddenly looked up, her gaze fixed on something beyond the firelight, into the deep shadows. Kael's head snapped up too, his hand instinctively going to the hilt of the worn hunting knife sheathed at his hip. Finn, still clutching Elara, sensed their tension and whimpered softly.

"Did you hear that?" Mara breathed, her voice barely audible.
