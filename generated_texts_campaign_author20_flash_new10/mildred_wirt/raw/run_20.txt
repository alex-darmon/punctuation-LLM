They were not just girls anymore, but secret agents, guardians of their town, protectors of the mill that produced vital parts for the war effort. Their everyday lives, once filled with school lessons, chores, and games, now had a thrilling, clandestine overlay.

Over the next few days, their routine underwent a subtle but profound transformation. Walking to school, they no longer skipped carelessly, pointing out dandelions or exchanging gossip about their classmates. Instead, their heads swiveled, their eyes darted, taking in every detail. They would tap messages to each other across the dinner table using their spoons, much to the confusion of their parents, who simply thought they were being exceptionally fidgety.

"Can you imagine what his messages must be like?" Betty whispered to Carol, her eyes wide with the imagined weight of secret intelligence.

Betty giggled, then quickly clamped a hand over her mouth. "Shush. Someone might hear."

They were huddled under the old oak tree at the edge of Betty's yard, its gnarled branches providing a natural canopy. Their mothers thought they were playing a quiet game before supper. In truth, they were planning their next day's surveillance.

The world had indeed transformed for them. Every familiar face now held a potential layer of secrecy. Every stranger was a possible threat. Their walk to school, once a simple journey, became a reconnaissance mission. They mentally mapped out every parked vehicle, noting license plates if they could read them, identifying the makes and models. A Ford parked where a Chevrolet usually sat was cause for suspicion. He was a young man, perhaps in his late teens, with unusually dark hair and eyes that seemed to dart around constantly, even as he stooped to prune a bush. He wore clothes that seemed a little too new, too clean for a gardener.

Betty nodded, her gaze fixed on him. "I've never seen him before. Mrs. Gable usually has Mr. Peterson."

Betty shook her head, her heart thumping a little faster. "I don't know. But it didn't look like a gardening tool."

Their observations of the gardener continued for the next few days. He was always there, meticulously tending Mrs. Gable's immaculate yard. But his presence felt wrong. The man moved with a purpose, his dark head down, seemingly oblivious to his surroundings. He stopped not at the main gate of the mill, but at a less-used service entrance around the back, near the loading docks. An old, beat-up panel van was parked there, its back doors slightly ajar.

The man quickly exchanged a few words with the driver, a burly figure whose face was obscured by the shadows inside the van. Betty and Carol pressed themselves further into the thick rhododendron bushes, their hearts hammering against their ribs. This was no ordinary gardener. This was something far more sinister.

"What do we do?" Carol whispered, her voice trembling slightly.

Betty's mind raced. They couldn't confront him. They couldn't tell anyone, not yet. Mr. Henderson's words echoed in her ears: "don't tell a soul." But they had seen something. Something that definitely did not look quite right.

Betty remembered an old trick from one of her detective stories: a 'dead drop' message, hidden in plain sight.

The Maple Creek General Store had a bulletin board near the entrance, where people posted notices for lost pets, yard sales, and sometimes even job openings. It was a public place, but also one frequented by Mr. Silas.

"We need a message," Betty said, her eyes fixed on the blueprint. "Something that connects to this, but doesn't give it away."

And the mention of Mrs. Gable's garden and "Mittens" was a nod to their cover story, a detail that would hopefully click with Mr. Silas once he realized the significance.

Carol looked at the message. "It's so clever, Betty. No one else would understand."

"Exactly," Betty said, a small thrill of triumph mixed with fear. "It's for him, and only him."

The next afternoon, after school, they executed the plan. They walked casually into the general store, Betty clutching the folded notice in her hand. Mr. Henderson, the store owner, was at the counter, chatting with a customer. Betty knew they had to be quick and unobtrusive.

A wave of relief washed over them, quickly followed by a fresh surge of fear. They were going to meet Mr. Silas. They were officially involved.

They arrived a few minutes before noon, their hearts thumping. Mr. Silas was already there, sitting at a table in a secluded corner of the reading room, ostensibly engrossed in a large, leather-bound book. He looked exactly as he always did: neatly dressed, spectacles perched on his nose, an air of quiet contemplation about him. Betty and Carol approached him, trying to appear nonchalant.

"Excuse me, Mr. Silas," Betty began, her voice a little too high pitched. "We were looking for a book on... on local history."

Mr. Silas slowly lowered his book, his eyes, usually gentle and faraway, now sharp and direct. He looked at them over the rim of his spectacles. He didn't smile.

"Local history, you say?" he murmured, his gaze sweeping over them, taking in their flushed faces and nervous fidgeting. "A fascinating subject. But perhaps you have something else on your mind, young ladies?"

Betty swallowed hard. This was it. She reached into her pocket and pulled out the crumpled blueprint. She laid it carefully on the table in front of him.

Mr. Silas's eyes immediately fell on the paper. His expression didn't change, but Betty could sense a subtle shift in his demeanor, a tightening around his lips, a sudden stillness in his hands. He picked up the blueprint, his fingers tracing the intricate lines of the diagram. He then looked at the numbers and the coded letters.

He didn't need to ask. He knew.

"Where did you get this?" he asked, his voice barely a whisper, but laced with an undeniable authority Betty had never heard from him before.

Betty quickly recounted their story: the new gardener, his suspicious behavior, the toolbox, the exchange with the van driver, and their daring retrieval of the blueprint. Carol chimed in, adding details about the gardener's watchful eyes and the close call.

Mr. Silas listened intently, his gaze never leaving their faces. When they finished, a long silence hung in the air, broken only by the rustle of pages from other readers.

Finally, he spoke. "You realize," he said, his voice grave, "that you have stumbled into something very serious. Far more serious than playing at detectives."

Betty and Carol nodded, their faces solemn. They knew. The encounter with the gardener had stripped away any lingering illusions of play.

"This is indeed a blueprint for a critical component produced at the mill," Mr. Silas continued, his finger resting on a specific part of the diagram. "And these numbers... they correspond to manufacturing specifications. The coded letters, however, are new. They suggest a communication method we haven't encountered yet."

He looked at them, a flicker of something akin to admiration in his usually reserved eyes. "You showed remarkable courage, and even more remarkable ingenuity. To deduce the nature of the threat, and to contact me in such a discreet manner..." He shook his head slightly. "You've done a great service."

But then his expression darkened. "However, you are now in grave danger. If they suspect you have this, and they will, you are targets."

Carol gasped, her hand flying to her mouth. Betty felt a cold knot form in her stomach.
