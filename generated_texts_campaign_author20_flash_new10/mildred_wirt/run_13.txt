Just as a wave of despair threatened to overwhelm her, her fingers brushed against a section of the shelf containing older, leather-bound volumes. One particular book, a hefty tome on agricultural law from the early 18th century, felt slightly looser in its placement than the others. She pulled it out. It was heavier than it should have been. She opened it. The pages within were indeed law, but a section in the middle had been carefully cut out, leaving a hollowed-out compartment.

Inside, nestled within the cavity, were three items. A thick, sealed envelope, unaddressed, secured with a broken wax seal bearing Edward’s crest. A smaller, tightly rolled parchment, tied with a simple black ribbon. And a small, intricately carved wooden box, no larger than her palm, with no visible lock or opening mechanism.

Clara’s hands trembled as she carefully extracted the items. This was it. This had to be what she was looking for. She held the sealed envelope up to the faint light. The broken wax seal seemed to mock her, yet also promised disclosure. She gently broke the remaining fragments of wax and carefully slid out the contents. It was a letter, penned in Edward’s elegant script.

Her eyes rapidly scanned the first lines: “To my beloved Josephine, and to Clara, her trusted guardian and companion, should this letter fall into her hands…” Clara’s breath caught in her throat. He had known. He had trusted her. The letter continued, detailing Edward’s growing suspicions about Alistair. He spoke of Alistair’s increasing debts, his clandestine meetings, and a particularly disturbing conversation he had overheard concerning the manipulation of his estate papers. “Alistair believes he has found a loophole, a way to circumvent my true intentions for Josephine,” Edward had written. “He is working with a solicitor, a man named Henderson, to create a spurious claim to my fortune, using a falsely represented document. He refers to it as the ‘copy’ which will replace the ‘original’ will I have carefully prepared for Josephine’s inheritance.”

Clara felt a cold dread wash over her. The "original" and "copy" and "tomorrow night" clicked into place with horrifying clarity. "Tomorrow night," Barnaby had said. That must be when the fraudulent will, the "copy," was to be formally presented or acted upon. Edward’s letter confirmed her worst fears. He wrote of his attempts to protect Josephine, to secure her future, but his illness had progressed too quickly. He stated that the true will, his "original," was hidden, along with further evidence of Alistair's deceit.

She turned to the rolled parchment. Untying the ribbon with fumbling fingers, she unrolled it. It was a meticulously drawn document, written on aged vellum, bearing Edward’s signature and the official seals of several witnesses. This was the "original" will. It bequeathed the entirety of Ashworth Manor and the bulk of his fortune to Josephine, placing her inheritance under Clara’s guardianship until she came of age. It explicitly stated that Alistair was to receive a small, fixed annuity, conditional on his good behavior and respectful treatment of Josephine. Any attempt to contest this will, it stated, would result in the forfeiture of even that annuity. This document was Josephine’s salvation.

Now, the small wooden box. It had no obvious lock. She turned it over, examining its smooth, polished surface. There must be a hidden mechanism. She pressed gently on various points, ran her finger along the seams. Finally, her thumb brushed against a barely perceptible indentation on one side. She pressed harder, and with a soft click, a small panel slid open. Inside, on a bed of faded velvet, lay a single, gleaming gold sovereign and a folded slip of paper. The paper held a single, terse instruction, again in Edward’s hand: “See Mrs. Albright. Number 17, Willow Lane. Show her the sovereign.” Mrs. Albright. Willow Lane. A name and an address she had never heard of. What could this mean? Was Mrs. Albright connected to Alistair’s schemes? Or was she an ally, someone Edward had trusted to hold further evidence?

The implications of her discovery flooded her mind. Alistair was not merely cold and unpleasant; he was a calculating villain, actively plotting to defraud Josephine of her inheritance. And "tomorrow night" was the deadline. She had found the original will. That was a monumental victory. But what about Mrs. Albright? What did she hold? And how was Clara to act before "tomorrow night" arrived? She needed to present this evidence, but to whom? The local constabulary would likely dismiss her word against Alistair’s, especially without irrefutable proof beyond a letter. A solicitor would be needed, someone incorruptible.

A sudden, sharp creak from the floorboards outside the study door sent a jolt of ice through her veins. Her small candle flame wavered wildly. Had she been heard? Was someone awake? She froze, her breath caught in her throat, every muscle tensed. She extinguished the candle with a quick, practiced flick of her fingers, plunging the room back into oppressive darkness. Her heart hammered, a frantic drum in the silence. She clutched the documents and the wooden box tightly. She pressed herself against the cold wood of the desk, straining her ears.

Another creak, closer this time, followed by the faint scuff of a shoe. Not the soft slipper of a maid. A heavier step. Alistair. Oh God, Alistair. Had he grown suspicious? Had he come down for another brandy? Or worse, had he come to retrieve something himself from the study?

Panic threatened to overwhelm her. She had to hide. The room was dark, but he knew its layout. There was no true hiding place. She risked everything on his possible inebriation, his assumed security. She stood absolutely still, blending into the shadows, a statue of terrified anticipation. The faint light from the corridor seeped under the study door, then faded slightly, as if someone had paused directly outside. Clara imagined Alistair’s shadow falling across the bottom of the door. She could almost feel his presence, cold and menacing. The silence was deafening, a suffocating blanket.

Minutes stretched into an eternity. Her muscles ached from the tension. She felt light-headed, dizzy with fear. Then, slowly, the light under the door brightened again, as if the person had moved away. The scuffing sound retreated, fading into the distance. He had moved on. He hadn't entered. He hadn't suspected. She let out a silent, shaky breath, her body trembling uncontrollably.

She gave herself a few precious moments to regain her composure, her heart still pounding a frantic rhythm. She then relit her small candle, her hands still shaking. She had to replace everything precisely as she had found it. Time was short. With meticulous care, she put the original will, Edward’s letter, and the small wooden box back into the hollowed-out book. She slid the book back into its place on the shelf, ensuring it was snug and unremarkable. No one would ever know it had been disturbed.

Before leaving, she took one final look around the study, ensuring that not a single paper was out of place on the desk, not a single book askew on the shelves. The room looked exactly as it had when she entered, a testament to Edward’s meticulous nature, and now, to her own careful transgression. She extinguished her candle, secured it and the tinderbox in her pocket, and unlocked the study door with extreme caution. The click was softer this time, almost imperceptible. She opened the door just enough to slip out, closing it silently behind her. Then, she locked it from the outside, the brass key turning smoothly in her trembling hand.

The journey back to her room was even more fraught with tension. Every shadow seemed to conceal a watchful eye, every creak of the house a warning. She moved with renewed purpose, the precious knowledge she carried burning within her. She reached her room without further incident, closing the door behind her with a sigh of immense relief.

She leaned against the door, her knees weak, the adrenaline slowly dissipating, leaving her utterly drained. She was safe, for now. But the night was far from over, and her mind was racing. She had the will, the proof of Alistair’s deceit. And the name: Mrs. Albright, 17 Willow Lane, to be presented with a gold sovereign. What further pieces of the puzzle did this mysterious woman hold? She knew her next steps, but they were fraught with peril. She needed to contact a trustworthy solicitor, and she needed to find Mrs. Albright, all before "tomorrow night." The phrase echoed in her head, a sinister countdown. The fragile hope that had flickered within her now blazed into a fierce determination. Edward had trusted her. Josephine’s future depended on her. She would not fail them. The game had not just begun; it had escalated into a desperate race against time.
