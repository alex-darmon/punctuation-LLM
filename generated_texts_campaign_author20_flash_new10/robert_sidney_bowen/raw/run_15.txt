Every breath he took felt heavy, dragging, as if the air itself was resisting his efforts to inhale.

His stomach gave a violent lurch, a hollow protest that echoed the emptiness inside him. He hadn’t eaten a proper meal in days. The last time he remembered consuming anything substantial was before the reconnaissance mission, a bland but filling nutrient paste. Now, the thought of food was both repulsive and intensely desirable. He eyed a sealed ration pack nearby, a foil-wrapped brick of concentrated calories. It sat there, an inert promise of energy, yet the effort required to tear it open, to chew, to swallow, felt like scaling a mountain. His jaw ached from clenching, his throat was dry and raw. He knew he needed fuel, his body was screaming for it, but the energy required to even *think* about eating seemed too great. He pushed the thought away, focusing again on Jan.

He pulled a small, digital thermometer from a medical kit, its stark red numbers glowing in the gloom. He carefully inserted it under Jan’s armpit, holding it in place with practiced gentleness. His fingers, despite the gloves, felt clumsy, stiff with cold. He watched the numbers slowly climb, then stall. The reading was still critically low. He sighed, a sound that seemed to scrape against his raw throat. He scribbled the number on a small, damp notepad, adding it to a long list of equally depressing readings. Each entry was a tiny hammer blow, chipping away at his already fragile hope.

“Still fighting, eh, old friend?” he murmured to Jan, his voice barely a whisper. “You always were stubborn. That time with the ice ax, remember? Off the coast of Svalbard? The ice was slick, you lost your footing. I thought you were gone. But you just dug in, held on with one hand, dangling there like a frozen icicle, and then pulled yourself back up as if it were an afternoon stroll.” He remembered the surge of adrenaline, the white-knuckled grip on the safety rope, the sheer terror that had seized him. And then Jan’s calm, almost bored, expression as he finally clambered back onto the ice shelf. “Just a bit of a slip, Mikael. Nothing to worry about.” Mikael had nearly collapsed with relief, while Jan had simply checked his gear and continued on. That unwavering resolve was what Mikael clung to now. He desperately needed that part of Jan to be still active, still fighting from within.

He felt the cold seeping deeper into his own bones, a pervasive chill that no amount of thermal layering seemed to repel. His extremities were numb, a dull ache in his fingers and toes. He shifted his position, trying to find a more comfortable spot against the supply crate, but every movement was a chore. His muscles protested, stiff and sore from the prolonged inactivity and the underlying tension. He felt a shiver ripple through his body, a deep, uncontrollable tremor that originated somewhere deep in his core. He hugged himself, trying to conserve what little heat he had.

The roaring wind outside intensified, rattling the metal walls of the shelter like a furious beast trying to tear its way in. It was a constant, omnipresent reminder of the unforgiving environment that lay just beyond their thin protection. He imagined the vast, desolate landscape outside, a swirling vortex of ice and snow, a truly alien world. The thought of stepping out into that maelstrom, even for a moment, sent a fresh wave of dread through him. They were trapped, marooned in a frozen hell.

He glanced at his watch again. Another five minutes had crept by. The 100 minutes he had initially marked as a critical milestone now seemed to stretch into an eternity. He had to keep track, had to stay rational, had to maintain some semblance of order in the chaos of his mind. If he lost that, he lost everything. He was the only one left to keep track, to make the decisions, to hold onto hope, however tenuous.

His thoughts drifted again, this time to the sheer improbability of their situation. They were two highly trained professionals, equipped with the best gear, backed by a global scientific community. They had faced countless dangers, overcome innumerable obstacles. And yet, here they were, brought low by a simple misstep, a treacherous patch of ice, a moment of lapsed vigilance. It felt absurd, a cruel cosmic joke. He wondered if anyone would ever find them. Would their last message have made it through the escalating interference? Had their emergency beacon been activated successfully? He couldn't remember. The last few hours before Jan’s injury were a blur of intense activity, a frantic race against the approaching storm.

He ran a hand over his face, feeling the grit of stubble and the faint sting of dried sweat. He needed to wash, to feel clean, to feel human. But such a luxury was unthinkable. Every drop of water, every calorie of energy, every moment of consciousness had to be conserved. He was a machine, a stripped down version of his former self, operating purely on instinct and a desperate will to survive. Yet, even machines needed maintenance, and his was rapidly breaking down.

A fleeting memory of a warm shower, the steam rising, the scent of soap, flashed through his mind. It was a tantalizing vision, almost unbearable in its contrast to his current reality. He pushed it away, gritting his teeth. He couldn't afford such distractions. He needed to be here, present, for Jan.

He leaned closer to Jan, placing his ear near his mouth, listening intently for any change in his breathing. It was still shallow, ragged, but present. A tiny spark of relief flickered within him, quickly extinguished by the overwhelming fear. He pulled back, his eyes scanning Jan’s face, looking for any sign of recognition, any flicker of movement. There was none. His face was still, a pale mask against the dark hood of his sleeping bag.

“I’m going to tell you about that time we went hiking in the Dolomites, Jan,” Mikael began again, his voice a little stronger this time, as if speaking of a happier memory could conjure some warmth into the air. “Remember that little village, with the church bells ringing, and the smell of fresh bread? We stayed in that guesthouse, and the landlady made the best apple strudel you’ve ever tasted. You ate three pieces, you greedy bastard. And we sat on that balcony, watching the sunset paint the mountains pink and gold. We talked about everything and nothing. About life, about purpose, about the stars. You said you felt closer to the universe out there than you ever did in a lab. I remember you had this look on your face, this pure, unadulterated joy. I haven’t seen you look like that in years, not since we started coming to these godforsaken places.”

He paused, a lump forming in his throat. The memories were a bittersweet torment, a reminder of what they had and what they might lose. He imagined Jan’s slow smile, the way his eyes would crinkle at the corners when he was truly amused. He missed that smile more than words could express. He missed Jan’s steady presence, his quiet humor, his unflappable calm.

He reached out and gently touched Jan’s gloved hand, his fingers resting lightly on the back of it. It was cold, unnervingly so. He wanted to rub it, to generate some warmth, but he knew it would be futile. The cold was too deep, too pervasive. It was an enemy that enveloped them both, slowly stealing their warmth, their energy, their very life force.

“We’ll go back there, Jan,” Mikael vowed, his voice thick with emotion. “When we get out of this. I promise. We’ll ditch the ice axes and the crampons for once. Just hiking boots and good food and warm beds. And no scientific instruments, just pure enjoyment. You’ll love it. You always did say you needed more balance in your life. Less ice, more sunshine. Less data, more genuine human connection.” He forced a weak smile, a desperate attempt to inject some lightness into the suffocating atmosphere. It felt hollow, brittle.

His eyes scanned the small space around them. Spare oxygen tanks, medical supplies neatly stacked, a communication array that was currently silent. Everything was in order, a testament to their rigorous training and Jan’s meticulous organizational skills. Even in this chaotic situation, there was a quiet efficiency to their emergency setup. It was one of Jan’s strengths; he could bring order to almost any situation, a calming presence in a storm. Now, Mikael was the one left to maintain that order, to embody that calm, and he felt utterly inadequate.

He remembered a particularly harrowing ascent they had made years ago, a jagged peak shrouded in mist. They had been caught in an unexpected whiteout, visibility dropping to mere feet. Mikael, less experienced then, had started to panic. He remembered his heart hammering against his ribs, the icy fear gripping him. Jan, however, had remained completely unperturbed. He had calmly unclipped his pack, retrieved a small GPS device, and without a word, had started triangulating their position. He had then taken the lead, moving slowly, deliberately, placing each foot with absolute precision. Mikael had followed, his eyes fixed on Jan’s back, a beacon of unwavering resolve in the swirling white void. “Just focus on the next step, Mikael,” Jan had said, his voice a low rumble, barely audible above the wind. “One foot in front of the other. That’s all we can do.” They had made it down, battered and exhausted, but safe. Mikael wished he could hear those words now, wished he could feel Jan’s steady presence guiding him through this internal whiteout.

He closed his eyes again, trying to replicate that focus, that single-minded determination. One minute at a time. One breath at a time. One pulse check. One temperature reading. Break it down into the smallest possible units, and maybe, just maybe, he could get through this.

He thought about the immense distances they had covered, the impossible landscapes they had traversed, all in the name of science, in the pursuit of knowledge. What was the point of it all, he wondered, if it led to this? To a cold, lonely death in the middle of nowhere? The question was a venomous whisper in his mind, threatening to unravel his last threads of resilience. He fought it back, forcing his thoughts towards purpose. They were here for a reason. They were contributing. Their data, if it ever made it back, would be invaluable. This thought provided a small, fleeting anchor.

He heard a faint creaking sound, not from the wind, but from somewhere inside the shelter. He tensed, his head snapping up, his senses on high alert. Was it just the structure settling, or something else? He strained his ears, listening over the incessant howl of the storm. Nothing. Just the wind. His imagination, frayed by exhaustion and fear, was playing tricks on him. He rubbed his temples, trying to clear his head.

He decided he needed to move, to keep his blood circulating, to stave off the deepening cold. He slowly pushed himself up from the supply crate, his joints protesting with a chorus of aches and stiffness. He walked a few paces, a slow, shuffling gait, to the small, makeshift stove they had managed to set up earlier. It was a crude affair, a modified fuel barrel with a small vent pipe leading outside, but it provided a precious, albeit minimal, amount of heat. The fuel was running low, a stark reminder of their dwindling resources. He knelt, checking the fuel gauge, his heart sinking at the sight of the needle hovering near empty. He had perhaps another few hours of warmth, if he was careful, if he rationed it. After that, they would be relying solely on their thermal layers and the inherent insulation of the shelter. He pushed a few small pieces of compressed fuel into the chamber, trying to make them last. Every decision was a calculation of survival, a grim arithmetic of dwindling resources versus time.

He returned to Jan’s side, kneeling beside him, his gaze fixed on his friend’s face. He knew he couldn’t give up. Not yet. Not while there was still a flicker of life, however faint. He remembered Jan’s own words during a particularly tough training session. “Pain is temporary, Mikael. Quitting lasts forever.” Those words, usually delivered with Jan’s characteristic dry wit, now resonated with a brutal truth. He couldn’t quit. He wouldn’t.

He picked up the small notepad again, flipping through the pages filled with Jan’s vital signs. Heart rate, respiration, body temperature. A grim medical chart of a life hanging by a thread. He focused on the last entry, the critically low temperature. He knew, intellectually, what this meant. He knew the statistics, the grim probabilities. But he refused to accept them. He had to believe in Jan’s resilience, in the strength he had witnessed so many times before.

“You’re going to be fine, Jan,” he said, his voice gaining a desperate certainty he did not feel. He was talking to himself as much as to Jan. “You’re a fighter. You always have been. We’ve been through worse, haven’t we? Remember that time with the polar bear? You stared it down, for crying out loud. A massive brute, and you just stood there, calm as you please, until it finally lumbered away. I was ready to empty my clip, but you just gave it that look, that famous Jan look, and it turned tail. Incredible.”

The memory, though tinged with adrenaline, brought a flicker of amusement to Mikael’s grim face. Jan had indeed possessed an almost supernatural calm in the face of danger, a quality Mikael had always admired and relied upon. He pictured the bear, a colossal white shadow against the snow, and Jan, slender and seemingly insignificant, yet radiating an undeniable authority. It was a testament to Jan’s incredible will, a will that Mikael now desperately hoped was still fighting within his unconscious body.

He felt the cold again, a creeping numbness in his feet. He wiggled his toes inside his boots, trying to restore circulation. He knew the dangers of frostbite all too well. It was a constant threat in this environment, a silent, insidious enemy that worked hand in hand with hypothermia. He had to be vigilant, not just for Jan, but for himself. If he succumbed, Jan would have no chance. The responsibility was a heavy cloak, weighing him down, but also providing a strange sort of focus.

He decided to perform another check of Jan’s pulse, even though it was not yet time for the next official recording. He needed the reassurance, however brief. He carefully located the artery in Jan’s neck, his gloved fingers pressing lightly against the cold skin. He concentrated, counting the faint, thready beats. They were still there, still weak, but present. A small victory, a temporary reprieve from the crushing despair. He let out a slow, shaky breath he hadn’t realized he was holding.

The weight of the 100 minutes until the next official check still loomed, a vast chasm of time. He had to fill it, had to keep his mind occupied, had to prevent himself from spiraling into the abyss of hopelessness. He decided to mentally review their evacuation protocol, going over each step, each contingency plan. It was a sterile, logical exercise, a way to impose order on the chaos. He visualized the arrival of the rescue team, the warm embrace of safe hands, the journey back to civilization. He forced himself to believe it was possible, that this nightmare would end.

He stood up again, slowly, and walked to the comms station. The screen was dark, the signal light off. He knew it was futile to try and transmit anything now; the storm was too fierce, the interference too severe. But he felt compelled to check, a ritual born of desperation. He gently touched the cold metal casing of the radio, as if willing it to life, willing it to connect them to the outside world. He imagined the satellite dish on top of their main module, currently buried under meters of snow and ice, a silent, useless sentinel.

He returned to Jan, his internal monologue resuming its desperate rhythm. “You know, I remember that time we got stuck in that blizzard in Patagonia,” he continued, his voice a little hoarser now. “We were pinned down for three days, remember? We thought we were out of food, out of options. And then you, you clever bastard, you found that old, forgotten supply cache, just a few tins of preserved beef and some crackers. It tasted like a gourmet meal then, didn’t it? And you kept our spirits up, telling those terrible jokes, making us laugh when we thought we’d never feel warmth again. I owe you for that, Jan. I owe you for a lot of things.”

He closed his eyes again, letting the memories wash over him, a temporary shield against the brutal present. He remembered Jan’s strength, his resilience, his quiet humor. He remembered the feeling of absolute trust he had in his friend, the certainty that no matter how dire the situation, Jan would find a way. Now, that trust was being tested to its absolute limits. Mikael had to be Jan’s strength, Jan’s resilience, Jan’s will to survive. He had to hold on for both of them.

He knew he was fading, his own energy reserves dangerously low. His vision flickered occasionally, the edges of his sight blurring. He could feel the cold seeping deeper, a heavy cloak that threatened to drag him down into unconsciousness. He fought it, every fiber of his being resisting the seductive pull of oblivion. He slapped his cheeks lightly, trying to rouse himself, trying to stay awake, to stay alert.

“Sleep is a luxury we cannot afford, Mikael,” he heard Jan’s voice in his head, clear and precise, as if he were right there, lecturing him. “Especially when lives are on the line.” The imagined admonition spurred him, sending a fresh jolt of determination through his weary body. He wouldn’t sleep. Not yet. Not until the 100 minutes were up, not until the next temperature check, not until he knew, for certain, that he had done everything humanly possible.

He looked at his watch again, the glowing digits a cruel testament to the slow passage of time. Only eight minutes had passed since his last check. The vast ocean of their wait still stretched before him, an endless expanse of cold and fear. He had to find a way to navigate it, to keep sailing, even if it was against the most ferocious of storms. He was alone at the helm, and the fate of two men rested in his numb, trembling hands. He was Mikael, and he would not give up. Not on Jan, and not on himself.
