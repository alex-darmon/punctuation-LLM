"What?" Colonel Harding exploded as he looked from Riley to Jenkins, and back again. "What in blazes was that?"

"Jenkins, sir," Captain Riley explained. "We made about a hundred guesses apiece as to what this mission was truly about. One of mine was that we were transporting the new cipher unit to North Africa, or beyond, for the Allied high command."

"Nobody heard you make that guess, did they?" the colonel asked, tight-lipped, as he fixed his eyes on young Riley.

"No, sir!" the American pilot replied. "Nobody."

"He's right, sir," Sergeant Jenkins spoke up quickly. "I remember when he made that guess he spoke so low I could hardly hear him, and I was strapped right next to him. In case you're wondering, Colonel, it wasn't until we were on approach to the relay station that the enemy fighters jumped us. So it's certain they didn't hear Maverick."

"Yes, of course you're right," the colonel said, and a flicker of something passed over his stern face. "So don't feel bad. It just gave me a start that you had hit so close to the mark. You were partly wrong, though. It wasn't the cipher unit, not yet anyway."

"And the packages, sir?" Riley asked when the colonel fell silent and stared out the compartment window at the desolate expanse of the Sahara sweeping by. "They are still very hush-hush stuff, as far as we're concerned? Could I ask if they contained information about the General's movements?"

The senior officer turned from the window and looked straight at him. "You can, and I'll tell you," he said. "Each package contained a high-frequency decoy transmitter, set to mimic the General's personal communications channel, the exact operational schedule, and the dummy codes to be used in case the transport ran into trouble, or danger, and all that sort of thing. In short, as I told you in Washington, the enemy would give almost anything to get hold of one of those sealed containers. With that information in their possession, they could have delivered a terrible blow to our forces. Think of it! The loss of the General and members of the Allied High Command! It would be like setting our war effort back to the darkest days!"

The horrible thought made Riley shiver in spite of himself, and he thanked God that Jenkins and he had secured their packages before the enemy fighters had descended. The General's death would have been loss enough, but to have added the loss of the great leaders of our military and naval forces would have been world shaking indeed.

"And now, sir?" Jenkins asked after several moments of silence. "Now another plan is to be carried out?"

Colonel Harding didn't answer for a moment. He stared down at his two hands folded on the edge of the small table, and the expression on his thin face seemed to show a reluctance to answer that question. Presently, though, he lifted his head and looked straight at the two youthful airmen. "We are now headed for Tobruk," he began quietly. "With the extra fuel tanks we have aboard, we can make it easily. If we reach Tobruk without any trouble, I will be as sure as a man can be that the enemy has not learned anything of the General's plan to fly there himself. If we don't--" The Chief of all Allied Intelligence let the rest trail off into thin air and made a little gesture with one hand.

Riley frowned and looked at him earnestly. "I don't think I get what you mean, sir," he said slowly.

"And neither do I, sir," Sergeant Jenkins spoke up.

For a moment the colonel held his lips pressed together in a thin, grim line, and a hard light glittered in his eyes. "In a thing like this," he said presently, "you can't afford to take _any_ chances. You've got to be dead sure; as dead sure of everything as it is humanly possible to be, from start to finish. I had utmost confidence in your making the complete flight to the relay station. And the way you two did handle yourselves, when the odds were actually all against you, proves that the confidence I had in you was justified. But in everything there is ever present the little item of fate. A tiny little something that is beyond man's power to see in advance, or even to counteract when it happens. For example, that radio operator at Benghazi. I would have staked my life on that man. But, as things turned out, I was completely mistaken. And so with you two, or with each of my agents at the stops you were to make. Because of something you couldn't guard against, or prevent before death came to you, the contents of one of those sealed packages might have fallen into enemy hands. What I mean is, one of the packages might have been opened, the contents read, and then the packages resealed."

"But, Colonel," Riley protested, "one of us would--"

"I know, I know," the colonel said, stopping him with a gesture of his hand. "But look at it this way. Suppose their commandos had knocked you both out while you still had the packages? Suppose they had opened one, read its contents, and resealed it so that you'd never have guessed? What then? When you came to and found you still had the packages, you'd never dream that they had been touched."

"But I'd be plenty suspicious, sir!" Riley interrupted. "I'd--"

"Would you?" the colonel's quiet but firm voice stopped him again. "But their intelligence was no fool! What if they stole your watches and Jenkins's money, too? What then?"

"I see what you mean, sir," Riley said, and grinned sheepishly. "We would have thought we'd been victims of some desert raiders."

"Exactly," the colonel agreed. "A crazy little twist of fate over which you had no control whatever. Yet the damage would have been done. So I had to do what I could to find out if there had been any crazy twist of fate. In other words, each of those sealed packages contained the information, in code of course, that the _next_ transport plane to pass through would carry the General, and members of his party."

Riley blinked, and suddenly the truth hit him between the eyes. "What, sir?" he gasped. "You--you mean this B-17 is _supposed_ to be carrying the General?"

"I mean just that!" the colonel confirmed grimly. "_If_ enemy agents have learned what was in those packages, they will believe that this bomber is carrying the General as a passenger. The General has already left Alexandria in secret, and it wouldn't take much checking by enemy agents to find out that he isn't at the forward base. Naturally they'd believe he was aboard this plane."

"Anything funny happen on your flight down, sir?" Sergeant Jenkins asked, as the senior officer paused for breath.

"Nothing that I noticed," Colonel Harding replied with a shake of his head. "But just because things don't happen doesn't mean that they _won't_, in time. So, as I said, we won't know for sure until we arrive at Tobruk."

"And maybe not even then," Riley mumbled to himself.

Colonel Harding gave the young pilot a sharp look, and then nodded his head. "That's right," he agreed. "And maybe not even then. Just another reason why an Intelligence man gets gray hair so early in life. You never can tell about a job until it's all finished and you're working on another. Then it's the same thing all over again."

The trio lapsed into silence, but not for long, because the question that had been plaguing Riley just had to come out. "Supposing we make it to Tobruk okay," he said, "and you feel sure that the enemy hasn't learned a thing about the General's trip, what then? The sealed orders Jenkins and I were to have delivered at the rest of the stops are destroyed, and you say you collected the packages we left at Benghazi and Tripoli. How will they know about the General's plane when it does come through?"

"A good question, but I've got the answer, Riley." The colonel smiled and pointed to a briefcase on his small table. "In there are duplicates of the orders, _without_ the part about the next bomber through being the General's plane. If we reach Tobruk safely, we'll turn around and head east for Alexandria, cross the desert to Suez, and deliver one of those sealed packages to each of the stops as we fly north to Cairo. I've allowed sufficient time for us to do that, in case that's the way it works out."

"Well," Riley remarked, and shifted to a more comfortable position on his chair, "there's nothing like a double cross-country--" But he never finished his sentence, because at that moment the nose of the B-17 dipped sharply.

The sudden, violent dip of the B-17 threw Riley forward against his harness, his hands instinctively gripping the control column. A sharp, grating noise, like a giant wrench tearing through metal, vibrated through the airframe, followed by the shriek of wind rushing through a new breach. Sergeant Jenkins, startled mid-sentence, lurched sideways, his head bumping hard against the side of the cockpit, but his hands were already moving, checking instruments, his eyes scanning the gauges with practiced speed. Colonel Harding, less accustomed to the brutal physics of aerial combat, was thrown completely out of his seat despite the seatbelt, landing with a grunt in the narrow aisle behind the pilots. His briefcase, which had been resting on the small table, skittered across the floor, spilling its contents in a cascade of papers and the duplicate sealed packages.

"What in blazes was that?" Jenkins shouted over the sudden roar, his voice tight with surprise and alarm.

"No idea!" Riley yelled back, wrestling with the controls. The B-17, usually so steady and reliable, felt like a child's toy being shaken by an angry hand. He fought to bring the nose up, pushing the throttles forward. The engines roared in response, but the bomber still wallowed, refusing to obey with its usual alacrity. "We've been hit!"

Before he could finish the sentence, the interphone crackled to life, a high-pitched voice piercing the chaos. "Fighters! Two of 'em! Coming in high on our six o'clock!" It was Sergeant Miller, the tail gunner, his words clipped and urgent. "Messerschmitts! Make that three! Three Me 109s!"

Riley swore under his breath. So it wasn't just turbulence. It was the confirmation of their worst fears. The enemy knew. They had taken the bait. And now they were paying for it. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drumbeat of adrenaline and cold fury. "Jenkins, get the guns going! Call out their positions!"

Jenkins, already recovered from his bump, was a flurry of motion. He flipped switches, his fingers dancing across the instrument panel. "Roger! Miller, you hear that? Keep 'em busy! Top turret, waist guns, bombardier, everybody on alert! These boys mean business!"

Colonel Harding, having scrambled back into his seat and secured himself, was surprisingly calm. He picked up his scattered briefcase contents, his eyes darting around the cabin, taking in the scene. He met Riley's gaze, his expression grim but resolute. "Looks like our ruse was a little too successful, pilot," he said, his voice strained but steady.

Another burst of machine gun fire ripped through the air, closer this time, accompanied by the dull thud of slugs impacting the metal skin of the bomber. A shriek of tortured metal echoed from somewhere in the rear fuselage. The B-17 lurched again, listing hard to port.

"Left outboard engine is smoking!" Jenkins reported, his voice a strained shout above the din. "And we've got some damage to the port wing flap! Control's sluggish!"

Riley felt the controls stiffen in his hands. The rudder pedals felt heavy and unresponsive. He fought against the plane's desire to roll over, struggling to maintain a semblance of level flight. "Tell Miller to focus on the one on our tail! Jenkins, keep an eye on those gauges. If that engine goes, we'll lose altitude fast." He knew they were in a bad spot. A four-engine bomber with one engine damaged was a sitting duck if they couldn't shake their attackers.

"They're coming around again!" Miller's voice screeched over the interphone. "Starboard side! Low and fast!"

Riley pushed the column forward, attempting a shallow dive to gain speed, then pulled back sharply, trying to throw the attacking fighter off its aim. The manoeuvre was sluggish, the damaged flap making the plane respond like a wounded beast. Outside, a stream of tracer rounds flashed past the cockpit window, a terrifying necklace of fiery beads.

"Top turret firing!" a new voice announced, Private Smith, the top turret gunner. The rhythmic roar of the twin .50 caliber machine guns reverberated through the bomber, a welcome sound of defiance.

"Waist guns too!" another voice added. The *Spirit of Alexandria* was now a flying porcupine, bristling with defensive fire.

But the Messerschmitts were fast and agile. They darted in and out, their attacks precise and deadly. One swept past, its cannons spitting fire, raking the fuselage. The air was filled with the acrid smell of burnt wiring and metal. Dust and debris showered the cockpit. A small section of the dashboard near Jenkins' knee sparked violently before going dead.

"Power's out to the radio!" Jenkins yelled, tapping the now dark panel. "And the auxiliary fuel tank gauge!"

"Forget the radio for now," Riley commanded, his voice sharp and focused. "Keep an eye on that engine. And tell me where those devils are coming from!"

The fight was a brutal ballet of destruction. The B-17, slow and ponderous, tried to weave and dodge, while the sleek German fighters zipped around it like angry wasps. Riley pulled the bomber into a shallow climb, hoping to make himself a harder target for the faster, lower attacks. He could feel the strain on the airframe, the protest of every rivet and joint.

"Another pass, port side, coming high!" Miller reported, his voice hoarse now. "He's lining up on the wing!"

Riley instinctively threw the plane into a violent evasive turn, grunting with effort. The bomber shrieked in protest, the port wing dipping alarmingly close to stalling speed. But the maneuver worked. The Messerschmitt's burst of fire streaked harmlessly past, missing the wing by mere feet.

"That was close!" Jenkins breathed, his knuckles white on the controls he wasn't even using.

"Too close," Riley muttered, wiping a bead of sweat from his forehead with the back of his gloved hand. He knew they couldn't keep this up. The B-17 was taking a beating, and their attackers seemed relentless.

"Sir, are we still heading for Tobruk?" Jenkins asked, as the lull between attacks stretched for a tense few seconds.

"What else can we do?" Riley replied, his eyes scanning the horizon, searching for any sign of escape, any cloud cover, anything. "We're in the middle of nowhere. Tobruk's our only chance."

Colonel Harding, who had been observing the fight with a grim fascination, leaned forward. "Are the engines holding?" he asked, his gaze fixed on the smoking port engine.

"The left outboard is losing power, sir," Jenkins confirmed, his finger hovering over a switch. "RPMs are dropping. I don't think she'll hold for much longer."

As if on cue, the left outboard engine coughed, sputtered, and then died with a shudder that ran through the entire aircraft. A trail of black smoke billowed from its cowling.

"Feather it, Jenkins!" Riley barked, feeling the immediate imbalance as the bomber began to yaw to the left. He pressed hard on the right rudder, fighting to maintain control. "Now!"

Jenkins swiftly cut the fuel to the dead engine and activated the feathering mechanism, the propeller blades slowly rotating to reduce drag. The immediate effect was a noticeable loss of speed and altitude. The B-17 began to sink, albeit slowly, into the vast, unforgiving desert below.

"Altitude dropping, Riley!" Jenkins called out, his voice tinged with urgency. "We're losing about five hundred feet a minute!"

"I know!" Riley snapped, focusing all his concentration on keeping the crippled bomber flying. He knew the drill. Three engines could keep a B-17 in the air, but not indefinitely, especially not with damaged controls and under attack.

"They're back!" Miller's voice shattered the brief reprieve. "Four o'clock high! Coming in for another run!"

This time, the attack was more coordinated. One Messerschmitt approached from the starboard, drawing fire, while another slipped in from below and behind, raking the belly of the B-17. The roar of the belly turret's guns joined the chorus, but it was a desperate defense.

Suddenly, a loud bang erupted from the rear of the bomber, followed by a sickening thud. The B-17 shuddered violently.

"Miller's gone silent!" a new voice, Sergeant Peterson from the waist guns, shouted over the interphone, his voice laced with panic. "Tail's hit bad! I think Miller's hit!"

Riley's gut clenched. Miller was a good man, steady and reliable. To lose him, to have the tail gunner silenced, left their most vulnerable quarter exposed. He tried to bank, to present a harder target, but the plane was sluggish, protesting every command.

"We need to get out of here!" Colonel Harding stated, his voice firm, cutting through the rising tide of fear. "Can you lose them, Riley?"

"Not at this speed, sir," Riley replied grimly. "We're too slow, and they're too fast. We need cover, or we need to fight them off."

"Then fight them off!" the colonel commanded, his voice surprisingly calm despite the raging battle. "This plane, this mission, they're too important. We don't give up."

Riley nodded, a fierce determination hardening his jaw. He wouldn't give up. Not with the General's reputation, and perhaps his life, indirectly resting on this decoy mission. He pushed the remaining three engines to their absolute limit, trying to coax every last ounce of speed from them. The airframe groaned in protest.

"They're circling!" Jenkins reported, peering through the bullet-pocked windshield. "They seem to be taking stock. They know we're crippled."

This momentary lull was almost worse than the active fighting. It was the predator circling its wounded prey, assessing the best way to deliver the killing blow.

"Any word from Miller?" Riley asked, his voice low, filled with dread.

"Negative, sir," Peterson replied, his voice heavy. "I can't raise him. And the tail section looks... bad. Really bad."

"Bombardier, navigator, anyone got eyes on the ground?" Riley asked. "Anywhere to go? Any place to put this bird down?"

"Nothing but sand, sir," replied Sergeant Cooper, the bombardier, his voice tight. "Just miles and miles of nothing."

"Wait!" Sergeant Davis, the navigator, chimed in, his voice slightly clearer. "I see something! A ridge line, about ten miles ahead, bearing two-seven-zero. Looks like it might have some wadis, some depressions."

"Wadis aren't good for landing a B-17," Jenkins muttered.

"They're better than nothing if it gives us cover!" Riley snapped, his mind already racing, calculating trajectories and angles. "Can we make it, Davis?"

"At our current speed and descent rate, it'll be tight, sir," Davis replied. "But possible. It's our only shot at shaking them off, or at least making ourselves harder targets."

"Alright then," Riley decided, his voice steel. "New heading, two-seven-zero. All guns, listen up! We're making a run for that ridge. If they come in again, hit them with everything you've got! Don't let them get a clean shot at us while we're going down."

He began to gently turn the B-17 towards the new heading, feeling the drag from the damaged wing and the dead engine. The bomber responded slowly, ponderously, like an old battleship trying to maneuver in a storm.

As they approached the ridge, the Messerschmitts, as if sensing their intent, sprang to life again. This time, they came in low, one on each side, attempting to box in the crippled bomber. Their cannons spewed fire, and machine gun rounds stitched lines of holes across the fuselage.

"Starboard engine's smoking!" Jenkins yelled, pointing frantically. "Number four engine, it's hit!"

A sickening wave of despair washed over Riley, but he pushed it down, hard. Not now. He was a pilot, and his job was to keep this plane in the air. Two engines. Two remaining engines against three highly agile fighters. The odds were rapidly dwindling to zero.

He felt the controls go even heavier, the B-17 yawing violently as the starboard outboard engine coughed, sputtered, and died, joining its counterpart in silent, feathered defiance. The bomber shuddered, its wings struggling to generate enough lift. They were sinking faster now, the ground rushing up at an alarming rate.

"We're losing altitude too fast!" Jenkins cried, his voice edged with panic. "We won't make the ridge at this rate, Riley!"

Riley didn't respond, his jaw clenched, his eyes fixed on the rapidly approaching terrain. He pushed the remaining two engines to full power, their roar a desperate plea for speed. He knew the limits of the B-17, knew that two engines, even healthy ones, were barely enough to keep a fully loaded bomber airborne, let alone one riddled with holes and carrying a dead engine on each wing. But he had to try. He had to.

"Top turret's hit!" Smith's voice, now raw with pain, gasped over the interphone. "I'm hit! They're coming around again!"

"Colonel, get ready for a crash landing!" Riley yelled, his voice strained. "Jenkins, try to dump whatever you can! Anything that'll lighten us!"

"Roger!" Jenkins immediately began flipping switches, releasing the bomb bay doors, though there were no bombs to drop. He hoped the slight reduction in drag might help.

Colonel Harding, instead of cowering, was already unbuckling his seatbelt. "What about the packages, Riley?" he asked, pointing to his scattered briefcase. "They can't fall into enemy hands!"

Riley spared a quick glance at the papers and packages strewn across the floor. "Forget them, sir! Your life is more important! Just brace yourself!"

But the colonel ignored him. With surprising agility, he began to gather the sealed envelopes and the duplicate orders, stuffing them back into his briefcase with frantic haste. He seemed to have a superhuman focus on this task, even as the bomber plunged downwards, the ground rushing up to meet them.

"They're still on us!" Peterson shouted from the waist. "They're making another pass!"

Riley heard the stutter of their machine guns, felt the impacts shudder through the airframe. He knew this was it. He had to make a decision, and fast.

"Brace for impact!" he roared, pulling back hard on the stick, trying to bring the nose up just enough to stall out rather than dive into the sand. He wrestled with the controls, fighting the wounded beast of an aircraft, trying to flatten its descent.

The B-17 hit the ground with a sickening screech of metal, not a graceful landing but a controlled crash. The impact was brutal, a series of bone-jarring jolts as the landing gear buckled and tore away, then the belly scraped against the hard desert floor. Sand, rocks, and debris exploded around them, filling the cockpit. Riley was slammed forward, his head snapping back against the headrest, then forward again as the bomber bounced, skidded, and finally spun, grinding to a halt in a cloud of dust and steam.

The silence that followed was deafening, broken only by the groaning of tortured metal, the trickle of fuel, and the ragged breathing of the surviving crew. The smell of burning oil and dust filled the air.

Riley, dazed but conscious, slowly became aware of the searing pain in his left arm, which felt twisted and useless. He unbuckled his harness, his hands shaking. "Jenkins? Colonel?" he rasped, his voice hoarse.

Jenkins was slumped over the control panel, a gash bleeding freely across his forehead. He slowly pushed himself up, groaning. "I'm... I'm alright, Riley. Just a bump." He wiped blood from his eyes, blinking furiously. "You?"

"Arm's busted, I think," Riley said, testing it gingerly. A sharp pain shot through him, confirming his suspicion. "Colonel Harding?"

Colonel Harding, though clearly shaken, seemed miraculously unscathed. He was still clutching his briefcase, its contents now more jumbled than before, but apparently secure. He looked around the shattered cockpit, his eyes wide with a mixture of shock and relief. "I'm... I'm intact, pilot. Remarkably so."

"Anyone else report in!" Riley yelled, pushing the interphone button, but it was dead. "Jenkins, go check on the crew! See who's still alive, who's hurt! Be quick!"

Jenkins, though unsteady, nodded and began to move, unbuckling his own harness. "Peterson! Davis! Cooper! Smith! Miller! Anyone hear me?" he shouted, his voice echoing in the wrecked fuselage.

Riley tried to assess their situation. The cockpit was a mess of twisted metal and shattered glass. Outside, through the broken windshield, he could see the desert stretching endlessly, punctuated by the ridge line they had been aiming for, now a good distance behind them. The B-17 lay broken, its wings crumpled, the engines ripped from their mounts or mangled beyond recognition. Smoke drifted lazily from the fuselage.

"We have to get clear of the plane!" Colonel Harding urged, his voice regaining some of its earlier authority. "If those Messerschmitts decide to finish the job, they'll be back, and this metal coffin will be a fire trap!"

"You're right," Riley agreed, pushing himself up despite the pain in his arm. He tried to open his side hatch, but it was jammed. "Jenkins, try the rear escape hatch!" he shouted.

Jenkins, already moving towards the rear, stumbled over debris. "I'm on it!" he yelled back.

Minutes felt like hours. Jenkins finally managed to pry open a bent escape hatch near the waist gunner's position. He emerged, his face grim. "Peterson's got a broken leg, but he's conscious. Cooper, the bombardier, is fine. Davis, the navigator, has a bad concussion. Smith, the top turret gunner, he's... he's gone, Riley. And Miller, the tail gunner... I couldn't even reach the tail section. It's completely torn away. I think he's gone too."

A wave of sorrow washed over Riley. Two good men, gone. Casualties of a decoy mission. The weight of command, the responsibility for his crew, pressed down on him. He forced himself to focus.

"Alright," he said, his voice raw. "Help Peterson out. We need to get everyone clear of the plane. Colonel, can you assist with Davis?"

The three able-bodied men worked quickly, pulling Peterson, who grimaced in pain but tried to help, and then the dazed Davis from the wreckage. They laid them gently on the sand, a safe distance from the smoldering bomber.

Riley looked up at the sky, scanning for any sign of the Messerschmitts. The sky was empty, but he knew they wouldn't be far. They would have seen the crash, would be reporting it, perhaps circling higher, waiting to confirm no survivors, or for reinforcements.

"This is it, then," Colonel Harding said, surveying the wreck of the B-17 and the desolate landscape. "Our mission. It worked, Riley. They believed it. They came for us." There was a strange, grim satisfaction in his voice, mixed with the sorrow for the fallen. "But now what?"

"Now we survive, sir," Riley stated, his eyes hard. He looked at the injured, then at the wreck. "We're in the middle of nowhere. We need water, food, and a way to signal for help. And we need to prepare for those Messerschmitts to come back. They might not be satisfied until they see our corpses."

He pulled out his emergency knife with his good hand, wincing as his left arm screamed in protest. "Jenkins, start scavenging. See what supplies we can salvage from the plane. Water, first aid, anything that can help. Colonel, keep an eye on the horizon. We don't want any surprises."

Jenkins nodded, his fatigue temporarily forgotten in the face of their new, immediate challenge. He disappeared back into the shattered fuselage. Colonel Harding, briefcase still clutched in his hand, stood watch, his gaze sweeping the empty expanse of sky and sand.

Riley knelt beside Peterson, his left arm throbbing. "How bad is it, Sergeant?"

"Busted clean, I think, sir," Peterson gasped, trying to keep a brave face. "But I can move. I can crawl."

"Good man," Riley said, his voice softening slightly. "We'll get you patched up as best we can. Davis, can you hear me?"

Davis moaned faintly, his eyes fluttering open. "Riley...? What happened?"

"We crashed," Riley explained, his voice gentle. "We're on the ground. You took a knock to the head. Just rest. We'll be alright." He looked around again, feeling the immense weight of their predicament. They were deep in enemy territory, exposed, vulnerable, and two men down. The "General" might be safe, but their own lives were hanging by a thread.

Jenkins emerged from the wreckage, dusty and grim-faced, carrying a couple of canteens, a first-aid kit, and a small canvas bag. "Got some water, Riley. And the medical kit. But the emergency rations are mostly crushed. And the radio, obviously, is toast."

"That's something," Riley said, taking a canteen and offering it to Peterson. He then took the first aid kit. He knew his arm needed attention, but he also knew Peterson's leg was a more immediate concern. "Help me with Peterson's leg, Jenkins. Then we'll deal with my arm and Davis."

As they worked on Peterson's leg, fashioning a makeshift splint from a piece of shattered fuselage skin and bandages, the sun began its slow descent towards the western horizon. The desert air, which had been scorching hot, now began to cool rapidly. The vast emptiness around them seemed to grow, and the silence, now that the roar of engines and gunfire had ceased, was oppressive.

"We need shelter," Colonel Harding stated, turning from his vigil. "Night falls fast out here, and it gets cold."

Riley looked at the distant ridge, now bathed in the orange glow of the setting sun. "That ridge is still our best bet, sir. It might offer some cover from the wind, and perhaps even some caves or overhangs. But we can't move Peterson and Davis far in their condition, not tonight."

"Then we make do here," the colonel decided, his eyes scanning the immediate surroundings. "Can we use any more of the wreckage?"

"We could try to fashion some kind of lean-to from a wing section," Jenkins suggested, looking at the twisted aluminum. "It'll be slow going, and noisy."

"Noise is a risk, but staying exposed is a bigger one," Riley said. "Let's see what we can manage. Jenkins, take the lead. You've got two good arms. I'll supervise and try to keep an eye out."

They worked quickly, their movements hampered by exhaustion, injuries, and the fading light. Using the limited tools they had, they managed to pry off a relatively intact section of the bomber's fuselage skin. It wasn't much, but propped up against a larger piece of debris, it offered a flimsy shield against the biting desert wind.

As darkness fell completely, casting long, eerie shadows across the wrecked plane, they huddled together, the four survivors, a small island of humanity in a sea of desolation. The cold was immediate and severe. Riley shivered, despite his adrenaline, feeling the sharp ache in his arm. He ripped a strip of cloth from his shirt and tied it tightly around his upper arm, hoping to slow the swelling.

Colonel Harding, ever the pragmatist, had opened his briefcase again. He pulled out one of the sealed packages, not the duplicates, but one of the original "decoy" orders. He held it up, a strange expression on his face. "This piece of paper, Riley," he said, his voice quiet in the vast stillness of the night, "caused all this. It saved the General, but it cost us two men, and nearly cost us all."

Riley nodded, staring into the flickering glow of a small fire they had managed to start using some oil-soaked rags and a spark from a damaged battery terminal. The fire cast dancing shadows, momentarily pushing back the encroaching darkness and cold. "It's a bitter victory, sir."

"All victories are, in their own way," the colonel replied, sighing. He reached into his briefcase again, this time pulling out a flask. "Whiskey. Emergency rations, you might say." He took a long swig, then offered it to Riley. "Helps with the cold. And the pain."

Riley hesitated, then took a grateful swig. The fiery liquid burned its way down his throat, providing a momentary warmth that spread through his chilled body. He passed it to Jenkins, then Peterson, who managed a weak smile. Davis was too dazed to take it.

"We need to plan, Riley," the colonel continued, once the flask had made its rounds. "We're here. The enemy thinks the General is dead, or at least out of play. That's good. But we're stranded. How far are we from Tobruk?"

Davis, surprisingly, stirred. "Roughly... one hundred and eighty miles, sir," he mumbled, his voice slurred. "North-northeast."

"One hundred and eighty miles," Riley repeated, the words hanging heavy in the air. "Across the desert. With injured men, and no transport. And hostile air patrols above us."

"We move at first light," Colonel Harding declared, his voice resolute. "We make for Tobruk. We cannot stay here. If those Messerschmitts report a crash, they will send ground patrols to confirm. We cannot be found."

"We can't carry Peterson and Davis that far, sir," Jenkins interjected, voicing Riley's own thoughts. "Not with Peterson's leg. He'll slow us down, and Davis isn't fit to walk."

The colonel's gaze was hard. "Then we make a stretcher for Peterson. And we help Davis. We simply must. There is no other option. We cannot be captured. Not with what I carry." He patted his briefcase. "Not with what we know."

The jeep passed through a heavy, reinforced gate, manned by armed guards who scrutinized the vehicle before waving it through. Riley felt a palpable sense of dread. This was it. His new reality.

A single, bare bulb illuminated a heavy wooden door. This was clearly an interrogation or holding facility, not a standard POW camp.

The new guard merely grunted. He motioned Riley forward. The two guards who had accompanied him in the jeep turned and left, their mission accomplished. Riley was now alone with the granite faced guard.

His face was devoid of any warmth, a mask of cold professionalism.

Riley remained silent, his eyes fixed on Kessler, trying to read the man, to understand his tactics. This was the true beginning of his ordeal.

Riley felt a fresh wave of shock and anger. They truly knew everything. This was not a bluff. His privacy, his very identity, had been stripped away. He tried to suppress the tremor that ran through him.

Serial number. He would repeat it, over and over again, until it was etched into his very soul. He would not give them anything. He would not betray his country, his squadron, or his comrades. He would not betray Martha.

The fight for survival, indeed, had only just begun. But it was no longer a battle against the elements or enemy aircraft. It was a battle against himself, against his own pain, his own fears, his own humanity. It was a lonely war, fought within the confines of these cold, grey walls, under the unblinking eye of a merciless enemy. His life, for now, belonged to them. what fate awaited him?

The ersatz coffee had provided a fleeting warmth, a small illusion of comfort, but it had done little to quell the deeper chill that permeated his bones, a cold that spoke of damp steel and the vast, unforgiving ocean. The meager sustenance, while momentarily silencing the clamor of his stomach, had merely sharpened his other senses, bringing into stark relief the throbbing ache in his shoulder and the relentless, low thrum of the vessel around him. It was a submarine, he was almost certain now. The constant, subtle pitch and roll, the rhythmic hum of machinery, the feeling of being enclosed deep beneath the surface, all pointed to it. The irony, as he had dimly perceived before succumbing to darkness, was truly cruel. He had survived the sea, only to be swallowed by it in a different, more insidious way.

The occasional clang of metal, the echoing shouts of men. He imagined a bustling dock, heavily guarded, impossible to penetrate. His heart sank. He was injured, weak, disoriented, and utterly alone in enemy territory. Escape, here, now, was a pipe dream.

But he had to survive. He had to get information. He had to get back. His duty was not over. His mission might have failed, but his war was far from it. He closed his eyes, focusing on the pain in his shoulder, using it as an anchor, a reminder of his physical reality. He would heal, as best he could. He would observe. He would listen. He would wait. Patience was a virtue, especially for a prisoner. He would gather every scrap of information, every detail, no matter how insignificant. He would not break. He would endure. For Eleanor. For Arthur. For Miller, Davies, Thompson, and Stevens. He would remember them. He would remember why he fought. He would live to fight another day.
