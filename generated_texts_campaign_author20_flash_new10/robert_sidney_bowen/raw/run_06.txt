The asset they were pursuing must have been more critical than he had initially understood. Or perhaps, it was simply a message. A brutal, decisive message delivered with a bone shattering impact.

A faint groan escaped Falcon’s lips. Vance leaned in closer, hope flaring. “Falcon? Can you hear me?” he whispered. No response. The sound had been involuntary, a reflex of pain from the depths of his unconsciousness. Vance sighed, a gust of cold air leaving his lungs. He continued his vigil, a silent, unmoving guardian in the bleak, star lit night.

The hours crawled by, each one an eternity. His mind drifted, replaying fragments of the mission, the hurried briefing, the pursuit, the sudden, devastating attack. He saw Kestrel falling, her cry swallowed by the wind. He saw Falcon, a blur of motion, trying to cover them, then the impossible impact, the sickening crunch of bone against rock. He blamed himself. He was the Chief. He should have anticipated. He should have been faster. Stronger.

No. Dwelling on blame would not help. It would only erode his resolve. He pushed the thoughts away, focusing on the present. On survival. On his team. He felt a profound, almost primal, bond with them. They were his responsibility. His family in the field. He would not fail them.

Just before the first faint hint of pre dawn gray appeared on the horizon, Kestrel stirred again. Her eyes fluttered open, a glint of pain and confusion in their depths. “Chief… it’s… cold,” she whispered, her voice hoarse, barely audible.

“I know, Kestrel. Try to stay still. We’ll get warm soon,” Vance replied, his own voice raspy from disuse and the dry air. He reached out, gently touching her forehead. Her skin was clammy, despite the cold.

“Falcon…?” she managed, her gaze drifting towards the unconscious agent.

“Still out. But he’s breathing. We’re going to get him through this,” Vance said, projecting an optimism he didn’t entirely feel. He needed her to believe. He needed her to fight.

“My leg…” She tried to shift, a sharp intake of breath.

“Don’t move it, Kestrel. It’s badly broken. We need daylight to deal with it properly. Just hold on a little longer. The sun will be up soon.”

She nodded weakly, her eyes closing again. Vance watched her, a knot of fear tightening in his stomach. Infection. Blood loss. Shock. These were their silent enemies, as dangerous as any hostile.

Finally, the first sliver of orange light began to paint the eastern sky. It was a slow, agonizing dawn, but it brought with it a fragile sense of hope. Vance rose, wincing as his shoulder screamed in protest. He had to be careful. One wrong move and he could render himself useless.

He scanned the landscape in the growing light. The plateau was truly isolated. No signs of roads, no distant lights, no smoke. Just endless, rugged terrain, rock formations stretching into the hazy distance. He needed water. Desperately. And he needed to signal for help.

He looked down the slope where their packs had fallen. Now, in the light, he could see them, scattered among the rocks, a few hundred feet below. It was a treacherous descent, but not impossible. He would have to go. He needed the medical kit. He needed whatever food or water they might have carried.

He turned to Kestrel. “I need to go down to retrieve our packs, Kestrel. There might be a medical kit, some water. Can you keep an eye on Falcon?”

She opened her eyes, her gaze clearer now, though still clouded with pain. “I… I’ll try, Chief. Just… be careful.”

“I will.” He gave her a reassuring nod, then turned, beginning the careful, deliberate descent. Each step was a calculated risk. His dislocated shoulder was a constant, searing pain, and his ribs ached with every breath. He used his good arm to brace himself against the rocks, digging his boots into the loose scree, testing every foothold before committing his weight.

The descent was slower than the ascent with Kestrel, but no less harrowing. He could feel sweat prickling on his forehead despite the chill. His vision blurred occasionally from the pain, but he forced himself to focus, to compartmentalize. He was an agent. He was trained for this. He would not break.

He finally reached the scattered remains of their gear. Two packs were relatively intact. Falcon’s and Kestrel’s. His own was nowhere to be seen, likely having tumbled further down the ravine. He quickly began to rummage through them. Kestrel’s pack yielded a small, compact medical kit, a few energy bars, and a half empty water bottle. Falcon’s pack contained more rations, another water bottle, and a small emergency signal mirror. A vital discovery.

He also found a small, foldable tactical shovel in Falcon’s pack. Not ideal, but it could serve multiple purposes. He checked the side pockets of the packs. No comms units. All destroyed or lost.

He secured the retrieved items to his belt and good shoulder, distributing the weight as best he could. Now for the climb back up. This would be the true test. He looked up at the plateau, so close, yet so impossibly far.

The ascent was a brutal, agonizing climb. He dragged himself upwards, hand over hand, foot over foot, using every ounce of strength and willpower he possessed. His muscles screamed, his lungs burned, and the pain from his shoulder was a constant, white hot presence. He paused frequently, leaning against the rock face, gulping air, trying to regain his composure. He imagined Falcon’s shallow breaths, Kestrel’s pained whimper. He imagined the hostile, somewhere out there, perhaps waiting for them to die. This fueled him. He had to make it. For them.

After what felt like an eternity, he hauled himself over the lip of the plateau, collapsing onto the rock, gasping for breath. He lay there for a moment, utterly spent, his body trembling uncontrollably.

Kestrel, her eyes open, watched him. “Chief… you made it,” she whispered, a faint tremor in her voice.

He pushed himself up, trying to project a sense of strength. “Barely,” he admitted, managing a weak smile. He crawled over to them, emptying the contents of the packs.

“Water,” he said, unscrewing the cap of a bottle. He held it to Kestrel’s lips, letting her take small sips. Then he moved to Falcon, gently moistening his lips, hoping some of it would penetrate. He took a few sips himself, the cool liquid a blessing to his parched throat.

Next, the medical kit. It was basic, but it was something. Antiseptic wipes, gauze, medical tape, a few pain pills, and a small roll of flexible splint material. He turned to Kestrel’s leg.

“This is going to hurt, Kestrel,” he warned her gently. “But we need to stabilize it. Try to focus on something else.”

She nodded, biting her lip, her eyes squeezed shut. Vance took a deep breath, steeling himself. He carefully, meticulously, began to manipulate the broken bones. He felt the sickening grind of bone, heard Kestrel’s sharp cry of pain, a choked sound she tried to suppress. He worked quickly, using the splint material to immobilize the leg as best he could, securing it with tape. It wasn’t perfect, but it would prevent further injury.

He then turned his attention to Falcon. He cleaned the head wound as best he could with the antiseptic wipes, then applied fresh gauze and tape. He also examined Falcon’s broken arm, using more of the splint material to create a rudimentary brace, securing it to his body with torn strips of fabric. He pressed a few of the pain pills into Kestrel’s hand. “Take these. They might help dull the edge.”

He had to address his own shoulder. He couldn’t effectively lead, or fight, with it dislocated. He knew the procedure. It was agonizing, but necessary. He stood, positioning his arm against a sturdy rock. He took a deep breath, exhaling slowly, then, with a grunt of pain, he slammed his shoulder against the rock, simultaneously twisting his arm. A searing, blinding pain shot through him, and he almost blacked out. But then, a sickening pop. He staggered, clutching his shoulder, sweat pouring down his face. It was back in. He cautiously moved it, the pain still intense, but now a dull ache instead of a sharp, crippling agony. He had some mobility back, though it would be weak and incredibly painful.

He slumped back down, utterly drained. He had done what he could with their immediate injuries. Now, for the signal. He took out the small mirror. The sun was rising higher, but the light was still weak. He would have to wait for brighter, direct sunlight.

He took out the energy bars. “Eat these,” he instructed Kestrel, handing her one. He ate one himself, forcing the dry, tasteless substance down. He left the remaining rations for Falcon, hoping he would regain consciousness soon.

His mind turned to the hostile. He scanned the surrounding ridges, the canyons below. Was he still here? Observing? Or had he moved on, confident in their demise? Vance had to assume the worst. He kept his sidearm close, loaded, ready. He placed Kestrel’s weapon within her reach, though he knew she was in no condition to use it. Falcon’s remained near him.

He had to find a better vantage point, to get a sense of their surroundings. But he also couldn’t leave Kestrel and Falcon for too long. He needed to be able to signal. The dilemma gnawed at him.

He decided on a compromise. He would climb the highest point of their small plateau, just a few feet higher, to get a better view, while still keeping an eye on his team. He slowly, painfully, pulled himself up. From this slightly elevated position, he could see more of the landscape. It was endless. Jagged peaks, deep canyons, a desolate, unforgiving wilderness. No sign of human life. No roads, no buildings, nothing but the raw, untamed earth.

He felt a pang of despair, quickly suppressing it. Despair was a luxury he couldn’t afford. He was the Chief. He had to find a way.

He looked for natural indicators. A distant change in vegetation, suggesting a water source. A potential path, however faint. Nothing. The landscape was a uniform tapestry of rock and sparse, hardy plants.

He brought out the signal mirror again. The sun was now high enough. He began to flash it, sweeping it across the sky, hoping for a reflection, a glint that might catch the eye of a distant aircraft, however unlikely. He repeated the motion, again and again, until his arm ached and his vision blurred. He kept it up for an hour, then two, his hope dwindling with each empty sweep of the sky.

He returned to Kestrel and Falcon, his energy reserves completely depleted. “Nothing,” he rasped, his voice heavy with defeat.

Kestrel, despite her pain, managed a small, brave smile. “Keep trying, Chief. Someone will come for us.”

He sat beside them, his hand resting on Falcon’s still form. He felt a fierce protectiveness. He would not let them die here. He would not.

He needed to establish a more permanent camp, however rudimentary. He began to gather what few dry branches and dead scrub he could find on the plateau. He worked slowly, painstakingly, using his good arm, his injured shoulder protesting with every movement. He built a small pile, hoping to light a signal fire if he could find something to ignite it with. He didn't have matches, or a lighter. He would have to rely on friction, or the faint hope of finding a flint.

He searched the packs again, more thoroughly this time. Nothing useful for fire starting. He cursed softly. This was a critical oversight. He looked around for flint. The rocks were mostly granite and shale. No obvious flint. He would try rubbing two rocks together, but he knew the chances were slim.

The day wore on. The sun, once a source of hope, became a tormentor, beating down on them, baking the exposed rock. Thirst began to set in again, despite the meager sips of water. Vance rationed it carefully. A few sips every hour for Kestrel, a drop on Falcon’s lips. Less for himself. He could endure more.

He had to explore. He had to find water. He couldn't wait for rescue if they died of dehydration first. He made a difficult decision.

“Kestrel, I have to go look for water. I can’t leave you two alone for long. I’ll stay within sight of the plateau, but I need to go down into the ravine.”

Her eyes widened slightly, a flicker of fear. “Chief… what about… him?”

“I’ll be careful. I’ll be back as soon as I can. Keep the weapon close. If anything happens, try to make noise, anything.” He handed her his sidearm, placing it carefully within her reach. He kept Falcon’s weapon. He couldn’t afford to be unarmed.

He gave Falcon one last check, pressing his ear to his chest again. Still breathing, still a faint pulse. He looked at Kestrel, his gaze firm. “Hold tight. We’ll get through this.”

He began his descent into the ravine, a harrowing journey into the unknown. The sun was high overhead, casting harsh shadows that distorted the landscape. The air was still and heavy, the only sound the crunch of his boots on the loose rocks and the occasional whimper of pain from his own body. He moved with extreme caution, constantly scanning his surroundings, his senses on high alert for any sign of movement, any hint of the hostile.

The ravine floor was a jumble of massive boulders, fallen trees, and sharp, jagged rocks. It was a maze, a potential death trap. He kept to the shadows where he could, using the terrain for cover. He moved slowly, methodically, his dislocated shoulder protesting with every jostle, his ribs aching with every breath.

He searched for any sign of water: a damp patch of soil, a trickling sound, a change in vegetation that might indicate a hidden spring. The ravine was bone dry. The sun had parched everything. He moved deeper, hope fading with each step.

After what felt like hours, he heard a sound. A faint, distant gurgle. He froze, his hand instinctively going to the weapon at his side. He listened, straining his ears. The sound came again, clearer this time. Water.

He moved towards it, his pace quickening despite his pain, a renewed surge of hope coursing through him. He found a narrow fissure in the rock, and from it, a tiny trickle of water emerged, collecting in a small, moss-lined pool at the base. It was barely enough to fill a teacup, but it was water. Pure, life-sustaining water.

He knelt, cupping his hands, letting the cool liquid wash over his parched lips. He drank sparingly, knowing he had to save most of it for Kestrel and Falcon. He took out one of the empty water bottles and began the slow, agonizing process of filling it, drop by precious drop. It took a long time, but eventually, he had a full bottle of water.

He also noticed something else near the water source. A faint trail, almost invisible, leading further into the ravine. It looked like a game trail, but there was something about it that made him pause. A broken branch, snapped too cleanly by an animal. A faint imprint in the dust that wasn't a hoof. Human. The hostile.

His heart pounded. He was close. Too close. He considered pursuing, confronting him. But then he remembered Kestrel and Falcon, alone and vulnerable on the plateau. He couldn’t abandon them. Not now.

He marked the location of the water source in his mind, committing every detail to memory. He would bring them here, if he could. But first, he had to get back. He had to protect them.

He turned, beginning the long, arduous climb back out of the ravine, carrying the precious water. The trail of the hostile was a chilling reminder of the danger that still lurked, a silent, unseen threat. He had a weapon, but he was hobbled. He was no match for a fully functional, prepared operative.

The ascent was even harder than the descent. His body was screaming for rest, for relief, but he pushed through it, fueled by the water and the urgent need to protect his team. He pictured their pale faces, their helpless forms. He would not let them down.

He finally emerged from the ravine, gasping for breath, his muscles trembling. Kestrel was still awake, watching him with wide, anxious eyes. “Chief! You’re back!” she exclaimed, relief flooding her voice.

He stumbled towards them, collapsing beside her, holding out the water bottle. “Water,” he croaked, his throat raw.

She took it, her hands shaking, and drank deeply, then passed it to him. He took a few sips, then poured a few drops onto Falcon’s lips again.

“I found a source,” Vance said, his voice stronger now. “But… I also found signs of the hostile. He’s been in the ravine. He’s still out there.”

Kestrel’s face paled. “What do we do?”

“We get ready,” Vance said, his voice grim. He looked around the small plateau. It offered little in the way of defense. They were exposed. He had to make it work. He had to make this desolate place their fortress. He began to gather more rocks, creating a low wall around them, a pitiful barricade against the unseen enemy. It was more for morale than actual defense, but it was something. It was an act of defiance.

The sun began its slow descent, painting the western sky in hues of orange and purple. Another day was ending, and they were still here. Still stranded. Still in danger. But they were alive. They had water. They had a plan, however tenuous.

Vance watched the horizon, his gaze hard, resolute. He was the Chief. He would lead. He would protect. And when the time came, he would hunt the hostile. The score was not settled. Not by a long shot. The fight had only just begun. And he would see it through, whatever the cost.
