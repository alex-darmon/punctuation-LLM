Julian Thorne, with a sigh that stirred the dust motes in the single shaft of moonlight, drew the rusted iron grille shut, its groan echoing briefly in the cavernous space before a heavy padlock clicked into place. He turned, the faint illumination of his electric lantern catching the pallor of his face, and found Elara watching him, her eyes wide with a question she dared not voice. "They will search the upper levels for a time, then," he stated, rather than asked, "and by the time they consider the forgotten service tunnels beneath the old railway yard, we shall be far from here. But for now, we have a moment's respite, a breathing space before the final push." He gestured towards a stack of forgotten crates. "Rest, if you can, my dear; the night is young, and our journey has barely begun."

She sank onto a crate, drawing her cloak tighter, the dark fabric almost entirely concealing her. "And the others?" she inquired, her voice a soft murmur in the vast silence. "Your former associates; will they not send a larger contingent, once they discover the theft?" Julian gave a grim chuckle, a sound devoid of mirth. "They will, undoubtedly, and with a vengeance such as only those who have been deeply betrayed can muster. But they are men of habit, creatures of the established order; they will follow the most obvious trails, search the most frequented haunts. This forgotten undercroft, this labyrinth of disused passages beneath the city's skin, is precisely what they will overlook, at least until morning light exposes their failure to their superiors." He paced a short distance, his boots crunching on loose gravel. "It is a precarious game we play, Elara, a wager on their predictability; yet it is the only hand I hold."

He stopped, turning to face her fully, his expression softening somewhat. "I have, I fear, dragged you into a predicament of my own making, an entanglement born of foolish ambition and a tardy awakening. But for my prior blindness, this moment would find you safe, perhaps at your quiet apartment, undisturbed by the machinations of men such as I once was. Therefore, we must establish a rendezvous, should the inevitable occur and we find ourselves separated in the chaos that awaits. The police, undoubtedly alerted by now, will cordon off the main thoroughfares; the river crossings will be watched; the railway stations patrolled. We cannot know what precise net they have cast until we emerge into the public gaze." He paused, considering. "Hotels and public houses are out of the question; we have little enough for our needs, and they will be observed. The foreign consulates offer no refuge at such an hour. There remain, unless you can propose a better alternative, only the venerable monuments, the places where a solitary figure might appear to be merely a tourist or a worshiper, rather than a fugitive."

"The old clock tower in St. James's Square," she suggested softly, her gaze fixed on his. "It is always accessible, its shadows deep, and its isolation at this hour would be a virtue."

"An excellent choice," he agreed, relief evident in his voice. "If we are separated, make your way there, and wait. I shall come for you." He reached out, his gloved hand finding hers, and she did not withdraw it. "But let us hope, my dear, that such a necessity does not arise."

With a renewed sense of purpose, they stole through the echoing tunnels, their footsteps barely disturbing the pervasive silence, until they emerged into the humid, fog-shrouded streets of the old district. Here, amidst the spectral forms of derelict warehouses and gaslit alleyways, they encountered few souls; those they did see, huddled against the raw damp, gave them no more than a passing glance, mistaking them, perhaps, for a late-night stroll by some eccentric scholar and his companion. Julian, in his dark coat and soft cap, strode rapidly, his head bent against the chill, while Elara, her hood drawn forward, clung to his arm, her presence a silent anchor. They navigated a circuitous route, avoiding the more prominent avenues, until they reached a dimly lit tram stop on a narrow street that ran parallel to the river.

From there, a rattling journey carried them several miles across the city, depositing them at last in a quiet, forgotten corner near the old market, where a modest eating house, its windows still faintly glowing, promised warmth and anonymity. Within moments, they were ensconced in a secluded booth, a haven from the biting wind and the ever-present threat. They partook of a simple but hearty meal: stewed beef, dark bread, and a robust ale, each mouthful a small victory. Julian, his previously taut features relaxing, found himself filled with a profound, almost intoxicating sense of well-being. The successful evasion, the taste of freedom, and, most potently, the steady, comforting presence of Elara beside him, had lifted his spirits to an unprecedented height. He was young, he was free, he was in love, he was—in short—supremely, gloriously alive. The shared danger, the silent understanding that passed between them, wove an invisible thread of intimacy, binding their souls.

He spoke, then, with an unburdening candor he had never before permitted himself, of the clandestine dealings of the 'Syndicate,' of the men who had once been his comrades and then his adversaries; of the cunning required to survive within their ranks, and the wearying toll it had taken upon his conscience. He spoke of the resolution that had, but a few weeks prior, gripped him with an almost physical force, driving him to sever ties, to embark upon this perilous flight toward an honest future. He spoke, not with bravado, but with the quiet determination of one who has glimpsed a dawn he thought forever lost. Elara, her chin resting upon her interlaced fingers, listened with an unwavering intensity, her gaze never leaving his face. She offered little of her own past, yet her attentive silence, punctuated by the occasional soft sigh, seemed to fill in the unspoken details, confirming his intuitive sense of a gentle, perhaps difficult, history.

"I fear I am monopolizing the conversation," he surmised at length, a fleeting blush rising to his cheeks, "my tongue, unused to such liberty, runs away with me."

She offered a small, knowing smile. "No, not at all. But I confess, Julian, I wonder at the absolute certainty with which you speak of this new path. You relate tales of a life steeped in shadows, of habits deeply ingrained; and yet you speak as though a single act of will, a sudden resolve, can entirely erase the past. I do not doubt your sincerity now; but the road ahead will be arduous, fraught with temptation and hardship. What then? When the novelty of freedom has worn thin, and the true cost of your decision begins to bite, will your resolve remain as steadfast?"

"If I fail," he replied, his voice firm, "then I am unworthy, and I shall accept my fate. But I shall not fail. This desire to live cleanly, to stand free of deceit, it is, I believe, a testament to some enduring spark of decency within me, a foundation upon which a new life can be built."

"I hope that is true," she said, her voice barely a whisper. She lowered her head, tracing an invisible pattern upon the rough wooden table. "You have, perhaps, intimated that my presence, my very being, has been a catalyst in this awakening. Suppose, Julian, suppose I were not what you imagine me to be? Suppose your faith in me were misplaced?"

"That is an impossibility," he declared promptly, his brow furrowed in protest.

She looked up then, her eyes holding a poignant, almost sorrowful wisdom. "But if it were? If some unforeseen revelation were to shatter your perception of me?"

"Then," he said slowly, deliberately, "I think I should feel as though the very ground beneath my feet had dissolved, leaving me suspended over an endless void."

"And you would revert to your former ways?" she pressed gently.

"How can I answer such a question?" he expostulated, a hint of desperation in his tone. "It is unfair, Elara, deeply unfair. I know not what I would do, save that it would require a betrayal of the most damnable kind to shake my faith in you!"

"You believe that now," she observed, her voice tinged with a compassionate tolerance. "But if circumstances conspired against me, if appearances were overwhelmingly damning—"

"They would have to be black indeed!"

"If you discovered I had deliberately deceived you, Julian, from the very beginning?"

"Elara!" He reached across the table, his hand covering hers, his grip tight. "There is no use in this prevarication, this circling around the truth. You must know what is in my heart!"

She flinched, pulling her hand away, her eyes wide with sudden apprehension, and a sharp, almost inarticulate protest escaped her lips: "No!"

"But you must listen to me," he insisted, his voice raw with emotion. "I want you to understand. My old mentor, the Professor, used to say to me: 'The man who lets love into his life opens a door no mortal hand can close, and God alone knows what will follow in its wake!' And he was right. That door, Elara, has been thrown wide open in my heart, and I believe, with every fiber of my being, that what follows in will be neither evil nor degrading. I have tried, with clumsy indirections, to convey this sentiment, but I may as well speak plainly now: I love you. It is the love of you, the hope that one day, when I have proven myself worthy, you might permit me to ask you for your hand, that compels me to seek this honest path. Perhaps, even now, you are in love with a man more deserving than I; I am prepared to risk that truth, for a year can bring many changes. Perhaps there is some doubt in your mind concerning my strength, my constancy, a doubt I cannot fathom. Only the outcome can declare that. But please understand this one thing: if I fail to make good on my resolution, it will be no fault of yours; it will be because I am unfit, and have proven it to myself and to the world. And then, if you will, you may say no to the question I shall put to you, and I shall not resent it, nor will it utterly destroy me; for if a man—"

for if a man, having been granted the singular opportunity to truly mend his ways, chooses instead to revert to the darkness that once held him captive, then that failure is his alone, a testament not to the cruelty of fate, but to the inherent weakness of his own spirit. And in such a case, I would accept my ruin with a humility born of absolute self knowledge, for I would have proven myself unworthy not merely of your hand, but of the very air I breathe."

Elara stared at him, her eyes wide, unfocused, as if she were looking not at him but through him, to some distant, dimly lit corridor of her own past. The raw, unvarnished passion in his voice, the sheer weight of his self abnegation, had struck her with an unexpected force, leaving her momentarily breathless. She had anticipated an argument, a playful skirmish of words, perhaps even a renewed attempt at persuasion of the more conventional kind, but not this. Never this. This was a man laying bare his soul, offering it up for judgment, and in so doing, placing an unbearable burden upon her own, a burden she felt ill equipped to carry. Her earlier protest, a sharp, almost instinctive cry, now seemed a feeble thing, inadequate to the seismic shift that had just occurred between them.

He, perceiving her distress, her sudden withdrawal, leaned forward slightly, his own face a mask of earnest concern. "Elara, what is it? Have I presumed too much? Have I offended you with the crudity of my confession?" His voice was softer now, tinged with a fresh anxiety. "I know it is not the fashion for men to speak so plainly, but I have found myself, since knowing you, less and less inclined to conform to the dictates of mere fashion."

She shook her head slowly, her gaze still distant. "No, Julian. You have not offended me. It is simply that I… I had not anticipated such a complete surrender of your heart. It is a powerful thing, to witness, and for a woman who has learned caution at a considerable cost, it is, forgive me, rather terrifying." Her voice, though soft, held an underlying tremor, a hint of something fragile, something long suppressed, now threatening to surface. "To be the recipient of such a profound declaration, one made with such absolute conviction, carries with it an immense responsibility. And I confess, I am not certain I am equal to it."

He reached for her hand again, this time with a gentle hesitancy, awaiting her permission, and when she did not withdraw it, his fingers closed carefully over hers, a silent promise in their light pressure. "You speak of caution, Elara, and I understand it. I have, after all, given you every reason to be cautious, and precious few to be otherwise. My past is a testament to imprudence, to self indulgence, to a heedless disregard for the consequences of my actions. I do not deny it. I do not seek to diminish the folly of my former life, for to do so would be to diminish the very path I am now resolved to tread. But consider, if you will, that perhaps it is precisely because of that past, because of the depths from which I now seek to ascend, that my commitment to this new course is all the more unshakeable."

He paused, collecting his thoughts, his gaze intense, pleading. "A man who has always walked in the light may take its presence for granted, may stray without truly understanding the darkness beyond. But one who has stumbled, who has lost his way in the shadows, who has felt the chill of despair and the sting of regret, such a man, Elara, when he finally beholds the light, will cling to it with a ferocity that a more fortunate soul may never comprehend. You are that light, for me. You have shown me a path I did not even know existed, a path of honour, of purpose, of genuine human connection. How then, could I ever willingly turn away from it, knowing what awaits me if I do?"

Her hand, still clasped in his, felt cold, unresponsive, yet she did not pull away. Her eyes, however, had sharpened, and she met his gaze directly, a flicker of something akin to pain in their depths. "You speak with the eloquence of a man newly converted, Julian, and I do not doubt the sincerity of your words in this moment. But moments pass. Resolutions, however fervent, are tested by the relentless tide of life. It is easy to vow fidelity in the warm glow of conviction, but what of the long, cold nights when the conviction wavers, when old habits whisper persuasively, when the path of least resistance beckons with its insidious charm?" She sighed, a sound laden with a weariness that seemed far beyond her years. "My caution, you see, is not merely a matter of intellectual appraisal, Julian. It is a matter of self preservation. I have witnessed too often the transience of human affection, the fragility of promises made under duress of emotion. I have learned, perhaps too painfully, that a heart given too freely, too readily, can be a heart easily broken, and that the shards, once scattered, are extraordinarily difficult to gather again."

His grip tightened imperceptibly on her hand, a silent acknowledgment of her unspoken hurt. "You speak of scars, Elara. I have seen them, though you have veiled them well. And I would not ask you to cast aside your armour without offering you the most profound assurance that I intend no further injury. Indeed, I seek only to heal, to prove to you, through action and not mere declaration, that my love is not a fleeting fancy, but a bedrock upon which a life, a shared life, might be built. You have every right to protect your heart, and I would not demand its surrender without first earning it, piece by painstaking piece, over the course of a lifetime, if need be. But grant me this year, Elara. Grant me the opportunity to demonstrate that this change is not a mere mask, but the very essence of a man reborn."

"A year is a long time," she mused, her gaze dropping to their clasped hands, her thumb idly tracing the lines on his knuckles. "And yet, in the grand tapestry of a lifetime, it is but a fleeting moment. What would this year entail, Julian? What are the precise terms of this… this experiment, this trial you propose? For I confess, the very notion fills me with a strange mixture of apprehension and, dare I say it, a fragile hope." The admission seemed to cost her something, a faint flush rising on her cheeks.

"The terms," he began, his voice firming, now entering the realm of the practical, "would be entirely of your devising, Elara. I ask only for the permission to undertake this transformation with the knowledge that a future with you, however distant or improbable, remains a possibility. I shall return to my estate, far from the temptations of the city. I shall immerse myself in the management of my lands, a duty I have so shamefully neglected. I shall engage with the tenants, improve their lot, restore the dignity and prosperity of a place that has suffered from my indolence. I shall read, I shall study, I shall endeavour to become a man worthy of serious discourse, of profound thought, rather than merely a purveyor of glib wit and superficial charm. And I shall, with every passing day, reflect upon the lessons you have imparted, the values you embody, striving to make them my own."

He paused, releasing her hand to gesture with a sweeping, almost theatrical motion, though his eyes remained earnestly fixed on hers. "As for communication, that too, is for you to decide. Should you wish for no contact whatsoever, I shall abide by it, though the silence would be a torment I willingly embrace if it means preserving your peace of mind. Should you permit a single letter, perhaps, once a quarter, reporting on my progress, I would write it with the utmost care and truthfulness. Or if you prefer, I shall simply vanish for the year, and present myself, unannounced, on the designated day, having either succeeded or failed in my endeavour." His jaw was set, his resolve evident in every line of his face. "Whatever you decree, Elara, I shall obey without question, for my desire to prove myself worthy of your regard transcends any personal discomfort or inconvenience."

Elara withdrew her hand completely then, placing it on the table, palm down, as if grounding herself. She took a deep, shuddering breath, her eyes closed for a moment, and when they opened, they held a newfound clarity, though still shadowed by an enduring caution. "Very well, Julian. I will grant you this year." The words were spoken softly, yet they resonated with an almost solemn finality. "But understand this: my agreement comes not from a conviction that you will succeed, but from a belief that every soul deserves the opportunity for redemption, and from a curiosity, I confess, to witness if such a profound transformation is truly possible. There will be no letters. No clandestine meetings. No intermediaries. Let the year be a period of absolute separation. During this time, you will return to your estate, and I to my life. I will make no promises, offer no guarantees. My heart will remain my own, guarded as it has always been, until such time as you return, and then, and only then, will I consider what, if anything, has changed within me."

She leaned forward slightly, her voice dropping to an almost conspiratorial whisper, though the gravity of her tone remained undiminished. "And should you, in the course of this year, discover that the effort is too great, that the old ways still hold their sway, that the sacrifices required are more than you are willing to make, then I implore you, Julian, do not return. Do not seek to deceive me, or yourself. Let the silence itself be your report. For while I may mourn the loss of a nascent hope, I would rather face that truth than endure the bitter disappointment of a promise broken."

Julian met her gaze, his own eyes burning with a mixture of hope and a profound, almost reverent gratitude. "I understand, Elara. And I accept your terms, every one of them, without reservation. Indeed, they are precisely what I require, a crucible in which to forge a new self, untainted by expectation or premature judgment. This year will be for me, and for me alone, a testament to my own strength, or my ultimate weakness. I shall not burden you with my struggles, nor shall I seek to influence your thoughts from afar. When I return, I shall present myself as I am, and you, with your discerning eye, will judge."

A faint, almost imperceptible smile touched his lips, a ghost of his former charm, but now imbued with a genuine tenderness that softened the edges of his resolute expression. "And should I succeed, Elara, should I return a man transformed, a man worthy, truly worthy, of standing before you, then I shall ask my question, as I promised. And whether your answer is yes or no, I shall accept it with grace, knowing that I have at least tried, with all my heart, to earn what I now so desperately desire." He rose slowly, drawing himself to his full height, a figure of renewed purpose and quiet dignity. "I believe I have taken up enough of your valuable time tonight."

Elara, too, rose, her movements fluid and elegant, her expression still unreadable, a complex tapestry of wariness and a nascent, carefully contained admiration. "You have spoken your truth, Julian, and for that, I am grateful. It is a rare thing, in this world, to hear such honesty. I wish you well on your journey." Her words were formal, almost regal, yet beneath the surface, Julian detected a faint warmth, a concession to the shared intimacy of the past hour.

He bowed slightly, a gesture that for once seemed entirely unforced, entirely sincere. "And I, Elara, wish you peace and contentment in the year to come. My thoughts, though unspoken, shall often turn to you, a quiet anchor in the stormy waters of my self re invention." He turned to leave, then hesitated, looking back at her one last time. "Thank you, Elara. For everything."

With that, he walked out of the room, leaving her alone in the quiet intensity of the flickering candlelight. The heavy oak door closed behind him with a soft thud, a sound that seemed to reverberate through the silent room, marking the beginning of their separation.

Elara stood for a long moment in the aftermath, the scent of extinguished candles and Julian's faint cologne lingering in the air. Her mind, usually so meticulously ordered, was in a state of uncharacteristic disarray. His words, his raw honesty, his passionate declaration, still echoed in her ears, a resonant chord struck deep within her carefully guarded heart. She had expected to feel relief at his departure, a sense of having navigated a difficult conversation with her equanimity intact. She walked slowly to the window, pushing aside the heavy velvet curtains to gaze out at the moonlit street. The night was still, the city hushed, bathed in a silver glow that lent an almost ethereal quality to the familiar buildings. Julian was gone, embarked upon a quest for self redemption, a quest he had undertaken entirely for her, or so he claimed. The weight of that knowledge, the sheer magnitude of his faith in her transformative power, was both exhilarating and profoundly unsettling. She was accustomed to being admired, to being pursued, but never before had a man offered to dismantle his very being, to rebuild himself from the foundations up, simply for the prospect of her eventual regard.

Her mind replayed their conversation, dissecting each phrase, each nuance of his expression. His carriage awaited him at the outskirts of the city, a discreet vehicle he had arranged earlier in the day. He had chosen this path not out of despair, but out of a profound, nascent hope, a belief that beneath the layers of artifice and indolence, a better man resided, waiting to be unearthed.

As dawn began to paint the eastern sky with hues of rose and grey, the familiar contours of the countryside began to emerge. The estate, Blackwood Hall, had been a distant abstraction for years, managed by a capable but perhaps overly deferential steward, its affairs handled by correspondence and intermittent, perfunctory visits. Now, it was to be his crucible, his sanctuary, his proving ground.

The carriage finally turned onto the long, gravel drive leading to Blackwood Hall. The house, a venerable stone edifice that had stood for centuries, emerged from the morning mist. He oversaw the planting, the preparation of the soil, the endless tasks that ensured the next harvest. He initiated several improvements to the estate infrastructure: repairing bridges, draining a problematic bog, and even investing in new, more efficient farming implements after careful study and consultation with local experts. He found himself negotiating with merchants, overseeing carpenters, and discussing drainage systems with the practicality of a seasoned landowner. The abstract theories he had once read now manifested as tangible changes, improvements that benefited both the land and its people.

The year was passing, marked by these cycles, by the steady rhythm of work and learning. Julian’s self doubt, though never entirely absent, had receded significantly. His mind had sharpened considerably. The frivolous chatter of drawing rooms now seemed distant, almost alien. His thoughts were deeper, more nuanced, capable of grappling with complex problems and finding practical solutions. He debated agricultural policy with visiting neighbours, discussed philosophy with the local vicar, and found that his opinions, once shallow and derivative, now possessed substance, grounded in experience and genuine reflection. He no longer felt the need to impress, only to understand and to act with integrity.

The manor house, once a silent, echoing monument to his family’s past, now felt like a living entity, vibrant with purpose and careful stewardship. He had learned patience, resilience, and the quiet satisfaction that comes from meaningful labour. He had learned that true worth was not found in external validation, but in the integrity of one’s actions and the cultivation of one’s character. He had discovered a profound connection to the land, to the people who worked it, and to the generations who had preceded him. He was no longer merely Julian Blackwood, the dissolute London gentleman, but the steward of Blackwood Hall, a man grounded in the earth, responsible for its prosperity and the welfare of its inhabitants.

And Elara. Her image, once a distant lodestar, now felt like an intrinsic part of his very being. Every ledger balanced, every field improved, every tenant aided, had been a quiet offering to her, a testament to his unspoken promise. He had not sought her approval in his actions, for that would have diminished the purity of his endeavour. Instead, he had sought to become the man he believed she deserved, a man of substance, of character, of profound purpose. The thought of standing before her again, not with empty words but with the tangible evidence of his transformation, filled him with a quiet, resolute anticipation. The journey was not over, but a significant chapter had reached its conclusion, and a new beginning, one filled with the promise of genuine connection and enduring purpose, lay just ahead. He felt a quiet joy, a deep, abiding contentment that transcended any fleeting pleasure he had ever known. He was, finally, truly alive.

The air was crisp, carrying the scent of damp earth and distant woodsmoke, a fragrance utterly alien to the London he had left behind. He closed his eyes for a moment, letting the sensory details of Blackwood Hall steep into his very being, a final, anchoring sensation before he severed this temporary solitude. He had spoken the words to himself. He was ready. This readiness was not a fleeting impulse, but a deep seated conviction, forged in the crucible of a year’s tireless effort and unwavering introspection.

The next morning dawned with a pale, resolute sun, mirroring Julian’s own renewed determination. He had already given instructions to Mr. Davies, his steadfast estate manager, for the continued oversight of Blackwood Hall. Davies, a man of few words but immense capability, had simply nodded, his eyes conveying a silent understanding of the magnitude of Julian’s journey and his impending departure. The estate accounts were in impeccable order, the harvest safely stored, the winter repairs well underway. Julian had left no stone unturned, no detail unattended. He had even penned letters to the tenants, reassuring them of the continuity of their welfare and his swift return. This was not merely a trip; it was a mission, one that required the absolute assurance that his responsibilities were in capable hands.

He packed a single, modest trunk. Gone were the elaborate wardrobes, the unnecessary finery. The journey south, towards London, was a deliberate passage through the landscape of his past and his future. As the horses trotted steadily, carrying him further from the green, rolling hills of his estate, Julian looked out the window, observing the gradual changes in the scenery. The fields, neatly ploughed and awaiting the spring, gave way to more fragmented farms, then to scattered hamlets, and finally, as he neared the capital, to the burgeoning outskirts of brick and smoke. He felt a detached curiosity, a quiet observation that was a stark contrast to his former self, who would have been either oblivious or disdainfully critical.

His memories of Elara from that period, however, were different. They were tinged with a delicate ache, a recognition of his blindness, his inability to see her true worth beyond her beauty, his failure to truly listen to the unspoken depths of her character. He had been too engrossed in his own self indulgence, too preoccupied with the external show, to appreciate the quiet intelligence, the unwavering integrity, the gentle kindness that had always resided within her.

He had arranged lodgings not in his old, ostentatious town house, which remained shuttered and overseen by caretakers, but in a discreet, respectable hotel in a quieter Mayfair street. It was comfortable, clean, and offered anonymity, which was precisely what he desired. He wished for no grand entrance, no public spectacle. His confrontation with Elara would be a private one, between them alone.

The first order of business upon settling into his rooms was to dispatch a letter to Elara. He chose his words with meticulous care, each phrase weighed and considered. He did not beg or flatter. He did not offer excuses or dramatic declarations. Instead, he wrote simply and directly, outlining his return to London and his earnest desire for an audience with her.

"My dearest Elara," he began, his hand steady, "I have returned to London after a year spent in profound contemplation and diligent effort at Blackwood Hall. He sealed the letter with his personal crest, a symbol of his lineage, but now, he hoped, also a symbol of his renewed integrity. He spent the intervening hours pacing his room, reading, attempting to distract himself, but his thoughts invariably returned to Elara, to her possible reactions, to the myriad ways this encounter could unfold. He was no longer the arrogant youth who presumed affection; he was a man who understood the profound value of genuine connection and the painful cost of its loss.

There was no warmth, no hint of softness in her words, only a formal, almost cold, acceptance. Yet, Julian felt a surge of relief. She had not refused him outright. She was willing to see him. That alone was a victory. The formality of her tone, the explicit mention of "courtesy" rather than affection, was entirely expected. He had earned her skepticism, and he would not shy from it. He would face it with honesty and humility.
