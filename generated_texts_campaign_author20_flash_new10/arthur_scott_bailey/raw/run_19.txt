XIII THE SHINY STONE

Pip Squeakmouse was scurrying along the edge of Farmer McGregor’s potato patch one sunny morning when his eye caught a glint. There, half-buried in the damp soil, lay something small and perfectly round, something that sparkled most delightfully in the sunshine. Pip pushed away the loose earth with his nose and carefully nudged the object out into the open. It was a button, a rather large, brass button, with a tiny, almost invisible, pattern etched upon its surface. But to Pip, who had never seen anything quite so bright, it was a magnificent treasure.

He hugged it close to his chest and scampered off, eager to show his wonderful discovery to someone. The first creature he met was Barnaby Badger, who was digging industriously for grubs near the old oak tree.

"Look, Barnaby, look!" Pip squeaked, holding up his find with both paws. "Isn't it the most beautiful thing you've ever seen?"

Barnaby Badger paused his digging and squinted at the shiny object. "A button," he grunted, wiping mud from his snout. "Just a common, brass button, Pip. Fell off some farmer's coat, no doubt. Not worth a single nibble of corn."

Pip’s tiny whiskers drooped. "Not worth anything?" he asked, his voice barely a whisper. "But it shines so brightly!"

"Shines, does it?" Barnaby replied, returning to his grubs. "Lots of things shine, Pip. Doesn't make them valuable. Now, if you'd found a plump beetle, that would be something worth getting excited about."

Disappointed, Pip clutched his button and slowly made his way home. He still thought it was beautiful, even if Barnaby Badger didn't. He spent the rest of the morning polishing it with a soft moss pad until it gleamed like a tiny sun.

Later that afternoon, Rosie Robin, a bird known for her swift wings and even swifter tongue, spotted Pip admiring his button by the burrow entrance. "What have you there, Pip?" she chirped, landing on a nearby twig.

"It's my treasure!" Pip declared proudly, holding up the gleaming brass. "Barnaby Badger says it's just a button, but I think it's very special."

Rosie tilted her head, her bright eyes examining the object. "A button, you say?" she repeated. "But it looks so grand! Why, it must be a special button, a valuable one indeed, if it shines like that. Perhaps it's from Farmer McGregor's _best_ coat, the one he wears to market!" And without another word, Rosie Robin zipped away, her head already full of exciting news.

By the time Rosie reached Oliver Owl, she had embellished the tale slightly. "Pip Squeakmouse has found a golden button!" she announced breathlessly. "A heavy, golden button from Farmer McGregor's market coat! It was lying right in the potato patch, shining like a star!"

Oliver Owl, who was always a bit sleepy, hooted thoughtfully. "Golden, you say? Such things are rare."

Rosie then flew to Frederick Fox. "Frederick!" she called, her voice full of excitement. "Did you hear? Pip Squeakmouse has discovered a precious, golden button, as big as a hazelnut! It's from Farmer McGregor’s old _grandfather's_ coat, a family heirloom, lost for years!" Frederick Fox, who had a good nose for valuable things, perked up his ears. "As big as a hazelnut, you say? Gold? Now that is interesting!"

The story traveled like wildfire through Pleasant Meadow. By the time it reached Barnaby Badger again, repeated by young Timothy Dormouse, it had changed beyond all recognition. "Barnaby!" Timothy squeaked. "Did you know Pip Squeakmouse found a magnificent gold coin, as big as a walnut? It fell out of Farmer McGregor's strong-box and glows in the dark!"

Barnaby Badger snorted so loudly that a pile of leaves scattered. "A gold coin!" he scoffed. "And glowing, no less! What utter nonsense. That little mouse found nothing but a common brass button. I told him so myself."

"A button?" Timothy repeated, his eyes wide. "But Frederick Fox said it was a family heirloom! And Rosie Robin said it was golden!"

Barnaby shook his head. "That," he declared, "is the trouble with tales that fly on wings: They always grow larger than life. I'll go and see Pip Squeakmouse and remind him exactly what he found." He was rather annoyed, for he didn't like being contradicted, especially when he felt he had been right in the first place. But he was also a tiny bit curious. Could he have been wrong about the button? No, he thought, it was just a button. Still, a glowing button would be quite something.

Barnaby made his way to Pip's burrow. He found Pip sitting outside, carefully unwrapping his button from its moss-pad. The brass gleamed, certainly, but it was just a button.

"See?" Barnaby said, pointing with a muddy claw. "Just as I said. A button. No gold, no glowing, certainly not as big as a walnut."

Pip Squeakmouse looked up, his small face thoughtful. "Yes, Barnaby," he said. "It's a button. But it's _my_ button. And I still think it's beautiful."

Barnaby sighed. He had meant to correct the wild rumors, but Pip's simple pride in his discovery made him feel a little foolish. He realized then that sometimes, even a common button could be a treasure, if it belonged to someone who loved it. And sometimes, he thought, a story could spread so far that the truth was almost forgotten. He still didn't believe in glowing gold walnuts, of course, but he did admit that Pip’s little brass button had a rather pleasing shine.

Barnaby sighed again, a puff of earthy air that stirred a few more fallen leaves from their slumber. He left Pip admiring his polished brass button, a small, unassuming circle of metal that held such immense significance for its tiny owner. As Barnaby lumbered back towards his own cozy sett, he couldn’t shake the feeling that he had stumbled upon a truth far more complex than a simple brass button. It wasn't the button itself, he mused, but what people chose to believe about it. The meadow was already aflutter with the grand, gilded version of the tale. He, Barnaby, the steady, sensible badger, had tried to correct it, but the embellished version had wings, while the truth merely trudged along on tired paws.

He imagined the vibrant, sparkling imagery of a "gold coin, as big as a walnut, glowing in the dark" dancing in the minds of the other creatures. It was a far more captivating narrative than "Pip Squeakmouse found a rather shiny brass button." Barnaby knew the difference between shiny and glowing, between brass and gold, between a button and a coin, but the rest of Pleasant Meadow seemed to prefer the more fantastical option. He hoped that, given time, the excitement would die down, and the truth would eventually trickle through, like slow-moving water eroding a myth. He hoped, but deep down, a familiar badgerly cynicism warned him that some stories, once they take flight, are impossible to recapture.

Indeed, the story had not merely taken flight; it had soared to dizzying new heights. Rosie Robin, having delivered her initial, somewhat exaggerated report, felt compelled to elaborate further upon seeing the enthusiasm of her listeners. Each time she retold it, the button grew a little larger, a little shinier, and its provenance a little grander. Frederick Fox, hearing the whispers of a "golden heirloom" and then a "glowing coin," began to reconsider his own skepticism. He started to recall vague, tantalizing stories from his great-grandmother about Farmer McGregor's family having secret hoards, tales he had previously dismissed as old wives' fables. Now, they seemed to gain a kernel of credibility. He found himself imagining the weight of such a coin, the gleam it would possess, the rich history it might embody.

Soon, the news reached further afield, carried by chatty squirrels and gossip-loving sparrows. Old Man Groundhog, who rarely left his burrow, heard it from a particularly excitable vole. "A gold coin, you say?" he rumbled, his whiskers twitching. "From McGregor’s strong-box? That miserly farmer wouldn't lose a penny, let alone a treasure!" Yet, the very notion of such a loss made the story all the more intriguing. If Farmer McGregor, known for his tightfistedness, had lost it, it must be truly valuable.

The meadow began to buzz with an unprecedented level of curiosity. Animals started making their way towards Pip Squeakmouse's burrow, not just out of neighborly interest, but out of a genuine desire to witness this proclaimed wonder. Some came openly, their eyes wide with expectation; others, more wary or perhaps a little envious, tried to appear nonchalant, as if merely passing by on their daily errands.

Pip, utterly unaware of the escalating melodrama surrounding his simple find, was having a delightful afternoon. He had found a perfectly smooth, flat stone and was using it to gently polish his button. He liked the way the brass caught the sunlight, reflecting tiny sparkles onto his paws. He liked the solid feel of it, a tangible piece of the larger world he had discovered for himself. He had even, with great effort, dug a small, perfectly sized nook in the wall of his burrow where the button could rest, safe and admired.

Then came the first visitors. A group of young field mice, their fur still soft and their eyes full of innocent wonder, tiptoed to the edge of Pip's burrow. "Is it true, Pip?" one squeaked, "Did you really find the glowing coin?"

Pip looked up, a little startled. He held out his button. "I found this," he said proudly. "It's a button. And it's very shiny."

The field mice peered at it, then at each other. It was clearly not glowing. It was also clearly not as big as a walnut. It was, undeniably, just a button. But the rumors had been so insistent, so vivid! "Oh," said another mouse, a tiny hint of disappointment in his voice. "But Rosie Robin said it glows."

Pip tilted his head. "It glows when the sun shines on it very brightly," he offered, trying to reconcile their expectations with his reality. "See?" He held it up again, and indeed, a brief glint of sunlight made the brass wink.

The young mice, though not entirely convinced it was the magical coin they'd heard about, were still intrigued. They stayed for a while, watching Pip polish his treasure, perhaps hoping that with enough polishing, it *would* start to glow or grow to walnut size.

More visitors arrived. Mrs. Goose, with her stern demeanor and sharp eyes, waddled up, followed by a trio of her goslings. "Let's have a look at this coin, young mouse," she honked, peering down at Pip with an air of official inspection.

Pip, a little intimidated by her size and booming voice, again presented his button. "It's a button, Mrs. Goose," he said softly. "It's brass."

Mrs. Goose squinted. "Brass? Nonsense! Timothy Dormouse said it was gold! And from Farmer McGregor's strong-box!" She poked at it with her beak, making Pip flinch. "Hmph. Looks like an ordinary button to me. Perhaps it only glows at night?" she speculated, trying to salvage the grand tale. She left, muttering about exaggerated stories, but still a tiny seed of doubt lingered. What if it *did* glow at night? What if Pip was hiding its true golden nature?

Barnaby, meanwhile, had settled into his evening routine, convinced that the initial flurry of excitement would soon dissipate. He was enjoying a quiet cup of chamomile tea he had brewed from wild herbs when he heard the sounds. A constant murmuring, an excited chattering, a shuffling of paws and wings outside his sett. Curious, he poked his head out. The path to Pip's burrow, usually a quiet track, was now a veritable procession route. There were squirrels, rabbits, even a few badgers from the other side of the meadow, all heading in the same direction.

Barnaby snorted. "Unbelievable," he mumbled. "They simply refuse to believe the truth." He had imagined a scenario where a few curious animals would come, see the simple button, and then the story would wither and die. Instead, it seemed to be thriving on the very contradiction. The fact that Pip showed a brass button only seemed to fuel the conspiracy theories: he was hiding the *real* treasure, or it was magical and only revealed its true nature under specific conditions.

He watched the stream of creatures for a while, growing increasingly annoyed. This wasn't just idle chatter; it was disrupting the peace of the meadow. Pip, a small and gentle creature, was now at the center of an unwarranted spectacle. Barnaby felt a familiar prickle of responsibility. He had been the first to see the button, the first to dismiss it as common, but also the first to witness Pip's quiet joy in it. He couldn't let this ridiculous charade continue.

"I need to put a stop to this," he grumbled, putting down his tea. He emerged from his sett and set off towards Pip's burrow, his sturdy frame parting the curious crowds like a boat through water.

As he approached, he could see Pip looking increasingly overwhelmed. The small mouse sat outside his burrow, his button clutched protectively in his paws. A particularly persistent group of hedgehogs was prodding him with their noses, trying to get a closer look, while a group of voles were earnestly debating whether it was really a part of Farmer McGregor's legendary buried treasure.

"Good heavens, Pip," Barnaby said, his voice a low rumble, instantly silencing some of the chatter. "Are you quite alright?"

Pip looked up, his whiskers drooping slightly. "There are so many animals, Barnaby," he whispered. "They keep asking about the gold coin. But it's just my button."

Barnaby sighed, surveying the assembled throng. "Yes, Pip, I know. And they know too, if they'd just open their eyes and listen to plain sense." He turned to the crowd. "Listen here, all of you! This is getting out of hand! Pip found a brass button, a perfectly ordinary brass button! There is no gold coin, no glowing heirloom, no secret treasure from Farmer McGregor's strong-box!"

A ripple of murmuring went through the crowd. "But Frederick Fox said..." began a young squirrel. "And Rosie Robin was so sure it was golden!" chirped a sparrow.

"Rosie Robin is prone to exaggeration!" Barnaby declared. "And Frederick Fox has a nose for trouble, not necessarily for truth! I saw the button myself, and it is brass! I even told Pip so the moment he found it!"

Just then, a sleek, shadowy figure detached itself from the fringes of the crowd. It was Percival Weasel, a creature known for his sly dealings and a keen eye for anything that might benefit him. Percival had heard the tales, embellished and distorted as they were, and his mind had been spinning with possibilities. A truly valuable item, lost by a miserly farmer, now in the possession of a small, naive mouse? It was too good an opportunity to pass up. He had not believed in the "glowing" part, but "gold" and "heirloom" were words that resonated deeply with his opportunistic nature.

Percival sauntered forward, a fawning smile plastered on his narrow face. "Barnaby, my good fellow," he purred, his voice dripping with insincerity. "You are quite mistaken. One must never dismiss the humble beginnings of true wonders." He turned to Pip, his eyes glinting. "Little Pip Squeakmouse, such a remarkable discovery! A truly discerning eye you have! Such a unique piece!"

Barnaby narrowed his eyes. He knew Percival Weasel's reputation all too well. "It's a button, Percival," he stated flatly. "And it is not for sale, trade, or inspection by shifty-eyed weasels."

Percival chuckled, ignoring Barnaby's admonishment. "Oh, but Barnaby, you underestimate the power of history! This is clearly no ordinary button. From the coat of a venerable farmer, you say? Perhaps it bears a crest, a symbol of ancient lineage!" He leaned closer to Pip, his gaze fixed on the brass disk. "May I have a closer look, little friend? Just a moment, to admire its craftsmanship." He extended a claw, long and surprisingly delicate, as if to gently take the button from Pip's grasp.

Pip, confused by Percival's sudden, overly friendly demeanor, hesitated. He had always been taught to be wary of weasels.

"Don't you dare, Percival!" Barnaby boomed, stepping forward and placing his large paw protectively over Pip and the button. "Leave the mouse and his button alone!"

Percival recoiled, his false smile slipping. "Barnaby, you are being most uncivil! I merely wished to appreciate a rare item! Perhaps even offer a fair price, should young Pip be inclined to part with it. I have some very fine, polished stones, you see, which might make a splendid trade for such a... *historic* artifact." He emphasized "historic" with a sly wink, implying he believed the gold-heirloom story.

The crowd stirred, excited by the prospect of a potential transaction, a clear sign of the button's supposed immense value. Some began whispering about what Percival might offer.

"Pip doesn't want your polished stones, Percival," Barnaby asserted, his voice firm. "He is perfectly happy with his button, as it is."

"But surely," Percival countered, his gaze sweeping over the assembled animals, "a mouse of such humble means could benefit from a more substantial find! This button, in the right hands, could perhaps lead to... greater discoveries!" He was clearly hinting at the larger implications of the "McGregor's lost treasure" myth.

This sent another wave of excited chatter through the crowd. The story, which Barnaby had thought he was quelling, was now gaining a fresh burst of energy, propelled by Percival's devious suggestions.

Barnaby knew he had to act decisively. Percival wouldn't give up easily, and Pip was far too vulnerable to this kind of manipulation. He needed to expose Percival for what he was, and more importantly, unequivocally demonstrate the true nature of the button to everyone present.

"Very well, Percival," Barnaby said, his tone surprisingly calm. "If you are so convinced of its grand value, then let us examine it properly. All of us, under the full light of day. Pip, would you mind showing your button to everyone one last time, very clearly?"

Pip, sensing the seriousness in Barnaby's voice, nodded bravely. He held out the button, rotating it slowly so everyone could see.

"Now, Percival," Barnaby continued, "you claim this is a golden heirloom, perhaps a part of some lost treasure. Look closely. Does this glint like gold? Does it have the weight of a gold coin? Is it truly as big as a walnut?" Barnaby pointed with a claw at the brass disk, which shone with Pip's careful polishing, but was undeniably made of brass, about the size of a berry, and certainly not glowing on its own.

Percival, caught off guard by Barnaby's direct challenge in front of a full audience, hesitated. He had hoped to whisk the button away for a private "appraisal." Now, under the scrutiny of dozens of eyes, he had to confront the reality. He peered closely. It was, clearly, brass. It was small. It did not glow. He had relied on the *story* of its value, not its actual appearance.

"Well," Percival stammered, trying to regain his composure. "Perhaps... perhaps the true value is not in the material, but in the history! A relic, you see, a historical curio!" He was clutching at straws, trying to maintain the illusion of its worth.

"Nonsense!" Barnaby declared. "It is a brass button. From a market coat. Nothing more, nothing less. Pip found it, and he likes it. Its only value is in Pip's affection for it, and that is a value Percival Weasel cannot understand or steal!"

The crowd began to buzz with a different kind of energy. The initial excitement was deflating, replaced by a collective realization. Many of them had seen the button up close, and now, with Barnaby's blunt truth and Percival's awkward fumbling, the fantasy was crumbling. Mrs. Goose honked loudly, a sound of disapproval aimed squarely at Percival. Frederick Fox, watching from a tree branch, finally shook his head. He had almost let his greed get the better of his common sense. Rosie Robin, observing the sheepish faces around her, felt a pang of guilt for her own part in the grand embellishment.

Percival, realizing his scheme had backfired spectacularly and that he was losing face, decided to cut his losses. "Hmph! Well, if it's just a button, then it's hardly worth my time!" he sniffed disdainfully, trying to sound as if he had known this all along. "A common trinket! I wouldn't waste my energy on such a paltry item." He then slinked away, disappearing into the undergrowth, his pride wounded and his ambition thwarted.

A hush fell over the meadow creatures. The truth, stripped bare of all exaggeration, stood before them. Pip still held his brass button, shiny and unassuming. The "gold coin as big as a walnut" was revealed to be a simple, well-loved button.

Timothy Dormouse, who had been among the most vocal proponents of the "glowing coin" theory, shuffled his feet. "So... it's just a button?" he squeaked, his voice barely audible.

Barnaby nodded. "Just a button, Timothy. But a beautiful button to Pip, nonetheless."

He looked at Pip, who was now gazing at his button with renewed affection, perhaps even a sense of quiet triumph. The small mouse had weathered the storm of exaggerated tales and greedy intentions with remarkable resilience.

The crowd began to disperse, many with sheepish expressions. Some of the older, wiser animals, like Old Man Groundhog, simply shook their heads. They had seen similar follies before, the human tendency to inflate a simple truth into a grand myth. But for the younger animals, it was a valuable lesson. The power of a story, they realized, was immense, capable of transforming a piece of brass into a golden treasure, and back again.

Rosie Robin flew down from her perch, landing gently near Pip. "Pip," she chirped, her voice unusually soft, "I am sorry. I let my imagination run away with me."

Pip looked up at her, his small eyes earnest. "It's alright, Rosie," he said simply. "My button is still beautiful."

Frederick Fox approached, his tail drooping a little. "Indeed, Pip," he said, a note of genuine admiration in his voice. "It is a fine button. And you, little mouse, are a fine example to us all." He had been tempted by the allure of the gold, but seeing Pip's simple contentment, he felt a deeper, more profound respect for the mouse and his unpretentious joy.

Barnaby watched these interactions, a rare, faint smile gracing his usually gruff face. He had set out to correct a rumor, to bring sense back to Pleasant Meadow, and in a way, he had succeeded. The magnificent gold coin was no more. But in its place, something equally significant had emerged: a quiet understanding of different kinds of value. The button, though still brass, had taken on a new kind of fame. It was no longer just Pip's button; it was *the* button, the one that had shown everyone the difference between illusion and reality, between greedy desire and simple appreciation.

From that day forward, whenever a new rumor started to spread through Pleasant Meadow, especially one concerning hidden riches or exaggerated claims, someone would inevitably reference "Pip's Button." It became a quiet, gentle reminder not to get carried away, not to believe everything you heard, and to appreciate the simple, genuine treasures in life, no matter how small or unpretentious they seemed.

Pip Squeakmouse continued to polish his button, cherishing it not because it was gold, or from a strong-box, or glowing, but because it was *his*. He carried it on special occasions, sewn safely into a tiny pocket he had skillfully fashioned inside his waistcoat. It was a tangible link to his grand adventure, a small, shining piece of brass that told a much larger story than any golden coin ever could. It was the story of how a simple discovery could cause a commotion, expose a scoundrel, and ultimately, teach a whole community a lesson about perception, truth, and the enduring beauty of things loved for what they truly are. And Barnaby Badger, whenever he saw Pip proudly displaying his button, would offer a contented rumble, knowing that sometimes, the quiet truth could shine brighter than the most embellished tale. He had finally learned that sometimes, a story didn't need to grow larger than life to be important; it just needed to be true, and held with affection. And that, he decided, was a discovery as valuable as any gold coin.
