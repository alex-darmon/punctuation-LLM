Pipkin, a young rabbit with remarkably long ears, was quite pleased with himself. He had just outwitted Farmer Giles's old hound, Barker, by darting through a gap in the stone wall that Barker, for all his size, could not squeeze through. Pipkin twitched his nose with satisfaction. He imagined himself the swiftest creature in the entire meadow, perhaps even in the whole wide world. His feet seemed to fly over the grass, barely touching the blades.

"You seem rather puffed up, young Pipkin," a gruff voice rumbled from a patch of clover.

"Puffed up, Master Barnaby?" Pipkin asked, trying to sound modest, though his whiskers still trembled with pride. "I have merely outrun old Barker. It was a splendid chase, I assure you."

"A splendid chase, indeed," Barnaby murmured, without looking up. "But a chase is not a race, Pipkin. And speed alone does not always win the day."

"But it helps, does it not?" Pipkin insisted. "I can be from here to the old oak tree and back again before you have found a single fat grub!"

Barnaby chuckled, a sound like dry leaves rustling. "Perhaps you could," he agreed. "But what if the path to the old oak tree were not quite so straightforward? What if there were a thorny tangle, for instance, or a boggy patch?"

"I would simply leap over it!" Pipkin declared, giving a little jump to illustrate his point. "My legs are strong, and my eyes are sharp. I see every obstacle."

"Do you, now?" Barnaby finally looked up, his small, dark eyes twinkling. "Then you must be very sharp indeed. I have found that sometimes the things we overlook are the very ones that trip us up."

Pipkin scoffed inwardly. Barnaby was old, of course, and therefore slow. He simply did not understand the true joy of speed. "I am going to explore the berry bushes by the stone wall," Pipkin announced. "They are said to have the very sweetest fruit, but they are guarded by sharp prickles."

"Take care, then," Barnaby advised, resuming his digging. "Sweet fruit often hides sharp lessons."

Pipkin bounded off, a blur of grey fur against the green. He reached the cluster of berry bushes in moments. Indeed, the berries were plump and red, glistening in the morning sun. And the thorns, too, were long and sharp, interwoven into a dense thicket. Most creatures, he knew, would approach with caution, perhaps nibbling at the edges or waiting for a gust of wind to dislodge a berry. But Pipkin was not "most creatures." He was Pipkin, the fastest rabbit in the meadow! He would simply snatch a berry from the deepest part of the bush and be gone before a single thorn could touch him.

He took a running start, lowered his head, and launched himself into the thicket, intending to grab a berry and recoil with lightning speed.

But the thorns were more numerous and more tenacious than he had imagined. His momentum carried him too far. He wriggled frantically, but every movement only drove the thorns deeper, tearing at his fur and skin.

"Oh, dear!" Pipkin whimpered, his bravado entirely forgotten. "Oh, dear me! I am stuck!"

He could hear Barnaby's distant, rhythmic scratching. The old badger was still digging for grubs, quite oblivious to Pipkin's plight. Pipkin tried to shout, but his voice came out as a frightened squeak. Tears welled in his eyes. He had been so sure of himself, so certain that speed would overcome all. But now, tangled and bleeding, he felt very small and very foolish.

"So," Barnaby said softly, "the straightforward path proved to have a few snags, did it not?"

Pipkin could only nod, a fresh wave of tears blurring his vision. "I am truly stuck," he confessed, "and it hurts most dreadfully."

Barnaby worked patiently, using his strong paws and snout to clear a path, breaking off small thorny twigs, and gently disentangling Pipkin's fur from the snags.
