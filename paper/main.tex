\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Strip the words from a novel and what remains is a sequence of commas,
full stops, quotation marks and semicolons.  That residue is enough to
name its author \cite{darmon2021}.  We asked two current Gemini language models
to write in the style of twenty such authors, with an excerpt and the
instruction placed before the model on every generation call, and then
asked the residue who had written it.  The answer was rarely the
requested author but it was the model.  Flash and
Pro land on their target 13.5\% and 16.0\% of the time, at or barely
above the 5\% of a guess; human passages scored the same way, 73.1\%.
What each model produces instead is a punctuation style of its own: one
that holds whichever author was requested, differs between the two
models, and is in place by the second thousand marks of a run.  Because
it holds, it can be recognised.  A test calibrated only on human
writing, with no labelled generated text and no access to the model,
separates generated from genuine 2,000-mark passages at AUC 0.96 and
0.84.

This is not forgetting.  The source excerpt and the style instruction
are in front of the model on \emph{every} call, so the request is never
out of view, and the deficit is the same.  Nor is it a failure of the
targets.  Ten of the twenty panel authors were admitted precisely
because their punctuation is recoverable, and on those ten the same
procedure identifies human writing correctly 88.8\% of the time in a
ten-way comparison; asked to imitate those same ten, the models reach
25.0\% and 23.0\%.  The models are not producing noise, and they are
not producing the requested author.  They are producing themselves.

Punctuation is a good setting in which to establish this.  The
representation is small enough that every step---the reference profile,
the divergence statistic, the decision threshold---can be inspected
directly, so a result can be explained rather than only reported.

Three strands of prior work bear on the question and none answers it.
Detection of generated text is well studied, but standard detectors
need labelled generated text, access to model probabilities, or both,
and they ask ``was this machine-written'' rather than ``is this
consistent with the author it claims''
\cite{ippolito2020,mitchell2023,sadasivan2024}.  Corpus-linguistic work
reports that instruction-tuned models occupy a narrower band of
stylistic variation than human writers and share habits across models
\cite{milicka2025,reinhart2025}, and imitation of named authors has
been measured directly, with results that are conditional: some surface
properties of a target are reproduced while the output remains
stylometrically distinct \cite{mikros2025}, and success varies by
register \cite{wang2025} and by decoding settings \cite{milicka2025}.
What has not been done in that strand is to ask the question on the
punctuation channel alone, against a named author's own profile, under
an evaluation that closes the two routes by which a passage can inform
its own assessment: the reference profile it is compared against, and
the threshold used to judge it.  Leakage of that kind has been examined
where topic similarity crosses train and test boundaries
\cite{sawatphol2024}; here the two channels are separated and each is
measured.  Section~\ref{sec:related} sets out each strand in full.

We use 60 books by 20 authors, and 600 passages generated by
Gemini~2.5 Flash and Gemini~2.5 Pro with the prompt repeated on every
call; a further 600 generated under a single initial prompt serve as a
contrast condition and are reported in the appendix. Every passage, human or genreated,
is evaluated against two objectives.  \emph{Attribution} asks which of the
twenty authors it most resembles.  \emph{Verification} asks whether it
is consistent with the author it claims to be, judged against a
threshold learned from other authors.  A passage is compared only with
profiles built from human books, and never with a profile containing
the book it came from.  Author selection, reference construction and
threshold calibration are varied deliberately, so that the contribution
of each to the reported number is measured rather than assumed.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Failed imitation defaults to the model's own
  style.}  With the excerpt and the author instruction before the model
  on every call, generated passages are attributed to their requested
  author 13.5\% (Flash) and 16.0\% (Pro) of the time against 73.1\% for
  human text, so the deficit is not an artefact of the request passing
  out of view.  Each model instead writes in a punctuation style of its
  own, to which its passages are attributed whichever author was
  requested; the two styles are distinct from one another, and each is
  in place by the second 1,000-mark window of a run.

  \item \textbf{The default style is recognisable.}  A test calibrated only on other human authors
  separates generated from human passages at AUC 0.959 (Flash) and
  0.842 (Pro) at 2,000 marks, at a held-out human false-positive rate
  of 5.36\%.  The rate is not a fixed property of a model: it rises
  with passage length, is higher for later text than for earlier text
  of the same length, and changes with how the text was prompted, so a
  single quoted figure does not transfer between generation conditions.

  \item \textbf{Leakage and panel selection.}  Which authors form the panel shifts ten-way
  human attribution by 25.7 points, ten times the difference between
  the two models.  Letting a scored book into its own reference raises
  detection by 9.0 to 33.0 points across the grid, and calibrating the
  threshold on the tested authors moves it by up to 6.5.  The reference
  leak inflates the rate by lowering the false-alarm rate on the
  calibration set alone, so the advertised operating point is wrong for
  any new document.  These choices are routinely left unstated, and a
  figure reported without them cannot be compared with another.

  \item \textbf{More books beat longer books.}  The overdispersion of the likelihood-ratio
  statistic is accounted for by a chunk-to-reference divergence near
  0.03 nats, stable in passage length and scaling with the number of
  marks.  Read as a design effect it orders reference designs out of
  sample: for a fixed budget of text, two books beat one book of twice
  the length at every budget tested, and two books of 1,000 marks match
  one of 4,000.  Simulation excludes first-order dependence and
  sentence-scale dialogue switching as sufficient mechanisms, placing
  the heterogeneity at the scale of scenes or larger.  These results
  answer three questions left open by Darmon et al.\ \cite{darmon2021}:
  how accuracy depends on sequence length, how much text a stylistic
  footprint requires, and whether the punctuation of part of a document
  stands for the whole.
\end{itemize}

The claim is about punctuation and not about a generat writing style: a model
that fails to reproduce an author's commas and semicolons may still
reproduce her vocabulary or syntax, and this paper does not test that.
The panel is constructed rather than sampled---half its authors were
admitted by a recoverability screen---so it is enriched for
recoverability and not representative of authors in general; figures
conditional on it should be read accordingly.  The human profiles are
built from Project Gutenberg editions, whose punctuation is the joint
product of author, editor and typesetter and cannot be separated into
those parts \cite{darmon2021}; the style a model fails to reproduce is
therefore an edition's style, and ``author'' is used throughout as
shorthand for that.  Both generators come
from one model family, so a default style common to them cannot be
separated from a property of that family.  None of the individual
components---the punctuation representation, KL divergence,
likelihood-ratio testing, resampling---is new; the contribution is
their combination and the analysis of where their assumptions fail.
The verification objective tests consistency with prior writing rather
than provenance: a passage that fails it is inconsistent with its
claimed author, which a different human author would also be.

Sections~\ref{sec:human}--\ref{sec:detection} report the results:

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Punctuation as a stylometric channel.}
Authorship attribution typically combines lexical, character, syntactic, and
structural cues \cite{stamatatos2009}.  Darmon et al.\ isolate punctuation
and define the frequency and transition features used here
\cite{darmon2021}; punctuation otherwise appears only within broader feature
inventories \cite{przystalski2026}. 

\paragraph{Detection of generated text.}
Existing detectors fall into three families: supervised classifiers trained
on labelled human and generated corpora \cite{ippolito2020,uchendu2021},
watermarks inserted at generation time \cite{kirchenbauer2023}, and
zero-shot methods that score a passage with one or more language models
\cite{mitchell2023,hans2024}.  Each dependency limits transfer when the
generator, domain, or editing practice changes \cite{sadasivan2024}, and
none conditions its decision on prior writing by the claimed author. What we study here does:
it asks whether a passage is
consistent with an author-specific human reference, requiring prior writing
by the claimant but neither labelled generated text, generator cooperation,
nor model access.  It cannot, in return, distinguish machine generation from
human impersonation.

\paragraph{Style of generated text and imitation.}
Generated language is not stylistically uniform.  Model-specific
fingerprints support attribution among generators \cite{bitton2025,rao2025}
and have been described as artificial idiolects \cite{rudnicka2025}; at the
same time, instruction-tuned outputs occupy a narrower region of human
variation and converge on shared styles across models
\cite{milicka2025,reinhart2025}.  Imitation is correspondingly conditional:
GPT-4o reproduces some surface properties of Hemingway and Shelley while
remaining stylometrically distinct \cite{mikros2025}, imitation is stronger
in structured registers such as news and email than in blogs and forums
\cite{wang2025}, and base models imitate better than their instruction-tuned
counterparts under some decoding settings \cite{milicka2025}.  The present
experiment---punctuation only, literary prose, two instruction-tuned Gemini
models---is narrower than any of these, and its negative attribution result
is evidence about that channel and protocol rather than a contradiction of
conditional success elsewhere.  Its contribution to this strand is to
locate that shared region in the punctuation profile and to show that
convergence toward it continues within a generation.

\paragraph{Leakage in authorship evaluation.}
Evaluation leakage in authorship verification has been studied chiefly
where topic similarity crosses nominal train/test boundaries
\cite{sawatphol2024}.
% TODO: add 1--2 references on evaluation design in verification
% (e.g.\ PAN shared-task protocols; cross-topic / cross-genre attribution).
The leakage examined here is different in kind: the scored book enters the
profile used to judge it, and the threshold is tuned on the authors being
scored.  Treating the two as separate channels, and measuring each, is what
allows the inflation they produce to be attributed.

\paragraph{Statistical antecedents.}
Likelihood-ratio and \(\chi^2\) references for transition tables follow
classical Markov-chain inference \cite{anderson1957}; the design-effect
interpretation and effective-sample-size calculation come from cluster
sampling \cite{kish1965}; corpus-linguistic work similarly normalises
generated-text shifts by variation between human segments
\cite{milicka2025}.  What is added here is the identification of a
chunk-to-reference heterogeneity term in the expected statistic, its
linear-in-length signature, and the use of that signature to separate
persistent textual heterogeneity from homogeneous serial dependence.

results: whether human authors are recoverable, whether the models imitate

\section{Data and methodology}
\label{sec:materials}

\subsection{Human corpus and author panel}
\label{sec:corpus}

The human corpus comprises 60 books by 20 authors, three per author, drawn
from Project Gutenberg.  All works are English prose, published between the
early nineteenth and mid twentieth centuries; no genre-matching rule was
applied, and novels and story collections coexist.  Books contain between
1,955 and 51,920 analysed punctuation marks (median 13,771; 1.04 million in
total); per-author totals range from 7,836 to 128,872.

The panel has two arms of ten authors.  The \emph{general} arm consists
of authors chosen without reference to punctuation recoverability.  The
\emph{screened} arm is admitted by a two-stage rule applied to human text
only.  Three independently published works per author are fixed from
bibliographic metadata, and exact-content aliases are not counted as
distinct books.  Stage one requires a human-only separability ratio \(D/C > 1\) under
\fThree, defined in Section~\ref{sec:dc}: the author's books must be closer
to one another than to the nearest other author.  The ratio is computed on
the full 651-author, 14,947-document Gutenberg snapshot of Darmon et al.\
\cite{darmon2021}; 24 labels clear this threshold, several of them
institutional rather than individual authors. Stage
two requires each author to be attributed correctly for at least two of
three held-out whole books in the 20-author \fThree comparison. No below-threshold control arm is
included.  The composite panel is therefore a conditional evaluation
population enriched for punctuation recoverability, and every estimate
downstream is conditional on it.

\subsection{Generated texts}
\label{sec:generated}

Text was generated with two instruction-tuned Gemini~2.5 models,
\emph{Flash} (\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}, 20 runs per author) and
\emph{Pro} (\texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}, 10 runs per author), under two
prompting protocols: 600 runs in each protocol, 1,200 in all.

The two protocols share everything but the delivery of the instruction.
The first call of a run supplies a 1,500-word excerpt taken from the
middle of one of the author's books, followed by an instruction to write
an original piece of fiction in the same style and to return plain prose
without em- or en-dashes (in line with the original paper
\cite{darmon2021}); the instruction also directs attention to the relative
use of commas, semicolons, colons, question marks, exclamation marks and
quotation marks.  When a response falls short of the 5,000-mark target, a
continuation call supplies the last 500 words of the accumulated text and
requests at least 2,000 further words, for up to 30 rounds.  Temperature
is 1.0 and each call is capped at 8,192 output tokens.  The source book
rotates across runs, so every run has a recorded \emph{prompt-source
book}.  Every run supplies at least 5,002 marks (median 5,254.5 for Flash
and 5,306.5 for Pro); the first 5,000 are analysed.

The protocols differ from the second call onward.  In the
\emph{repeated-prompt} runs, analysed in
Section~\ref{sec:llm-attribution}, every continuation call repeats the
excerpt and the author instruction ahead of the 500-word tail, so the
model never writes without the request in view.  In the
\emph{single-prompt} runs the instruction appears in the first call only
and each continuation carries nothing but the tail; these are retained as
a contrast condition (Appendix~\ref{app:superseded}) and are never merged
with the repeated-prompt runs.  Because the first call is identical under
both protocols, the two batches are directly comparable at the opening of
a run; Section~\ref{sec:position} reports that check.

\subsection{Punctuation features and analysis units}
\label{sec:representation}

Text is reduced to a sequence over a ten-symbol alphabet,
\[
  \texttt{!}\quad\texttt{"}\quad\texttt{(}\quad\texttt{)}\quad\texttt{,}
  \quad\texttt{.}\quad\texttt{:}\quad\texttt{;}\quad\texttt{?}\quad\texttt{\^{}},
\]
where the caret stands for a three-dot ellipsis and single and typographic
quotation marks are mapped to the straight double quote.  All other
characters are discarded without breaking adjacency, and the same parser is
applied to human and generated text.  Two feature vectors are computed from
a sequence: \fOne, the ten marginal frequencies, and \fThree, the 100-cell
joint distribution of consecutive pairs, obtained by weighting each row of
the transition matrix by the marginal frequency of its first mark.
\fThree is the primary feature because it was the most discriminating of
the six features of \cite{darmon2021} for author recognition under the
same divergence-based classifier: with ten authors it reached 74\% test
accuracy against 69\% for \fOne, and the gap widened with panel size (41\%
against 27\% at 400 authors).  \fOne is retained as a sensitivity
throughout.

\label{sec:units}

Human books are tiled into consecutive, non-overlapping chunks of exactly
\(m\) marks, with the incomplete tail discarded.  At \(m = 1{,}000\) the
panel yields 1,003 chunks; at \(m = 2{,}000\), 485; at \(m = 4{,}000\),
225.  A whole book, scored as a single unit, is also used in
the admission gate of Section~\ref{sec:corpus} and in the separability
diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:dc}.  Four books fall below 4,000 marks,
three of them by one author, so 56 books and 19 authors contribute at that
length.  Generated runs are analysed as
fixed-length \emph{prefixes} of the same three lengths, so the same 600
runs appear at every length and the longer prefixes contain the shorter
ones.  Positional analyses use five consecutive 1,000-mark windows over the
first 5,000 marks of each run, and an equal-length contrast between marks
1--2,000 and marks 2,001--4,000 of the same run.  The primary specification
throughout is \fThree at 2,000 marks.  Position is not randomized, and
remains associated with narrative phase, accumulated generated context,
continuation-call number and proximity to termination.  Call boundaries
are recorded for the runs analysed here (Section~\ref{sec:generated}),
and Section~\ref{sec:position} uses them.


\subsection{Reference profiles and leakage control}
\label{sec:references}

Both objectives of Section~\ref{sec:intro} (attribution, which asks which
panel author a passage most resembles, and verification, which asks whether
it is consistent with the author it claims to be) are comparisons against
the same profile.  We describe how we construct that profile in this section.

Let \(\Theta = \{\theta_1, \dots, \theta_{10}\}\) denote the set of
punctuation marks of Section~\ref{sec:representation}, and let \(B_a\)
denote the set of books that we include for author \(a\).  We concatenate
the mark sequences of the books in \(B_a\) into a single sequence
\(D_a = \{\theta^a_1 \mid \dots \mid \theta^a_{n_a}\}\) of \(n_a\) marks, so
that longer books contribute more marks.  The profile \(R_a\) of author
\(a\) is the pair of count arrays
\begin{equation}
  C^a_i = \big|\{\theta^a_l \in D_a \mid \theta^a_l = \theta_i\}\big|,
  \qquad
  C^a_{ij} = \big|\{\theta^a_l \in D_a \mid \theta^a_l = \theta_i
  \text{ and } \theta^a_{l+1} = \theta_j\}\big| .
  \label{eq:profile-counts}
\end{equation}
\(C^a_i\) counts how often each mark occurs; \(C^a_{ij}\) counts how often
each ordered pair occurs (mark \(i\), then mark \(j\)).  Every later
quantity is a normalisation of these counts.  We write \(R^1_a\) for the
marginal distribution over marks, \(R^2_a\) for the row-conditional
transition matrix (what tends to follow each mark), and \(R^3_a\) for the
joint distribution of consecutive pairs, following Darmon et al.\
\cite{darmon2021}:
\begin{equation}
  R^{1}_{a,i} = \frac{C^a_i}{n_a}, \qquad
  R^{2}_{a,ij} = \frac{C^a_{ij}}{\sum_{j'} C^a_{ij'}}, \qquad
  R^{3}_{a,ij} = \frac{C^a_{ij}}{\sum_{i'j'} C^a_{i'j'}} .
  \label{eq:profile-norms}
\end{equation}
Note that \(R^{3}_{a,ij} = R^{2}_{a,ij} R^{1}_{a,i}\).  These are their
features \(\boldsymbol{f}^{1}\), \(\boldsymbol{f}^{2}\), and \(\boldsymbol{f}^{3}\),
computed on the concatenation of an author's included books rather than on a
single document, and we retain that numbering throughout the paper.
Thus \(R^1_a\) has ten entries and \(R^2_a\), \(R^3_a\) are
\(10\times 10\) tables; we call an entry of any of them a cell.

The two objectives use different normalisations of the same counts.
Attribution compares a text to \(R^1_a\) and \(R^3_a\) unsmoothed,
because its divergence discards empty cells rather than evaluating them
(Section~\ref{sec:attribution}).
Verification compares a text to \(R^1_a\) and to the rows of \(R^2_a\),
which must be strictly positive.  We therefore smooth on the verification
path only, adding a constant \(\epsilon\) to every count before
normalising:
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde R^{1}_{a,i} = \frac{C^a_i + \epsilon}{n_a + |\Theta|\epsilon},
  \qquad
  \widetilde R^{2}_{a,ij}
    = \frac{C^a_{ij} + \epsilon}{\sum_{j'} C^a_{ij'} + |\Theta|\epsilon} .
  \label{eq:smoothing}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:smoothing} is the posterior mean of a multinomial cell
probability under a symmetric Dirichlet prior with parameter \(\epsilon\),
so \(\epsilon\) is a pseudo-count: the reference behaves as though
\(\epsilon\) occurrences of every mark, and of every transition out of
each mark, had been observed alongside the author's books.  The primary
value \(\epsilon = 0.5\) is the Jeffreys prior for the multinomial
\cite{jeffreys1946}. The influence of
\(\epsilon\) on a row is of order \(|\Theta|\epsilon/N\), where \(N\)
is the row total: 0.02\% for the comma row of the example below, and the
entire content of a row with few observations, so smoothing acts where the
reference is thin and is negligible where it is not.  Its consequences for
the test are taken up in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.

As an example, consider Jane Austen with \emph{Pride and Prejudice} held
out.  A chunk of that book is scored against a profile built from the other
two books only, \emph{Emma} and \emph{Sense and Sensibility}.  They
contribute \(n_a = 45{,}462\) marks and 45{,}461 ordered pairs, of which
21{,}828 marks are commas, so the comma entry of \(R^1_a\) is
\(21{,}828/45{,}462 = 0.480\).  The comma row of \(C^a\) records what
follows a comma:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \texttt{!} & \texttt{"} & \texttt{(} & \texttt{)} & \texttt{,}
      & \texttt{.} & \texttt{:} & \texttt{;} & \texttt{?} & \texttt{\^{}} \\[2pt]
    312 & 1{,}705 & 76 & 56 & 11{,}267 & 6{,}054 & 100 & 1{,}928 & 330 & 0
  \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The comma--period count \(6{,}054\) is the same number in both objectives,
read two ways.  Attribution uses the joint share
\(R^{3}_{a,ij} = 6{,}054/45{,}461 = 0.133\), equivalently
\(0.480 \times 0.277\): among all consecutive pairs, how often this pair
occurs.  Verification uses the conditional
\(R^{2}_{a,ij} = 6{,}054/21{,}828 = 0.277\): given a comma, how often the
next mark is a period.  The comma--ellipsis cell is empty, as are 30 of the
100 cells even at 45{,}461 pairs.  Attribution can ignore that cell;
smoothing, used only in verification, sends its conditional to
\(0.5/21{,}833 = 2.3 \times 10^{-5}\) and leaves the comma--period
conditional at 0.277.

Which books enter \(B_a\) is an explicit argument of every profile build,
never a default, because the choice determines what the experiment
measures.  Under the primary \emph{leave-one-book-out} policy, we score a
human chunk against a profile of its author from which the chunk's whole
source book has been removed, and a generated run against a profile of its
requested author from which the prompt-source book
(Section~\ref{sec:generated}) has been removed.  Every build is checked
programmatically for the excluded content and recorded;
Appendix~\ref{app:audit} describes the audit.  Under
the alternative \emph{pooled-all-books} policy, the scored book remains in
its own reference; we compute it on a separate path and report it only as a
leakage contrast, never as a headline estimate.

\subsection{Attribution}
\label{sec:attribution}

Attribution asks which of the panel authors a text most resembles.
Resemblance is measured by the directed Kullback--Leibler divergence: for
distributions \(p\) and \(q\) on the same cells,
\(\KL(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_i p_i \log(p_i/q_i)\).  The sum is small when the
text uses the same cells as the author, at similar rates, and large when it
concentrates on cells the author rarely uses.  Following the
implementation of \cite{darmon2021}, cells that are zero in either
distribution are dropped and the other is renormalised over the remainder,
so the divergence stays finite.  At 2,000 marks a chunk rarely fills all
100 \fThree cells, so this convention is the typical comparison rather than
an edge case.  A text \(x\) is assigned to the author whose profile is
nearest in this sense,
\[
  \widehat a(x) = \arg\min_{a} \KL\!\big(f(x)\,\|\,R^{3}_{a}\big),
\]
in the primary specification, with \(f(x)\) the joint pair distribution of
the text; the \fOne sensitivity replaces \(R^{3}_{a}\) by \(R^{1}_{a}\)
and \(f(x)\) by the text's marginal.  A human chunk is counted correct when assigned to its own
author; a generated prefix is counted correct when assigned to the author
named in its prompt, the style it was asked to imitate.  The primary
comparison is 20-way.  The two arms of Section~\ref{sec:corpus} are also
evaluated as separate 10-way tasks against their own members, isolating the
effect of which authors enter the panel.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

Verification asks a different question of the same profiles: not which
author a text is nearest to, but whether it is consistent with one
nominated author at all.  Applied to a generated passage, verification is
detection: a passage that fails the test is flagged as not written by its
claimed author.  A distance cannot answer this on its own: a
divergence from an author's profile means nothing until one knows how far
that author's own chunks typically fall.  What is needed is a null
distribution, and the score is therefore a likelihood-ratio statistic.  For
\fOne,
\[
  G = 2 \sum_{i} O_i \log\frac{O_i}{n\,q_i},
\]
where \(O_i\) are the observed mark counts, \(n\) their total, and
\(q = \widetilde R^{1}_a\) the smoothed reference marginal of
\eqref{eq:smoothing}.  Writing \(\widehat p = O/n\) for the chunk's
empirical marginal, \(G = 2n\,\KL(\widehat p \,\|\, q)\) up to the
treatment of empty cells, so verification uses the same divergence as
attribution, scaled by the number of marks.  Under the hypothesis that the
text was drawn from \(q\), \(G\) has nine nominal degrees of freedom.
The nominal distributions are those of Wilks' theorem \cite{wilks1938}:
assuming independent draws from a known \(q\) (a stationary first-order
chain for \fThree), \(G\) is asymptotically \(\chi^2\) with degrees of
freedom equal to the number of free cell probabilities the hypothesis
fixes.  For \fThree the
statistic is taken row by row against the smoothed transition matrix,
\[
  G = 2 \sum_{i}\sum_{j} O_{ij}
      \log\frac{O_{ij}}{O_{i\cdot}\,\widetilde R^{2}_{a,ij}} ,
\]
where \(O_{ij}\) are the observed pair counts and \(O_{i\cdot}\) the
observed row totals, with \(|\Theta|(|\Theta|-1) = 90\) nominal degrees of
freedom \cite{anderson1957}.  Conditioning on the observed row totals makes
this a test of the transitions of \(R^{2}_a\), whereas attribution compares
the joint distribution \(R^{3}_a\); \eqref{eq:profile-norms} relates the
two.  Larger \(G\) indicates greater departure from the nominated
author.  Human chunks are scored against their own
author's leave-one-book-out profile and generated prefixes against the
requested author's prompt-book-excluded profile, exactly as in attribution.

Turning \(G\) into a decision requires a threshold, and where that
threshold is estimated is the second leakage channel.  Thresholds are
therefore calibrated \emph{out of author}.  The 20 authors are assigned by
seed to five folds of four, stratified by arm; the assignment is recorded
with the results.  For each fold, the threshold is the 95th percentile of
human \(G\) scores from the other four folds and is applied only to the
human chunks and generated runs of the held-out authors, so the objective
measures transfer of the decision rule to authors it has never seen.
Held-out scores are divided by their fold threshold before pooling, which
places the five folds on one operating-point scale and allows a single
out-of-fold ROC curve and AUC to be formed.  The reported quantities are
out-of-fold AUC, the empirical human false-positive rate, and the
generated-text true-positive rate.  An \emph{in-sample} contrast sets one
threshold on all human scores and is reported only to bound the effect of
calibration leakage, in the same way that the pooled-all-books policy
bounds reference leakage.

Smoothing enters the test at exactly one point.  Because
\eqref{eq:smoothing} is applied to the reference and never to the observed
counts, \(\epsilon\) shifts the null against which a text is judged rather
than the text itself, and it bounds the term that any single unobserved
transition can contribute to \(G\).  Thresholds are high quantiles of an
empirical \(G\) distribution that Section~\ref{sec:calibration} shows to be
strongly overdispersed, so \(\epsilon\) is the one free constant that could
move a decision through the tail rather than through the data.  We report
\(\epsilon \in \{0.1, 1.0\}\) as sensitivities
(Section~\ref{sec:estimands}); the effect is bounded in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:estimands}

Each objective can be run under different settings: two features, three chunk
lengths (Section~\ref{sec:units}), three author sets (all twenty, the
general ten, the screened ten), two reference policies
(Section~\ref{sec:references}), and three smoothing values
\(\epsilon \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}\).  We run all of them and report the results in a grid.  One cell is the
headline: \fThree, 2,000 marks, all authors, leave-one-book-out,
\(\epsilon = 0.5\), and a 5\% target false-positive rate.  This cell, and
the quantities defined below, were written into a configuration file
before any inference was run.  Every other cell is either a sensitivity
check, which asks whether the answer survives a change of one setting, or
a contrast, which measures what leakage would have added.

Results are read against two reference points.  The first is chance: with
twenty candidate profiles a passage assigned at random is correct 5\% of
the time, and 10\% within a ten-author arm.  Chance says only whether a
method carries information.  The second is human text scored in exactly
the same way---same feature, same reference policy, same chunk length,
same folds---which says how much of the available signal a method
recovers; for imitation this is the informative comparison, since a model
that beats chance but falls far short of human text has not imitated its
target.  Verification has no chance baseline, because its decision rule
is calibrated rather than guessed.  There the reference point is an
operating point: the threshold is set so that 5\% of genuine human chunks
are flagged, and every rate is read at that cost.

The unit of analysis is the author, not the chunk or the run.  Authors
differ in how many chunks they contribute, and Flash has twice as many
runs as Pro, so a pooled accuracy would be dominated by the longest books
and the larger batch.  We therefore compute every accuracy and rate for
each author separately and average the twenty; pooled figures are
reported alongside for comparison only.  Uncertainty follows the same rule.
Chunks and runs from one author are not independent observations, so the
bootstrap resamples authors: each of 2,000 draws selects twenty authors
with replacement and carries each author's books, chunks, prompt-source
groups, and runs together.  We report the central 95\% interval of the
draws.  For verification, each draw also re-estimates every fold
threshold, so the interval includes the uncertainty in the threshold
itself; a draw that leaves a fold empty is discarded and redrawn.

The Flash--Pro comparison is paired by author: for each author we take
the difference in target attribution between the two models and summarise
the twenty differences by their mean, a paired author bootstrap interval,
and a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test.  Pairing removes the large
differences in difficulty between authors from the comparison.  Binomial
tests of individual runs against chance appear in the results as
description only; runs within an author are not independent, so they are
not used for inference about the population.

\section{Human authors are recoverable}
\label{sec:human}

Human punctuation must be recoverable before failure to imitate it is
meaningful.  This section measures how well the panel authors are
recognised from their own writing, how much that depends on who is in the
panel, and whether the screening rule used to admit half of them ranks
recoverability.

\subsection{Attribution in the two design arms}

At the primary \fThree, 2,000-mark setting, leave-one-book-out
macro accuracy is 63.1\% in the ten-author general arm and 88.8\% in the
ten-author screened arm, each against 10\% chance.  The 25.7-point difference
shows how strongly the reported value depends on panel construction; because
admission used document attribution on the same books, the screened-arm chunk
estimate is post-selection rather than an independent estimate of the effect
of screening.

When all 20 profiles compete, composite chunk accuracy is 73.1\% (95\%
author-bootstrap interval 59.3--85.0\%) against 5\% chance.  Within that
20-way candidate set, raw chunk accuracy is 66.3\% for the general arm and
89.1\% for the screened arm.  The composite value is therefore an evaluation
of this deliberately constructed panel, not an estimate for arbitrary
authors.

\subsection{Separability diagnostic}
\label{sec:dc}

The screening rule of Section~\ref{sec:corpus} used a human-only
separability ratio \(D/C\); this subsection defines it and the cross-fit
under which it is evaluated.  In each of three rotations one book per
author is held out, for every author at once, so that every profile in the
rotation is built from two books.  Write \(f^3(b)\) for the joint pair
distribution of a single book \(b\), and \(b_{a1}, b_{a2}\) for the two
books retained for author \(a\), so that \(B_a = \{b_{a1}, b_{a2}\}\).
The ratio is computed under \fThree, the primary feature
(Section~\ref{sec:representation}), with \fOne as a sensitivity.  \(C_a\)
is the two-book case of the author-consistency measure of
\cite{darmon2021}, symmetrised; \(D_a\) replaces their random-pairs
baseline by the nearest rival, so that the comparison is made per author
rather than against the corpus average.  The within-author inconsistency is the symmetrised divergence between the
author's two retained books,
\[
  C_a = \tfrac12\big\{\KL\big(f^3(b_{a1})\,\|\,f^3(b_{a2})\big)
       + \KL\big(f^3(b_{a2})\,\|\,f^3(b_{a1})\big)\big\},
\]
and the between-author distance is the divergence from the author's
two-book profile to the nearest rival's two-book profile,
\[
  D_a = \min_{a' \neq a} \KL\big(R^3_a \,\|\, R^3_{a'}\big).
\]
The held-out book contributes to neither quantity.  The geometric mean of
\(D_a/C_a\) over the three rotations is compared with whole-book and chunk
attribution of the held-out books by Spearman correlation, with an
author-bootstrap interval.  A positive
association would show that the diagnostic ranks recoverability before any
attribution is run on the held-out material.

\subsection{Cross-fitted \texorpdfstring{\(D/C\)}{D/C} as a recoverability diagnostic}

Across the ten general-arm authors, who were not selected using the present
diagnostic, cross-fitted \(D/C\) has Spearman \(\rho=0.818\) with held-out
chunk accuracy (\(p=0.0038\)).  This arm provides the selection-independent
evidence within the study that the diagnostic ranks recoverability.  Across
the screened arm the association falls to
\(\rho=0.283\) (\(p=0.428\)), as expected after restricting the predictor's
range.  The pooled association, \(\rho=0.534\) (95\% author-bootstrap interval
0.021--0.895), is reported for completeness but mixes these two designs.
Under \fOne the pooled association is \(\rho = 0.576\) (0.082--0.936,
\(p = 0.008\)), so the ranking does not depend on which feature defines
the ratio.

\section{Imitation fails by convergence}
\label{sec:llm-attribution}

Each generated text was prompted to write in the style of one panel
author and each generation campaign had different generation instructions.
In the \emph{repeated-prompt} runs the excerpt, the
author's name and the style instruction are placed in front of the model
on every call, not only the first (Section~\ref{sec:generated}), so the
model is never writing without the request in view.  In the
\emph{single-prompt} runs the instruction appears in the first call only,
and every later call carries nothing but a tail of the model's own
output. We then apply the attribution
test of Section~\ref{sec:human} to the repeated-prompt texts: which of
the twenty authors does each most resemble?  If imitation worked, the
answer would be the requested author.  Guessing would give the right
answer 5\% of the time; human chunks reach 73.1\%.

\subsection{Each model has a default style}

Generated predictions also concentrate on a few panel authors: Dickens
receives 104 of 400 Flash runs at 2,000 marks and Shelley a further 101,
while Wells receives 44 of 200 Pro runs and Doyle 42.

quotation marks, semicolons and exclamation marks than the panel median,
whichever author was requested, and each has a region of its own.  We
call this the model's \emph{default style}: the punctuation it produces
regardless of which author is requested.  
Flash is comma-heavy (59.0\% of marks, against a panel median of
40.1\%), which sends it to Dickens and Shelley, two of the panel's most
comma-heavy authors and Shelley the heaviest.  Shelley is also the
panel's heaviest user of semicolons, 11.1\% against Flash's 0.8\%;
attribution is on the pair distribution, and Flash's comma-dominated
pairs match hers despite that mismatch.  Pro sits nearer the median on
commas and full stops and is attributed to Wells and Doyle.

references.  No panel author receives more than 17 of the 113
misattributed human chunks, and the four authors nearest the two
defaults receive few of them: human chunks by the other authors are attributed to Dickens in 13
of 451 cases (2.9\%), to Shelley in 7 of 465 (1.5\%), to Wells in 9 of
474 (1.9\%), and to Doyle in 4 of 468 (0.9\%), against 26\%, 25\%,
22\% and 21\% of the model runs.  Runs asked to imitate twenty
different authors are attributed to two.  The generated texts resemble
one another more than they resemble their targets: each model writes in
a punctuation style of its own, and that style, not the requested one, is
what the attribution recovers.  These shares and counts are descriptive
summaries computed from the cached sequences and attribution records,
and were not fixed in advance.

\subsection{Positional analyses}
\label{sec:positional}
\label{sec:position}

Table~\ref{tab:length-attribution} showed that imitation weakens as texts
lengthen, and Section~\ref{sec:detection} will show that longer passages
are easier to detect.  The obvious reading is that more text means more evidence,
as it does for a human chunk.  But a generated run is not a sample of a
fixed style: it is produced left to right, and under the repeated prompt
every continuation still sees the excerpt and the author instruction,
together with a 500-word tail of the run's own output.  A 4,000-mark
prefix is not just more text; it is the opening plus a later stretch
written with that self-generated tail in view.  So a result that changes
with length has two possible causes, more text and later text, and the
prefix design cannot tell them apart.  The positional analyses serve two
purposes.  The first is a control: cutting the run into windows of equal
length holds the amount of text fixed, so that any difference between
windows is position alone.  The second is to watch the default style
take hold.
Table~\ref{tab:corner} shows where generated text ends up, pooled over
the first 2,000 marks; scoring each window separately shows whether the
model starts there or moves there.

Every result so far scored a generated run through its \emph{prefix}: the
first 1,000, 2,000 or 4,000 marks, so that a longer unit always contains
the shorter one.  To see whether a run changes along its length we cut
it differently, into five consecutive \emph{windows} of 1,000 marks each
(Section~\ref{sec:units}), and run two analyses.

The first tracks the run window by window.  Each window is scored against
the profile of the author the run was asked to imitate, built without the
prompt book, and four things are recorded: how far the window is from
that author, \(\KL(f(w)\,\|\,R^3_a)\); the \emph{margin}, how much closer
the window is to the requested author than to the nearest other author
(negative once a rival is closer); the requested author's \emph{rank}
among the twenty profiles; and whether the requested author is the
nearest of all, the \emph{hit}.  If the run drifts away from its target,
divergence and rank should rise and margin and hit should fall.  For each
author we take the mean change from the first window to the fifth, and
test the direction of change across the twenty authors with one-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, adjusted by Benjamini--Hochberg
\cite{benjamini1995} over the 16 tests formed by two models, two features
and four measures.  We also ask whether the predictions are collapsing
onto fewer authors as runs lengthen, using the entropy of the predicted
authors across runs, \(-\sum_a \pi_a \log \pi_a\), at each prefix length,
with human chunks as the control: a fall in entropy means the runs are
being attributed to a narrowing set of authors.

The second analysis is the control for length.  A later window differs
from an earlier one in position, but a longer prefix differs from a
shorter one in both position and amount of text, so the window analysis
alone cannot say which is doing the work.  We therefore take two blocks
of the same run and the same length, marks 1--2,000 and marks
2,001--4,000, score both under the verification protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:verification} with the same folds and thresholds, and
report the author-level paired difference in detection rate.  Two blocks
of equal length contain the same amount of evidence; any difference
between them is position.

\subsection{Drift away from the requested author}

Later text is further from the requested author and easier to detect
than earlier text of the same length, and the change happens at the
between the first and second 1,000-mark windows rather than gradually.
The windows show this from
the attribution side, and show the default style taking hold rather
than already in place.  Mean target
divergence across windows one to five is 0.262, 0.307, 0.295,
0.291 and 0.300 for Flash and 0.139, 0.156, 0.158, 0.151 and 0.148 for
Pro: Pro begins closer to its target than Flash and stays closer, and
both take their step between the first window and the second, then
settle.  Under \fThree the
first-to-fifth-window change in target divergence is \(+0.038\) for Flash
(95\% CI 0.019 to 0.058) and \(+0.010\) for Pro (\(-0.008\) to 0.023);
the margin to the nearest rival falls by 0.021 and 0.018, and the
target's rank among the twenty profiles worsens by 0.85 and 0.79 places.
The six directional tests on divergence, margin and rank survive family
adjustment (Table~\ref{tab:drift}).  The signed-rank tests are the
directional tests fixed in advance, and all six find the predicted
direction; but four of the six author-bootstrap intervals include zero
(Flash's rank, and Pro's divergence, margin and rank), so for those the
direction is established and the size is not.  Only Flash's divergence
and margin are resolved in both senses.  The hit rate, a coarse 20-way
indicator, moves by \(-3.25\) and \(-6.0\) points and does not survive
adjustment under \fThree (\(p_{\mathrm{BH}} = 0.199\) and 0.126), though
Pro's does under \fOne: the continuous geometry detects movement that
the hit indicator, with that one exception, is too blunt to register.

by 6.3 and 10.5 points.  And the drift has a direction.  Prediction entropy
for human chunks barely changes with length, 2.802 to 2.743 nats between
1,000 and 4,000 marks; for Flash it falls from 2.104 to 1.920 and for Pro
from 2.361 to 2.005, while the share of runs nearest Wells rises from
7.8\% to 14.7\% for Flash and from 22.0\% to 33.0\% for Pro between the
first window and the fifth.  These descriptive summaries show the default
style taking hold as a process, and the process is entirely within the
repeated-prompt batch.  It is
present from the start: in the first window the requested author is the
nearest profile for 13.0\% of Flash runs and 19.0\% of Pro runs, against
5\% by chance, and Wells is already the nearest for 7.8\% and 22.0\%.
What changes along the run is how firmly the default takes hold: by the fifth
window the hit rates are 9.8\% and 13.0\%, and the sharpest step for both
models is between the first window and the second.  That step is not
where the prompt leaves---the instruction is in view the whole time.  Nor
does it mark the first continuation call: a first response supplies a
median of 238 marks, so a 1,000-mark window spans a median of three
calls, and the model is already continuing its own text well before the
window closes.  What the windows establish is that the change happens
early and then stops, not why.
The model does not fail to imitate from the first word, but nor does it
succeed there; what little the prompt imparts is real but shallow, and
the model's own habits dominate from the second window onward.  Human text varies around its author's profile at the scale of
scenes (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}); generated text moves away from the
requested author's profile and toward the model's own.  Two practical
consequences follow: a detector should look at later text rather than
openings, and a test of imitation should not be run on a short sample.

\subsection{What the windows can and cannot say}

The repeated prompt removes fading from view by construction: every call
sees the excerpt and the author instruction.  Call boundaries are
recorded.  A within-batch regression of window target divergence on
window index, calls elapsed, and marks since the last boundary finds that
window position does not survive once call structure is in the model
(Flash calls-elapsed coefficient \(0.005\) [0.002, 0.008]; both
window-index intervals include zero).  For Pro the remaining term is
marks since the last boundary (\(0.023\) [0.0005, 0.046]).  Nineteen
authors supply a within-book human positional control; neither the
start-aligned nor the interior first-to-fifth change in target
divergence is resolved (0.012 [\(-0.011\), 0.038] and 0.029
[\(-0.003\), 0.075]), both intervals including zero.

What remains unseparated is why a new call, or the first marks after one,
should move the text: accumulated self-generated context, continuation
number, and proximity to termination still change together along a run.
``Drift'' names the observed association, not a residual mechanism.  The
other boundary is the comparison across batches: first-window target
divergence already differs between the repeated-prompt and single-prompt runs
(\(+0.029\) Flash, \(-0.043\) Pro), so no contrast against those older
runs carries a causal reading.

\FloatBarrier
\section{What the \texorpdfstring{\(G\)}{G} statistic measures}
\label{sec:g-statistic}

Recognising generated text means deciding whether a passage is consistent
with the author it claims to be, which is the verification objective of
Section~\ref{sec:verification} and its statistic \(G\).  Before that
statistic can be used for detection, its behaviour on genuine human text
has to be known.  This section shows that the textbook reference
distribution for \(G\) fails on human text by a wide margin, that the
shape of the failure identifies its cause, and that two consequences
follow: thresholds must be learned empirically, and a reference gains more
from a second book than from a longer passage of the first.

\subsection{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:overdispersion}

The nominal reference for \(G\) in Section~\ref{sec:verification} assumes
that a chunk is a sample from its author's profile.  Whether that holds is
tested through the ratio \(\phi(n) = E[G_n]/\mathrm{df}\), computed from
human chunks at each length with author-bootstrap intervals.
Two further quantities describe the failure of the nominal
reference: the empirical variance of \(G\) across chunks, whose nominal
value is \(2\,\mathrm{df}\), and the fraction of human chunks rejected at
the nominal 5\% level of the \(\chi^2_{\mathrm{df}}\) distribution, whose
nominal value is 5\%.  If instead each chunk \(c\) carries a latent
profile \(p_c\), its own local punctuation distribution, with mean
divergence \(\delta = E_c[\KL(p_c \,\|\, q)]\) from the reference,
then to first order \(E[G_n] = \mathrm{df} + 2n\delta\), so \(\phi\) grows
linearly in \(n\) and \(\delta\) is recovered as
\(\{\phi(n) - 1\}\,\mathrm{df}/2n\) at each length.  The expansion assumes
a stable chunk-level heterogeneity distribution and regular cell counts,
and is used as a diagnostic rather than a model.  A homogeneous
dependent process, in which marks depend on their neighbours under a rule
that does not change along the text, inflates \(\phi\) by a roughly
constant factor instead; the two hypotheses are separated by the shape of
\(\phi(n)\).
Writing \(\phi = 1 + (n-1)\rho\) gives the design effect of cluster
sampling \cite{kish1965}; \(\rho\) is estimated by least squares from
\(\phi(n) - 1 = \rho n\) with zero intercept across the three lengths, and
the effective number of independent marks is
\(n_{\mathrm{eff}} = n/\phi(n)\), with asymptote \(1/\rho\).  The estimates
and the aggregation rule are pinned as numbers of record.

\subsection{Observed overdispersion, heterogeneity, and effective marks}
\label{sec:observed-phi}

Table~\ref{tab:phi} reports the diagnostics on human chunks.  The nominal
value of \(\phi\) is one; the observed values climb from 8.6 to 22.4 for
\fOne and from 1.5 to 3.6 for \fThree as chunks lengthen from 1,000 to
4,000 marks, and every author-bootstrap interval excludes one.  At the
primary \fThree, 2,000-mark cell, \(E[G]\) is 201.6 against a nominal 90;
the variance of \(G\) across chunks is 29,673 against a nominal
\(2\,\mathrm{df} = 180\), which accounts for 0.6\% of it; and 71.1\% of
human chunks are rejected at the nominal 5\% level (95.3\% for \fOne).
At these lengths \(G\) is principally a measure of between-chunk
heterogeneity, not of sampling error.

The excess \(\phi - 1\) roughly doubles as \(n\) doubles for \fThree
(0.51, 1.24, 2.56): the linear signature of persistent profile mismatch,
not the constant inflation of a homogeneous dependent process.  Inverting
\(E[G_n] = \mathrm{df} + 2n\delta\) at each length gives \(\widehat\delta\)
between 0.023 and 0.034 nats across both features and a fourfold range of
lengths.  A value stable across feature dimension and length is what a
length-stable heterogeneity distribution would produce; it is consistent
with that reading rather than proof of it.

The same numbers bound how much independent evidence a chunk carries.
Observed \(n_{\mathrm{eff}} = n/\phi\) rises only from 117 to 179 for
\fOne and from 661 to 1,125 for \fThree as chunks quadruple.  A single
\(\rho\) fitted across the three lengths is 0.0056 for \fOne and 0.00063
for \fThree, giving ceilings \(1/\widehat\rho\) of 178 and 1,589 effective
marks; these are the numbers of record (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).  The
length-specific values \(1/\rho(n)\) run from 132 to 187 for \fOne, so
the fixed-slope ceiling is a descriptive summary rather than a bound; the
\fThree range, 1,565--1,954, is more stable.  The design conclusion that
follows, that more books are worth more than longer passages, does not
depend on the extrapolation; Section~\ref{sec:design-test} tests it
directly.

\subsection{Two books beat one longer book}
\label{sec:design-test}

The design conclusion was tested directly, with the predictions written
down before the run (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).  For each
author one book is held out and a reference is built from the other two
under a fixed budget in one of two ways: a single random window of
\(2N\) marks from one book, or a window of \(N\) marks from each.  Every
author's reference is built the same way, so the comparison is between
two ways of building a panel, not two references for one author.  The
held-out book is scored in 2,000-mark chunks by the attribution rule of
Section~\ref{sec:attribution}, over the six rotations per author and 20
random window draws.  Books shorter than \(2N\) drop out, so the panel
has 20 authors at \(N \le 1{,}000\), 18 at 2,000 and 17 at 4,000.

Table~\ref{tab:design-test} gives the result.  Two books beat one at
every budget, by five to eight points of accuracy, with 14--19 of the
authors favouring the split and every interval excluding zero; the
divergence from a held-out chunk to its true author's reference falls by
13--17\% at every budget.  Two books of 1,000 marks match one book of
4,000 (difference \(+0.019\), interval \(-0.021\) to \(+0.058\)), so for
a fixed budget of reference text, splitting it across two books is worth
about a doubling of that budget.  Across the eight design-by-budget
cells the effective-mark prediction \(n/\phi(n)\) orders the accuracies
almost perfectly (Spearman 0.976): \(\rho\), fitted only to the
overdispersion curve, predicts which reference designs work and in what
order.

What the test does not support is the mechanism the ceiling suggests.
If a single passage saturated, the advantage of two books would grow
with \(N\); it does not (5.5, 7.9, 8.2 and 4.8 points from \(N = 500\) to
4,000, with every difference between budgets inside its interval).  Two
books help at every budget because books differ from one another, not
because a passage of 8,000 marks has run out of new information; that is
also what the between-book divergence of Section~\ref{sec:simulation}
says.  The sixteen sensitivity cells (rivals with full references, middle
windows, \fOne, 1,000-mark test chunks) give gaps of \(+0.05\) to
\(+0.12\), all but one with intervals excluding zero.

\subsection{Simulation: three artificial authors}
\label{sec:simulation}
\label{sec:simmethods}
The diagnostics above showed that real chunks vary far more than
sampling allows, and that the excess grows with length: an author is a
mixture of local styles.  It did not say what produces the mixture.  We
now build three artificial authors and ask whether any of them produces
the same pattern: one that draws marks independently, one that follows a
first-order Markov chain, and one that switches between a dialogue mode
and a narration mode.  The question is whether any of these is
enough to reproduce the observed growth and tail of \(\phi\).
The answer bears on two decisions: whether a
model-based null could replace the empirical calibration of
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, and whether the ceiling on effective marks
above reflects structure at a longer scale than a chunk.


The independent model draws each mark from the author's marginal
distribution.  The Markov model draws each mark from the author's
transition table, so it has exactly the kind of dependence that \fThree
represents.  The two-state model alternates between a dialogue mode and a
narration mode, each with its own transition table, staying in a mode for
a run of marks whose length is drawn from what real books show.
Quotation marks are used to label which mode each passage of a real book
is in.  That labelling was checked by hand before the inferential run: in
a blinded sample of 200 marks, 100 per mode, the automatic labels were
wrong on 6, a 3\% rate in each mode, against a pre-set limit of 10\%.
Fitted mean run lengths are 6.2--7.0 marks for narration and 8.4--8.8 for
dialogue across folds.

Each model is fitted on the books of four author folds and then used to
generate synthetic books at the lengths of the held-out fold's real ones,
so that two synthetic books form a reference for a third, as in the
three-book rotation of Section~\ref{sec:dc}.  The independent and Markov
models are scored against both the true simulating distribution and a
reference estimated from the synthetic books, as
in the real analysis.  The two-state model is pushed harder than reality:
64 variants cross eight run-length multipliers with eight levels of
contrast between the modes, with 250 replicates each and 2,000 at the
fitted point.  For every chunk, real or simulated, we also record its
\emph{excess divergence}, \((G - \mathrm{df})/2n\): how far it sits
above the textbook expectation, per mark, the chunk-level counterpart of
\(\delta\) and compare the human and simulated distributions of this
quantity by two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance and by their upper
tails.

\subsection{No sentence-scale process reproduces the excess}

Table~\ref{tab:simulation} sets the simulated \(\phi\) curves beside the
observed one.  Neither of the two homogeneous models produces any excess
at all: for \fThree both stay below one at every length, the finite-table
effect of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}.  For \fOne at 2,000 marks the
Markov model adds \(\phi - 1 = 0.25\) over independence, against an
observed excess of 12.41 or about 2\%.  Dependence between neighbouring
marks is real, but it is not where the excess comes from.

The two-state model does no better at the settings measured from real
books: \fThree \(\phi\) is 0.76, 0.88 and 1.02 against the observed 1.51,
2.24 and 3.56, and its fitted slope \(\rho\) is effectively zero.  Even
the most extreme of the 64 variants ( runs four times longer and modes
twice as different as reality, the corner of the grid farthest from the
fitted process ) reaches only \(\rho = 0.000163\), 26\% of the observed
0.000629 (Figure~\ref{fig:hsmm-sweep}).  The tails fail in the same way:
at \fThree/2,000 the KS distance between the human and simulated excess
distributions is 0.659, and the simulated 95th percentile is 0.007 against
a human 0.095, a tail fourteen times too light (Figure~\ref{fig:f3-tail}).

\subsection{Where the heterogeneity must live}

The two-state model fails because of scale.  If a book switches mode every
seven to nine marks, a 2,000-mark chunk contains roughly 250--300
switches, and by the time a chunk is that long every chunk has about the
same mix of the two modes: the switching averages out long before the
analysis scale.  What does not average out is the mix itself.  The
dialogue fraction of the 60 panel books ranges from 0.21 to 0.88, far more
than the fitted process produces within a chunk.  The pairwise divergences
of the settings grid say the same thing directly: at \fThree/2,000, two
human chunks from the same book are 0.056 nats apart on average, two
chunks from different books by the same author 0.107, so about half of
what separates an author's chunks is which book they come from.  The
excess therefore lives at the scale of scenes, chapters, books, genres or
editorial conventions.

An exploratory extension of the sweep, run after the main analysis and
outside it, puts a number on that scale.  The two modes are far from
being the limiting factor: if every chunk were entirely one mode, the
excess would be about 0.15 nats, five times the observed 0.028, so the
main sweep fails on run length rather than on contrast.  Keeping the
contrast at its measured value and lengthening the runs, the simulated
\(\rho\) reaches the observed 0.00063 when modes persist for about 250
marks (five-fold mean 0.00065 at a mean run of 244 marks; 0.00049 at
183).  A two-mode contrast of dialogue size, persisting for a page or two
rather than a sentence, is therefore enough to reproduce the level of the
excess.  It does not reproduce its shape: at every single run length the
simulated curve is flatter than the observed one, too high at 1,000 marks
and too low at 4,000 (1.86, 2.58, 3.38 at 244 marks against 1.51, 2.24,
3.56), so something slower than a scene is varying as well, which is what
the same-book and different-book divergences above already indicate.
\placeholder{Exploratory: one seed per fold, computed from the fitted
parameters but not part of the main grid.  To be replaced by an
extended-sweep phase of the pipeline (run lengths 8--96 times measured,
five folds, three seeds, fixed before running) before these numbers are
cited.}  A model that could reproduce both level and shape would need a
scene-scale state together with a slower book-scale one; it is left as
future work rather than fitted after seeing this failure.

Two consequences carry forward.  No sentence-scale null is available for
\(G\), so the detection thresholds of Section~\ref{sec:detection} must be
learned from other authors rather than derived from a model.  And because about
half of the variation is between books, a reference gains more from a
second book than from a longer passage of the first, which is what
Section~\ref{sec:design-test} finds at every budget.

\subsection{Consequence: empirical calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

Part of the shortfall is the finite table itself.  Under simulated
independent marks with an estimated reference
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation}), \(\phi\) for \fThree is 0.69, 0.80 and
0.92: sparse 100-cell tables have not reached their asymptotic form at
these lengths, so the nominal reference is wrong even before heterogeneity
enters, and in the opposite direction.  A rescaled nominal \(p\)-value
would therefore not repair it.  Thresholds are instead learned on other
authors (Section~\ref{sec:verification}).  At a 5\% target the held-out
false-positive rate is 5.36\%, with an author-bootstrap interval from
3.98\% to 14.52\%: the point estimate transfers, but with only 20 authors
the uncertainty remains material.

Smoothing is the only free constant on the verification path, and
it fixes how far a single unobserved transition can push a score into the
tail where the thresholds sit.  Across \(\epsilon \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}\) the
fold thresholds move by at most 2\%, the primary AUC by at most 0.005, and
the true-positive rate by at most 2.5 points, so the detection result is a
property of the data rather than of the pseudo-count.

\section{Generated text is detectable}
\label{sec:detection}

\subsection{Most generated passages are flagged}

Section~\ref{sec:llm-attribution} showed that generated text does not land
on its requested author but in a region of its own.  In this section we explore
whether that region is far enough from every human author for generated
text to be recognised.  Detection is verification applied to generated
text: the test of Section~\ref{sec:verification} asks whether a passage is
consistent with prior writing by the author it claims to imitate, and a
generated passage that fails is flagged.  The thresholds are learned empirically on
other authors for the reasons stated in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, and are calibrated as described in
Section~\ref{sec:verification}.  The detector needs no labelled generated
text, no cooperation from the generator, and no access to model
probabilities.  Unlike a general AI detector it needs a named claimant
and reference books, and it cannot say whether an inconsistent passage
came from a language model or from a different human: it is a narrow,
interpretable verification design, not a universal classifier.

We find that generated text is detectable, and that how easily depends on
the model and on how much of the text the detector is given.
Table~\ref{tab:detection} reports both across the three lengths.

The AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen generated passage scores
higher than a randomly chosen human chunk.  It rises with length for both
models, from 0.922 to 0.974 for Flash and from 0.776 to 0.879 for Pro, and
stays far above the 0.5 of an uninformative test everywhere in the grid.
Because it does not depend on where the threshold falls, it is the cleaner
comparison across lengths.  At the longest length a Flash passage looks
less like its claimed author than a genuine chunk does about
thirty-eight times in forty, and a Pro passage about seven times in eight.

Turning scores into decisions costs some of that separation, because a
decision uses one threshold rather than averaging over all of them.  We
set that threshold so that 5\% of genuine human chunks are flagged, which
places it at the far right of the human score distribution; a generated
passage is caught only if it scores above all but the most extreme
twentieth of human text.  At that cost the share of generated passages
flagged rises with length from 58.2\% to 91.0\% for Flash and from 19.5\%
to 54.0\% for Pro.  The two models are ordered the same way at every
length and by a wide margin: Flash's default style is far enough from most
authors' own writing to be recognised four times in five at 2,000 marks,
whereas Pro's, which Section~\ref{sec:llm-attribution} showed to be the
less strange of the two, is caught little more than one time in three.

The gap between the two measures is a statement about where the generated
text sits, not about whether it differs.  Dividing each score by its fold
threshold puts the operating point at one: at 2,000 marks the median
human chunk scores 0.33, the median Flash run 1.52 and the median Pro run
0.88.  The typical Flash run clears the bar and the typical Pro run does
not, yet Pro still scores well above typical human text.  What separates
the two models at this operating point is therefore not whether their
output is unusual but by how much.  Relaxing the false-alarm rate
recovers the difference: at a 10\% rate 55.0\% of Pro passages are
flagged, at 20\% three quarters, and catching 80\% of them requires
accepting false alarms on 23\% of genuine chunks.  Short Pro passages are
the hardest case in the study, flagged one time in five at the 5\%
operating point, although even there the score separates the two
populations at AUC 0.776.

The operating point itself is only approximately transferable.  On
held-out authors the realised false-positive rate is 4.99\%, 5.36\% and
7.11\% at the three lengths, so the longest row runs looser than the
target.  This is the calibration failure of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
in operational form: the threshold is a quantile estimated on sixteen
calibration authors and applied to four held-out ones, and the human score
distribution grows more dispersed as chunks lengthen (\(\phi = 1.51\),
2.24 and 3.56 in Table~\ref{tab:phi}), so the quantile transfers less well
at exactly the lengths where detection is otherwise strongest.

The rate at any one length is not a fixed property of a model.  Both
rates rise with length (Section~\ref{sec:position-detection}), and the
single-prompt runs of Appendix~\ref{app:superseded}, scored by the same
detector with the same thresholds, are flagged at 75.5\% and 71.5\%.  A
quoted true-positive rate travels no further than the generation
conditions it was measured under.

The intervals on the detection rates are wide, and the width is not a
matter of sample size: 600 generated passages cannot narrow an interval
that is set by 20 authors, because detection differs by author far more
than by passage.  Per author, the share of imitations flagged runs from
5\% to 100\% for Flash and from 0\% to 100\% for Pro.  Every Flash
imitation is caught for nine authors and every Pro imitation for three,
while no Pro imitation at all is flagged for six---Bailey, Dickens, Dell,
Goodwin, Austen and Bowen---and thirteen of the twenty are at or below
one half.  The false alarms concentrate in the same way: 16 of 18
Wilde chunks and 10 of 22 Wells chunks are flagged as not their author's,
and these are the two least self-consistent authors of
Section~\ref{sec:human}.  Concentration by rate is not concentration by
volume: Charlotte Bront\"{e} contributes 16 flagged chunks as well, from a
much larger 120 and at a rate of 13.3\%.  The test flags what does not look like the
claimed author, whether the reason is a language model or an author who
does not resemble himself.

If the book being scored is allowed into its own reference, it is compared

serves as the threshold falls with them, and more generated text ends up
above it.  Table~\ref{tab:leakage-grid} shows how much.  Across both
features, three lengths and two models, the pooled reference raises the
detection rate in all 12 cells, by 9.0 to 33.0 points; at \fThree and
2,000 marks Flash goes from 81.2\% to 97.8\% and Pro from 38.5\% to
63.5\%.  The gain is largest for Pro, whose output sits closest to the
human range, where a small move in the threshold carries many passages
across it.

The second leakage channel is much smaller.  Setting one threshold on all
human scores rather than cross-fitting it out of author moves the

movement being \(-6.5\) points for Pro at \fOne and 4,000 marks.
Reference leakage is therefore the larger of the two channels by a factor
of about five, and the one that a reported rate most depends on.

The gain is not one a practitioner could use.  A document under suspicion
is never part of the profile it is tested against, so it always scores as
it does under leave-one-book-out; what the pooled policy changes is the
human chunks that set the threshold, which are made to look more
self-consistent than genuinely new writing by the same author would.  A
threshold calibrated that way sits too low, and a fresh, honest document
by the claimed author---which, being new, is no more part of the
reference than the suspect document is---could be flagged far more often
than the 5\% the procedure advertises.  The pooled row buys
a higher detection rate by making its false-alarm rate fictional.  Holding
more of an author's genuine writing does help, but through the reference
rather than through the leak: Section~\ref{sec:design-test} shows that a
second book is worth more than a longer passage of the first.



\subsection{Later text is more detectable at equal length}
\label{sec:position-detection}

Table~\ref{tab:detection} showed detection improving with length for both
models.  Part of that gain is simply more evidence, as it would be for a
human chunk.  But a 4,000-mark prefix is not more of the same text: it
contains the first 2,000 marks and then 2,000 more, written later in the
run.  Only 19 authors supply complete
4,000-mark chunks.

  \centering

    \resulttable{tables/inference_v3/position_matched.tex}}
  \caption{Equal-length out-of-author detection at 2,000 marks on the
  repeated-prompt runs.}

The equal-length comparison of Section~\ref{sec:positional} is the
control.  If detection were only a
matter of evidence, the two halves of a run would be equally detectable;
they are not.  Holding the length at 2,000 marks and moving from marks 1--2,000 to marks 2,001--4,000 of the
same run raises the detection rate by 6.25 points for Flash (95\% CI
1.5--12.0, adjusted \(p = 0.012\)) and 10.5 for Pro (4.0--17.5,
\(p = 0.010\)), and the later window on its own accounts for most of
the gain from doubling the length: 87.8\% against 91.0\% for Flash and
47.5\% against 54.0\% for Pro.  Window by window at 1,000 marks the
detection rate rises from 58.3\% to 73.3\% for Flash and from 19.5\% to
24.0\% for Pro between the first and fifth windows, with the whole of the
rise between the first window and the second (75.8\% and 27.5\%) and no
further gain after it.  So the length gain is
largely a position gain: what makes a long passage detectable is that it
reaches into the later part of the run, where the text sits further from
the requested author.

\section{Provenance and reproducibility}

\subsection{Campaigns and prompts}

The Flash configuration uses \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}; the Pro configuration
uses \texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}.  Both set temperature to 1.0 and cap each call
at 8,192 output tokens.  The versioned generation
script contains the initial and continuation templates and the deterministic
middle-excerpt and source-rotation rules.  Campaign summaries retain each
run's source identity and processed output; the extension additionally
retains raw responses and a hash of the prompt protocol.  Discovery metadata
do not contain the corresponding prompt hash or call timestamps, and the
four earlier campaigns do not retain call boundaries; the positional
campaign records every boundary, which is what makes the regression of
Section~\ref{sec:position} possible.  Source identity nevertheless permits the
prompt-source book to be excluded when constructing the target reference.

\subsection{Pinned artifacts}
\label{sec:provenance}

Analysis and panel configurations, the parse cache, assembled generation
conditions, package versions, git state and every declared output are
pinned by SHA-256 in a manifest; the primary seed is 20260908.  The feature
and distance code is the antecedent study's \cite{darmon2021}, vendored
unchanged.  Regeneration from the cached sequences requires no model calls.

\subsection{Reproducibility record}
\label{app:repro}

The canonical inference manifest pins the analysis and author-panel
configurations, parsed cache, assembled generation conditions, Python and
package versions, git commit, and every declared output by SHA-256.  The
position-matched and process-simulation manifests independently hash their
inputs and outputs.  The reference-design test of
Section~\ref{sec:design-test} has its own manifest in the
\texttt{reference\_design} results directory, and its predictions were
recorded in a campaign file, \texttt{reference\_design\_test.json},
before the run.  The process package additionally records the 200-item
manual segmentation validation, frozen parameter artifact, effective
simulation settings, and source hashes.  Reproducibility verification checks
the artifact chain and manuscript dependencies; regeneration does not require
new model API calls once the assembled text conditions and parse cache exist.

\section{Reference audit}
\label{app:audit}

Every reference construction call records the scored author, scored source
book, requested exclusion, and books included in the resulting profile.
Automated checks resolve byte-identical aliases and fail if the excluded
content remains in the reference.  The canonical inference package contains
2,100 audited profile-build rows.  The pooled-all-books policy is generated by
a separate, explicitly labelled path and cannot be mistaken for the primary
leave-one-book-out rows in the output tables.

\section{Superseded analyses}
\label{app:superseded}

The earlier four- and ten-author studies served as discovery analyses.  They
motivated whole-book exclusion, the length grid, model comparison, and
positional windows, but they do not contribute observations to the present
headline uncertainty calculations.  In particular, earlier statements that
one model imitated several authors or that Flash showed no drift are
superseded by the 20-author, author-blocked analysis.  Retaining this history
as an appendix distinguishes hypothesis development from confirmatory
interpretation without pooling incompatible candidate sets.

A second, earlier batch of 600 runs used a single-prompt protocol: the
excerpt and the author instruction appeared in the first call only, and
every later call carried a 500-word tail of the run's own output.  At the
\fThree, 2,000-mark cell Flash was attributed to its requested author
8.75\% of the time (3.25--15.75\%) and Pro 8.0\% (1.0--19.5\%), both
intervals reaching chance; the screened arm gave 21.0\% and 12.0\%, the
general arm 14.0\% and 7.0\%, and the paired Flash-minus-Pro difference
was \(+0.75\) points (\(p=0.395\)).  Those runs concentrated on Wells
(148 of 400 Flash runs and 115 of 200 Pro runs) and drifted along a run
by \(+0.092\) and \(+0.168\) in target divergence, against \(+0.038\)
and \(+0.010\) under the repeated prompt.  The two batches were generated
several weeks apart, and first-window target divergence---where the two
protocols send an identical prompt---already differs between them
(\(+0.029\) for Flash, \(-0.043\) for Pro, neither interval including
zero).  The single-prompt runs therefore establish the same deficit
independently, but no difference between the batches can be read as an
effect of the protocol.  Detection rates for both batches are given in
Section~\ref{sec:detection}.
