“We don’t have ninety seconds,” Frank warned, as another series of explosions rocked their prison. The ceiling above them was beginning to crack. “Kane is going to bring this whole mountain down on top of us.”

“Just keep us level, Frank,” Jack ordered. He keyed the comms. “Kane! You want a deal? Let’s talk.”

Kane’s laugh was cold and devoid of humor. “I am not in the mood for negotiations, Wingar.”

“You should be,” Jack bluffed, trying to buy Salty the time he needed. “This cavern is more unstable than you think. You’ve sealed us in, but you’ve also sealed yourself out. My ship is a Mole. It’s designed to dig. What do you think happens when I point my drill at that wall and get out the other side, leaving you trapped in a geode that’s about to implode?”

There was a brief silence on the other end. Jack had planted a seed of doubt. Kane was arrogant, but not stupid. The thought of being entombed with his prize just out of reach would be intolerable to him.

“You are bluffing,” Kane’s voice boomed, but it had lost some of its conviction.

“Am I?” Jack retorted. “There’s a whole network of tunnels in this asteroid. You might catch us eventually, or you might be stuck in this glowing bauble forever. Your call.”

“Thirty seconds,” Salty whispered urgently.

“What are your terms?” Kane asked, his voice dangerously soft.

“Just buying a little time,” Jack said, winking at Frank. He cut the comms link. “Salty, now!”

“Emitters charged!” Salty shouted. “Eleanor, are you sure about this frequency?”

Eleanor nodded, her hand still on the crystal. “I’m sure.”

“Frank, get us as close to the forward blockage as you can,” Jack commanded.

Frank nudged the Mole forward until its nose was nearly touching the wall of fallen rock and crystal. “This is it.”

“Salty, do it!”

Salty slammed a large, red button on his console. There was no sound at first, only a deep, powerful thrum that vibrated up through the soles of their feet. The Mole’s lights flickered as power was shunted to the sonic emitters. Outside, the world began to change.

The crystals embedded in the rock wall in front of them began to glow, their light intensifying from a soft shimmer to a blinding radiance. A low hum filled the air, quickly rising in pitch to a piercing, unbearable shriek. The vibrations grew more violent. Small crystals shattered into dust. Larger ones began to crack and splinter.

“It’s working!” Eleanor cried over the noise.

The effect was not limited to their small tunnel. The resonant frequency was propagating through the entire geode. On the main viewscreen, which showed a rear view of the tunnel entrance, they could see the Juggernaut being shaken violently. Alarms blared from the massive ship, audible even from a distance. Kane had no idea what was happening. He was likely assuming the cavern’s collapse was accelerating on its own.

“Keep it going, Salty!” Jack yelled.

“The emitters are overloading! I can’t hold this for more than a few more seconds!” Salty screamed back, wrestling with his controls as sparks flew around him.

The rock and crystal wall in front of them did not just crumble. It disintegrated. It turned into a churning wave of pulverized dust and glittering fragments. The sonic energy had found the geologic frequency of the geode and torn it apart from the inside. A new tunnel opened before them.

“Go, Frank! Go now!” Jack roared.

Frank slammed the thrusters to full power. The Mole surged forward into the newly created passage, flying through a blizzard of crystal dust. The shriek of the emitters cut out as Salty performed an emergency shutdown. The sudden silence was almost as jarring as the noise had been.

They shot through the tunnel, which was wider and more stable than the one they had just left. It was a natural lava tube, one that Eleanor’s scans must have detected. Behind them, they heard a cataclysmic roar. The fissure they had been trapped in collapsed completely, followed by a significant portion of the main cavern.

Frank brought the Mole into a wider section of the lava tube and killed the main thrusters, letting them drift in the silence. He switched the viewscreen back to the rear camera. The image was shocking. The beautiful geode cavern was gone. It was a churning cauldron of collapsing rock, exploding crystal, and plasma discharges from the dying Juggernaut. Kane’s mighty warship was being buried alive. It struggled for a moment, its thrusters firing uselessly against the tons of rock pressing in on it. Then, with one final, titanic groan of stressed metal, it was swallowed by the mountain.

Silence descended in the cockpit of the Mole. For a long moment, no one spoke. They simply stared at the screen, at the place where their enemy had been.

Salty was the first to break the silence. “My emitters,” he whispered in an awed, horrified tone. “They’re completely slagged. The focusing lenses are melted.”

Frank let out a long, shaky breath and leaned back in his pilot’s chair. “Well, Jack,” he said, a slow grin spreading across his face. “When you make a plan, you don’t do it by halves, do you?”

Jack clapped a hand on Frank’s shoulder. “It wasn’t my plan. It was Eleanor’s.”

Eleanor looked up, startled. “I just felt the frequency. You all were the ones who…”

“Nonsense,” Jack cut in. “We would be a permanent part of that rock collection if it wasn’t for you. You saved us, Eleanor.”

A faint blush colored her cheeks, but she smiled, a genuine, relieved smile. She looked down at the satchel. The artifact within was quiet now, its pulsing energy subsided. It was safe. For now.

“Let’s not stick around to see if he can dig himself out,” Frank said, his pragmatism returning. He brought the thrusters back online. “This lava tube should lead to the outer crust. From there, we can make for the Serpent’s Nebula. We can lose ourselves in the dust clouds for a while, make some repairs.”

“Good idea,” Jack agreed. He looked around the battered cockpit. The ship was damaged, they were exhausted, and they had just made an enemy of one of the most powerful and ruthless men in the galaxy. But they were alive, and they were free. He looked at Eleanor, who was watching him with an expression of gratitude and newfound respect.

“What is that artifact, Eleanor?” he asked quietly, the question that had been hanging in the air since this all began. “What is it that Kane was willing to tear apart a mountain for?”

She hesitated, then opened the satchel. She drew out the object. It was a sphere of polished, dark metal, about the size of her fist. It was covered in swirling, intricate etchings that seemed to shift and move if you stared at them for too long. In the dim light of the cockpit, it seemed to drink the light around it.

“I don’t know everything,” she admitted, her voice low. “My father called it the Keystone. He believed it was a map. Not a map of stars or planets, but a map of existence itself. A key to understanding the fundamental architecture of the universe.”

Jack, Frank, and Salty stared at the object, a sense of profound awe settling over them. They had not just stolen a treasure. They had stolen a miracle or a curse, and they could not yet tell which.

“A map of the universe,” Frank breathed. “No wonder Kane wanted it.”

“And he won’t stop coming for it,” Jack finished, his expression hardening. He looked out the viewscreen as the Mole continued its journey through the dark, silent tunnels of the asteroid. “This is just the beginning.” The darkness of deep space awaited them, promising both refuge and a thousand new dangers. Their journey had just begun.

The Mole shuddered as it burst from the asteroid’s rocky shell, a cork popping from a bottle of compressed chaos. Frank wrestled with the controls, his knuckles white against the worn grips. The ship groaned in protest, a symphony of tortured metal and popping circuits. A final, violent lurch threw them clear of the debris field. Outside the viewscreen, the last remnants of Kane’s mining operation tumbled in silence, a graveyard of ambition.

“Status report,” Jack ordered, his voice steady despite the tremor running through the deck plates.

From the small access hatch that led to the engineering section, Salty’s hulking form emerged. The Xylotian was a creature of few words and immense physical presence. His four thick arms were smudged with grease and his broad, flat face was set in a permanent expression of grim concentration. He made a series of low, guttural clicks and rumbles, a language few understood, before his translator implant crackled to life. “Primary power coupling is fused. Running on auxiliary. Life support is… functional. FTL drive is… questionable. Very questionable.”

“Define questionable,” Frank grumbled, coaxing the ship into a slow, limping trajectory.

“If we attempt a jump now,” Salty’s translated voice buzzed, “there is a seventy three point four percent probability we will rematerialize as individual atoms spread across a parsec.”

“I’ll take that as a no, then,” Frank said dryly. He glanced at the navigation charts. “The Serpent’s Nebula is two days at this speed. Assuming we don’t fall apart first.”

“We’ll make it,” Jack said, though he wasn’t sure if he was convincing anyone, including himself. He turned to Eleanor, who was still cradling the satchel containing the Keystone. Her eyes were wide, fixed on the starfield rushing toward them. She had faced down a psychic storm and the fury of a tyrant, but the sheer, unforgiving emptiness of space seemed to hold a different kind of terror for her.

“He’ll have people everywhere,” she whispered, not looking at him. “Ports, stations, trade routes. There will be bounties.”

“Let them come,” Jack said, his voice softer now. “We’re ghosts. The Mole isn’t a registered vessel, and our transponder codes are… flexible. In the nebula, we’ll be one more wisp in the fog.”

She finally turned her gaze to him. The fear was still there, but beneath it was a core of steel he hadn’t seen before. “My father… he ran for years. He always said the hardest part wasn’t the running. It was the moment you had to stop, and you didn’t know if the ground beneath your feet was solid.”

“We’ll find solid ground,” Jack promised.

The next two days passed in a tense, weary rhythm. Frank flew with a surgeon’s delicacy, nursing the damaged ship through the void. Salty spent most of the time in the sweltering engine room, his low clicks and the clang of tools the only sign of his tireless work. Jack and Eleanor took turns on watch, their conversations brief and practical, orbiting the one subject they were all trying to avoid. The Keystone.

It sat on the small galley table, a sphere of perfect, silent darkness. It didn’t pulse anymore. It didn’t hum. It just sat there, absorbing the light, a miniature black hole in their cramped reality. Yet its presence was immense. It was the reason they were here, the reason they were hunted, the reason their lives had been irrevocably shattered and rebuilt into this strange new shape.

On the third day, the edge of the Serpent’s Nebula appeared. It was not a gentle mist. It was a chaotic maelstrom of cosmic dust and ionized gas, a cosmic bruise of violent purples, angry reds, and sickly, glowing greens. Vast, stellar filaments twisted like tortured spirits, and dark nebulae blotted out the stars behind them, creating pockets of absolute blackness.

“Ugly, ain’t it?” Frank remarked as he carefully guided the Mole into the outer tendrils. The ship immediately began to buffet, rattled by gravimetric distortions and stellar winds. “Sensors are already getting squirrely. This is like flying blind in a hurricane.”

“That’s the point,” Jack said. “If we can’t see out, they can’t see in.”

Frank found them a haven, of sorts. A hollowed out asteroid, billions of years old, adrift in a relatively calm pocket of the nebula. It was large enough to conceal the Mole completely. He settled the ship down on the asteroid’s floor with a final, weary sigh from the landing gear. He cut the main thrusters, and a profound silence fell over the ship, broken only by the hum of life support and the faint, ever present creak of stressed metal.

“Home sweet home,” Frank muttered, slumping in his chair. “So. Who’s hungry for nutrient paste?”

For a week, they existed in this suspended state. Salty cannibalized non essential systems to reinforce the hull and patch the worst of the damage. Frank recalibrated the ship’s stealth systems, layering false signatures and ghost signals to confuse any long range scans. Jack took inventory of their supplies, the grim arithmetic confirming they could last a month, perhaps two, if they were careful.

Eleanor spent her time with the Keystone.

At first, she just watched it. Then, tentatively, she would touch it, her fingers tracing the swirling etchings that covered its surface. They felt cool, impossibly smooth. She expected some reaction, a psychic jolt or a sudden vision, but there was nothing. It was inert, a beautiful but dead object.

Frustration grew. This was the key to everything, the map of existence, and it was as silent as the grave. One evening, fueled by exhaustion and a growing sense of desperation, she did something different. She didn’t just touch it. She opened her mind to it. The way she had on the asteroid, when she felt for the frequency. She didn’t push, she didn’t demand. She listened.

She sat cross legged on the cold deck of the cargo bay, the Keystone resting in her lap. She closed her eyes, shut out the hum of the ship, the distant clang of Salty’s tools, the worried thoughts that were her constant companions. She reached out with her consciousness, a thread of silent inquiry.

For a long time, nothing happened.

Then, a flicker.

It wasn’t a sound or an image. It was a feeling, a change in the texture of her own thoughts. The etchings on the sphere began to glow, not with an external light, but with an internal, soft blue luminescence. The light projected itself onto the walls of the cargo bay.

Jack found her like that. He had come to tell her it was her turn for watch, but stopped dead in the hatchway. The cargo bay was no longer a drab metal box. It was a celestial sphere. The glowing lines from the Keystone had formed a web, a three dimensional lattice that filled the entire space. It looked like a star chart, but infinitely more complex. There were nodes of bright light, connected by shimmering pathways. Some pathways were thick and steady, others were faint and flickering. The whole structure was slowly, almost imperceptibly, rotating.

“Eleanor?” he said softly, afraid to break the spell.

Her eyes snapped open. They were unfocused for a moment. The glowing lattice wavered, then collapsed back into the sphere, leaving only a faint afterimage burned into his vision. A trickle of blood ran from her left nostril.

“Are you alright?” he asked, rushing to her side. He handed her a cloth from his pocket.

She wiped her nose, her hand trembling slightly. “I… I think so.” She looked down at the now dark sphere, then back up at him, her eyes alight with a mixture of terror and exhilaration. “It’s not a map. Not a flat map. My father was right, but he was thinking too small.”

“What is it?” Jack asked, his gaze fixed on the artifact.

“It’s a network,” she said, her voice breathy. “Everything. It’s all connected. Not just stars and planets. Ideas. Moments in time. People. You and I, we’re a connection. Our meeting, our escape, it’s a line in that… that web. When we escaped Kane, the line brightened. It strengthened.”

Jack stared at her, trying to process the enormity of what she was saying. “A map of cause and effect?”

“More than that,” she said, shaking her head. “It’s the structure. The architecture. It showed me… I saw us, on the asteroid. Not like a memory. I saw it from outside. I saw the line representing Kane’s pursuit, and how it almost intersected ours. I saw the line that was the lava tube, our path to escape.” She took a shuddering breath. “And I saw other lines. Lines leading away from this moment. Possible futures.”

A heavy silence settled between them. The implications were staggering. A device that didn’t just show you where you were, but where you could go, and what the consequences of that path might be.

“No wonder Kane wants it,” Jack finally said, his voice a low whisper. “You could predict markets. Win wars. You could rule the galaxy with that thing.”

“Or be driven mad by it,” Eleanor countered. She touched her temple. “It’s too much. The possibilities, they’re infinite. It’s like trying to drink an ocean.”

Their sanctuary was shattered a few days later. Not by Kane’s warships, but by a simple, brutal reality.

“The primary FTL capacitor is shot,” Salty’s translator buzzed. He held up the component. It was a crystalline matrix the size of Jack’s head, but it was cracked down the middle, a dark, ugly fissure marring its delicate structure. “Repairs are not possible. I have bypassed it with the secondary unit, but it is a short range emergency system. It will burn out after one or two jumps. We are effectively trapped in this system.”

Frank swore, a long, creative string of curses that echoed in the quiet cockpit. “A new capacitor. A Mark IV Tantalus array? You can’t just pick those up at the corner store. They’re rare. Military grade.”

“There is one place we might find one,” Jack said, pulling up a nebula chart. He zoomed in on a blinking icon deep within the densest part of the dust clouds. “The Gantry.”

Frank groaned. “The Gantry? Jack, that’s not a space station, it’s a cesspool with an airlock. It’s run by the Syndicate. Every bounty hunter, smuggler, and cutthroat from here to the Core Worlds uses it as a watering hole.”

“Which is why no one will be looking for us there,” Jack countered. “It’s neutral ground, of a sort. The Syndicate doesn’t care about Imperial warrants. They only care about profit. We go in quiet, find a parts dealer, make a trade, and get out.”

“And what do we trade with?” Frank asked, throwing his hands up. “Our good looks? We’re broke, Jack. We used the last of our credits getting that intel on Kane’s operation.”

Jack glanced at the cargo bay. They had one thing of immense value, but using it for a trade was unthinkable. “We’ll find something. There’s always work for people willing to get their hands dirty on a place like The Gantry.”

Eleanor, who had been listening quietly, spoke up. “We don’t have a choice, do we?”

Her question hung in the air, a simple statement of their desperate reality. Jack looked at the faces of his small crew. Frank, the worried pragmatist. Salty, the silent pillar of strength. Eleanor, the reluctant oracle. They were all in this together, chained to a fate dictated by a small, dark sphere.

“No,” Jack said. “We don’t.”

The journey to The Gantry was short and tense. Frank flew the Mole with the stealth systems on full, a ghost slipping through the glowing veils of gas. The station appeared on their screens as a spiderweb of docking arms, gantries, and habitation modules, all welded together with no apparent design, clinging to a massive, pitted asteroid that served as its anchor. Lights from a thousand portholes and docking bays glittered like malevolent eyes in the gloom.

They found an anonymous berth in the lower levels, a section reserved for unsanctioned and unregistered ships. The air that cycled into the Mole when the airlock hissed open smelled of ozone, spilled fuel, and unidentifiable cooking spices.

“Alright,” Jack said, strapping a heavy blaster pistol to his thigh. “Salty, you’re with me. We’ll find a parts dealer named Scrax. He’s a Xylotian, deals in rare components. Maybe a little professional courtesy will get us somewhere.”

Salty gave a low rumble of assent, his large hands flexing.

“Frank, Eleanor, you stay here,” Jack continued. “Keep the engines on standby. If we’re not back in three hours, or if I send the signal, you leave. No questions asked. You take the Mole, you take the Keystone, and you disappear.”

“Jack…” Frank started to protest.

“That’s an order, Frank,” Jack said, his tone leaving no room for argument. He looked at Eleanor. “You have the most important job. Keep this ship and that artifact safe.”

She nodded, her expression grim. “Be careful. The Gantry… it feels wrong. Too many sharp edges.”

Jack and Salty stepped out into the chaotic swirl of the station. The main concourse was a river of bodies, a noisy, jostling collection of a dozen different species. Humans in worn vac suits haggled with four armed merchants. Hulking, reptilian mercenaries watched the crowds from the doorways of dimly lit bars. The air was thick with competing music, shouted deals, and the hum of countless languages.

They found Scrax’s shop in a dark alley off the main thoroughfare. It was less a shop and more a junk pile with a door. A faded, flickering sign above it read “Scrax’s Scraps”.

Scrax was, as advertised, a Xylotian, though much older and thinner than Salty. His four arms moved with a nervous energy, constantly sorting and rearranging piles of electronic components on his workbench.

“A Mark IV Tantalus array?” Scrax clicked his mandibles, the sound like rocks grinding together. His translator was a cheaper model than Salty’s, giving his voice a tinny, robotic quality. “Very rare. Very expensive. What do you have for trade?”

“We have skills,” Jack said. “We can acquire things. Run cargo. Provide security.”

Scrax let out a dry, hissing laugh. “Everyone on The Gantry has skills. Most of them involve a blade or a blaster. I need liquid assets. Credits. Or something of equivalent, verifiable value.” His multiple eyes settled on Salty. “A strong back. Perhaps a term of service? The Syndicate always needs enforcers.”

Salty tensed, his low rumble vibrating through the floor.

“He’s not for sale,” Jack said, his hand moving closer to his blaster.

It was then that Eleanor’s voice came crackling through his comm-bead, urgent and sharp. “Jack. Trouble. Three o’clock, your position. A Rodian. He’s been watching you since you entered the concourse. I can feel it… he’s recognized you. There’s a bounty.”

Jack didn’t turn. He kept his eyes on Scrax. “Salty,” he said in a low voice. “To your left.”

Out of the corner of his eye, Jack saw him. A green skinned Rodian, classic bounty hunter a dime a dozen. He was trying to be subtle, pretending to examine a tray of faulty power cells at a nearby stall, but his hand was already on the grip of his heavy disruptor pistol. He was raising his wrist comm, no doubt to call for backup and confirm the bounty.

There was no time for negotiation. No time for a plan.

“Now,” Jack said.

The alley exploded into motion. Jack kicked the table between them and Scrax, sending a shower of spare parts across the floor. He drew his blaster as he dove for cover behind a stack of rusted engine cowlings. Salty didn’t bother with cover. With a roar that was pure, untranslated Xylotian fury, he charged.

The Rodian got off one shot. A bolt of red energy sizzled past Jack’s head, melting a black scar on the wall behind him. Before he could fire again, Salty was on him. The Xylotian’s size was deceptive; he moved with a speed that belied his bulk. He grabbed the Rodian’s gun arm with one hand and lifted the bounty hunter clean off the ground with another. There was a sickening crack of bone, a strangled scream, and the Rodian’s blaster clattered to the ground.

Shouts were coming from the main concourse. The single blaster shot had drawn attention.

“We have to go!” Jack yelled, grabbing a handful of what looked like valuable data chips from Scrax’s counter as he ran. Scrax was huddled under his workbench, his four arms covering his head.

“My payment!” Jack shouted, tossing the chips at the terrified Xylotian.

They sprinted back the way they came, shoving their way through the now panicked crowd. Alarms started to blare across the station, a shrill, ugly sound.

“Frank, we’re coming in hot!” Jack yelled into his comm. “Get ready to lift!”

“Already on it!” Frank’s voice was a tight coil of stress. “Docking clamps are retracting. Just get your asses on board!”

They rounded the final corner to their docking bay. Two Syndicate enforcers in heavy armor were already there, raising their pulse rifles. Salty, still running on pure adrenaline, didn’t even slow down. He barreled into them like a living battering ram. The enforcers went down in a clatter of armor and surprised grunts.

Jack and Salty scrambled up the ramp into the Mole just as it began to lift from the deck. The ramp retracted, the outer door sealing with a clang and a hiss. Frank slammed the thrusters, and the ship shot out of the docking bay, scraping its side against the gantry with a sound like a giant tearing a sheet of metal.

Jack stumbled into the cockpit. Through the viewscreen, he saw The Gantry receding, a chaotic nest of lights and metal. A few smaller ships were already peeling away from the station, starting to give chase.

“Where to, skipper?” Frank asked, his eyes darting across his console, looking for an escape vector.

Jack looked at Eleanor. Her face was pale, but her eyes were clear. The brief, violent encounter had hardened something in her.

“Eleanor?” he asked. “Those possible futures you saw. The lines leading away from here. Is there one that doesn’t end with us getting caught?”

She closed her eyes for a moment, her hand resting on the satchel that held the Keystone. The artifact, as if sensing her need, gave a faint, warm pulse that only she could feel. A new set of lines bloomed in her mind, clearer than before. A single, bright pathway, leading away from the nebula, away from Imperial space, into the unknown regions. It was a faint line, a path of low probability, but it was steady. And at its end was a name she had read once in her father’s oldest journals. A myth. A ghost story for archaeologists.

She opened her eyes. “There is,” she said, her voice filled with a new, strange certainty. “A place my father was looking for. A research outpost from the Old Republic, lost for centuries. A repository of forbidden knowledge. The Keystone wants us to go there. It’s a place called Xylos.”

Salty, standing behind the pilot’s chair, let out a series of soft, wondering clicks. His translator remained silent.

Frank looked at the navigation charts, then at Jack. “Xylos? Jack, that’s just a legend. It’s on the other side of the Outer Rim. We’ll never make it on the backup capacitor.”

“We will if we can find another one on the way,” Jack said. His eyes met Eleanor’s. They had run, they had hidden, and they had been found. Now, for the first time, they were not just running away from something. They were running toward it.

He pointed to a flickering point of light on the star chart, a destination on the edge of charted space. “Frank, set a course. We’re going to Xylos.”

Frank’s knuckles were white where he gripped the flight yoke. The *Mole* shuddered again, a deep, groaning protest from its very frame. On the tactical display, three red triangles, the Syndicate pursuit ships, were closing the distance with alarming speed. Another two blinked into existence as they cleared the tangled superstructure of The Gantry. Five of them. Far too many for a beat up freighter with a failing power system.

“They’re faster than us, Jack,” Frank grunted, his voice tight. “And they’ve got a better turning radius. On an open run, we’re scrap metal.”

“Then don’t make it an open run,” Jack said, his own voice calmer than he felt. He leaned over the console, his eyes scanning the navigation chart. The Gantry was situated at the edge of a dense, chaotic asteroid field known to local spacers as the Serpent’s Teeth. It was a graveyard for rookie pilots and a haven for smugglers who knew its twisting, treacherous paths. “Get us in there. The Teeth.”

Frank shot him a look, a mixture of disbelief and grudging respect. “You’re crazy. One wrong move and we’ll be part of the graveyard.”

“Better than being a prisoner of the Syndicate,” Jack retorted. He looked back at Eleanor, who was gripping the back of the copilot’s chair, her face a mask of determined concentration. “Hold on.”

Frank didn’t argue further. He angled the ship, the main thrusters firing in a desperate, powerful burst. The *Mole* leaped forward, a wounded animal diving for cover. The first volley of plasma fire from the pursuit ships streaked past their cockpit, brilliant green lashes against the black of space. An alarm screamed from a side console.

“Shields are down to forty percent!” Frank yelled. “And that’s the last of the main capacitor’s juice. We’re on backup only now. It’s not going to recharge them.”

The first of the asteroids loomed in the viewscreen, a pockmarked giant of rock and ice, tumbling slowly through the void. Frank didn’t flinch. He threaded the *Mole* around it, the ship’s damaged port side coming so close that Jack could see the ice crystals fracturing on its surface. Then they were in.

The universe outside the cockpit became a maelstrom of grey and brown. Asteroids, from the size of a fist to the size of a small moon, crowded the space around them. Frank was no longer just a pilot, he was a dancer, his hands moving with an artist’s grace over the controls. The *Mole* dipped, rolled, and surged, its engines whining in protest. A chunk of rock the size of a groundcar scraped along the top of the hull with a deafening shriek of tortured metal.

“They’re following us in!” Jack said, watching the tactical display. Two of the red triangles had broken off, circling the edge of the field, but three were still hot on their tail. They were more cautious than Frank, their progress slower, but they were persistent.

Salty, who had been an unnervingly still presence behind them, suddenly let out a series of sharp, excited clicks. He pointed a long, three jointed finger at the forward view. His translator remained silent, but his intent was clear. Dead ahead, a massive asteroid, hollowed out by some ancient mining operation, was spinning on its axis. Its interior was a dark, gaping maw.

“What is he seeing?” Frank grunted, his eyes fixed on the path ahead.

“He sees a shortcut,” Jack interpreted, a grim smile touching his lips. “Through the gut. Think you can make it?”

Frank let out a short, bark like laugh. “At this point, what’s one more impossible maneuver?”

He pushed the thrusters. The *Mole* shot toward the spinning cavern. The Syndicate ships, seeing their intent, opened fire again. One shot clipped the rear stabilizer, sending the ship into a lurching fishtail. Eleanor cried out, stumbling against a bulkhead. Jack grabbed her arm, steadying her.

“Almost there,” Frank muttered, his focus absolute.

They plunged into darkness. The inside of the asteroid was a disorienting tunnel of jagged rock. The only light came from their own running lights, reflecting off crystalline deposits in the walls. For a few heart stopping seconds, they were flying completely blind, relying on Frank’s memory of the asteroid’s trajectory. Then, light bloomed ahead. They burst out of the other side, back into the chaotic dance of the Serpent’s Teeth.

Jack checked the tactical display. It was clear. “They’re gone. They must have missed the opening.”

Frank let out a long, shuddering breath and eased back on the thrusters, letting the *Mole* drift in the shadow of a particularly large asteroid. The silence in the cockpit was profound, broken only by the hum of the life support and the frantic beeping of a dozen different damage alarms.

“Damage report,” Jack said, his voice low.

“It’s not good,” Frank said, wiping a sleeve across his sweating brow. He tapped a few commands into his console. “That last hit took out the rear stabilizer. The port side is scraped to hell, we’ve got micro-fractures all along the hull. And the backup capacitor… Jack, it’s bleeding energy. The system is trying to divert power to recharge the main, but it’s like trying to fill an ocean with a leaky bucket. We’ve got maybe six hours of power left. Tops. After that, we’re a dead stick. Life support, lights, everything.”

The weight of their situation settled heavily in the small cockpit. They had escaped, but they were crippled, adrift, and on a rapidly ticking clock.

Salty, who had been observing the console over Frank’s shoulder, finally spoke. His translator crackled to life, the monotone voice a stark contrast to his earlier excitement. “The path to Xylos is long. This vessel will not complete the journey.”

“We know that, Salty,” Jack said, rubbing his tired eyes. “What we need is a solution.”

“There is a place,” Salty’s translator continued. The clicks that accompanied the words were softer, more thoughtful. “A nest of scavengers. A place of rust and ruin. It is called Port Muck. It is two hours from here at current speeds. Unsavory. But they have parts. Many parts. Broken ships sing songs of their components. I can hear them.”

Frank looked up from his console. “Port Muck? Salty, that place is a cesspool. It’s an entire moon dedicated to ship breaking, run by the worst dregs in the sector. We go there, we’re as likely to get stripped for parts ourselves as we are to find what we need.”

“It’s a risk,” Jack said, “but it’s a better one than floating here until the lights go out. We need a Type 4 Plasma Conduit. A new primary capacitor. Do you think we can find one there?”

Salty gave a slow nod, his large, dark eyes blinking. “A warship of the old Kalidoran Hegemony crashed there a cycle ago. A cruiser. They used Type 4 conduits. The wreck will sing loudly.”

Jack nodded. The decision was made. “Frank, plot a course. Keep it low and slow. The last thing we need is to show up on anyone’s long range sensors.”

As Frank began coaxing the wounded ship toward their new, temporary destination, Jack turned to Eleanor. She had retreated to a corner of the cockpit, her hand once again resting on the satchel at her side. She hadn't said a word during the entire chase. Her face was still pale, but the fear was gone, replaced by a deep, unsettling calm.

“You okay?” he asked softly.

She looked up, her eyes seeming to look straight through him for a moment. “When we were in the asteroid, I closed my eyes.”

“Probably for the best,” Jack said with a weak smile.

“No,” she said, shaking her head. “The Keystone… it showed me more. Not just the path, the line to Xylos. It showed me… glimpses. Fragments. A city made of living crystal, humming with a light I’ve never seen. Towers that pierced the clouds of a gas giant. I saw beings of pure energy and heard a language that was like music. And then I saw it all fall. A great darkness, a silence. It felt like a memory. A profoundly sad memory.”

She finally opened the satchel. The Keystone inside was no longer just warm. It was glowing, a soft, internal luminescence pulsing in time with a slow, steady heartbeat.

“My father thought Xylos was just a research outpost,” she whispered, her voice filled with awe and a touch of fear. “He was wrong, Jack. It wasn't just a repository of knowledge. It was the heart of something. Something that was lost. Something the Keystone wants to find again.”

Before Jack could process the sheer scale of what she was implying, Salty spoke again, his translator startling them both in the quiet cockpit.

“Xylos,” the monotone voice said. Salty moved to stand beside Eleanor, his gaze fixed on the glowing artifact. His own clicking became softer, a series of rhythmic taps that sounded almost like a chant. “In the tongue of my people, the name does not mean ‘forbidden knowledge’. It means ‘The Source’. Or ‘The Beginning’. The oldest stories say we were not always as we are. We were… more. We came from the star-tide, from a place of light. We came from Xylos. My people have forgotten. We are scavengers now. We pick at the bones of the galaxy. But we were once architects.”

Jack stared from the insectoid alien to the young academic, a sudden, dizzying sense of vertigo washing over him. This wasn't a simple treasure hunt anymore. He wasn't just smuggling a person and a relic. He had stumbled into the middle of a myth, a forgotten history that was clawing its way back into the present. He was in way, way over his head.

Two hours later, the *Mole* limped into orbit around Port Muck. It was less a port and more a planetary scab. The small moon was a single, sprawling junkyard, its surface almost completely obscured by the skeletons of ten thousand ships, piled on top of each other in a chaotic tangle of rusted plating and twisted girders. Dim lights flickered from makeshift settlements built into the hulls of larger wrecks, and the entire moon was shrouded in a thin, greasy haze of vented coolant and burnt atmosphere.

“Home sweet home,” Frank muttered, guiding them toward a dark patch on the moon’s far side, away from the main settlements. “Okay, here’s the plan. We can’t land. Our power signature is too unstable, and the landing gear is probably slag. I’ll keep us in a low, stationary hover, maybe fifty meters up. Salty, you said you can find this wreck?”

“Its song is strong,” Salty confirmed. “The metals are in agony. It is close.”

“Right,” Jack said, pulling on a heavy jacket and checking the charge on his blaster pistol. “Salty and I go down. Eleanor, you stay here with Frank. Keep the engines warm. If we’re not back in three hours, or if you hear from us to run, you get this ship out of here. Don’t wait. Don’t argue. Just go. Got it?”

Eleanor looked like she wanted to argue, but she saw the hard set of his jaw and simply nodded. “Be careful, Jack.”

Frank lowered the ramp part way, not daring to extend it fully. It hung at a steep angle, a precarious bridge to the junk-strewn ground below. A magnetic grapple line was their only way down.

“Try not to pick a fight with the locals,” Frank called down as Jack prepared to descend. “They eat people like us for breakfast.”

“Don’t worry,” Jack said, clipping the line to his belt. “I’m always polite.”

He rappelled down, landing with a soft crunch on a field of shattered plasteel. Salty followed, moving with a silent, spiderlike grace that belied his size. The air was thick with the smell of ozone, rust, and decay. The silence here was different from the silence of space. It was a heavy, waiting silence, full of the ghosts of dead ships.

Salty stood still for a moment, his head tilted as if listening to something only he could hear. His clicks were sharp and precise now, like a navigator taking readings. Then, without a word, he set off, weaving a path through the metallic canyons. Jack followed, his hand never straying far from his pistol. They passed hulking, shadowy shapes that were once proud cruisers, now just hollowed out husks. Eerie green and purple mosses grew on the metal, feeding off leaking power conduits.

After twenty minutes of walking, Salty stopped. He pointed a long finger toward a massive, jagged tear in the side of a mountain of wreckage. “In there. The cruiser. Its heart is still.”

They climbed into the wreck. It was a Kalidoran Hegemony ship, just as Salty had said. The design was militaristic and angular. Inside, emergency lights flickered on and off, casting long, dancing shadows. The air was stale, the metallic tang of old blood still hanging faintly in it.

They moved deeper, toward the engineering section. Salty’s clicks grew more rapid as they neared their goal. They rounded a corner and saw it. The engineering bay was a mess of torn cables and ruptured pipes, but in the center, on a raised platform, was the main power junction. And sitting within it, miraculously untouched, was a Type 4 Plasma Conduit. It was a dense, cylindrical object, a meter long, coiled with thick copper wiring and glowing with a faint, residual energy.

“There it is,” Jack breathed, a wave of relief washing over him. “Let’s get it and go.”

As Salty moved toward the platform to begin decoupling the conduit, a harsh, grating voice echoed through the bay.

“Well, well. Look what the junk-worms dragged in.”

Jack spun around, his blaster in his hand before he’d even fully registered the threat. Standing in the shadows at the far end of the bay were four figures. The one who had spoken was a large, brutish human with a scarred face and a crude cybernetic arm that ended in a three-pronged claw. He and his crew were dressed in piecemeal armor scavenged from a dozen different uniforms. They all held weapons, a motley collection of rusty slug-throwers and jury-rigged laser rifles.

“I don’t recall giving you permission to board my ship,” the scarred man growled, taking a step forward. “That conduit is mine. Everything in the ‘Iron Heart’ is mine.”

Jack kept his blaster level. “We’re not here for trouble. We just need the part. We can pay.”

The man laughed, a horrible, wet sound. “Pay? You’ve got nothing I want. Except maybe that fancy pistol. And whatever your pet bug is worth. Now drop your weapon, and we’ll make this quick. My boys are hungry.”

Behind him, Salty had frozen on the platform. The soft, wondering clicks he had made on the *Mole* were gone. They were replaced by a low, guttural chittering from deep in his thorax, a sound Jack had never heard before. It was not a sound of fear. It was a sound of rage.

Jack’s mind raced. He was outgunned. A firefight in this enclosed space was suicide. He needed a distraction, an advantage. He glanced up. Above them, a heavy maintenance gantry, half-torn from its mountings, hung precariously over the spot where the scavengers stood.

“Last chance,” Jack said, his voice steady. “Walk away.”

The scavenger boss just grinned, raising his own weapon. “Wrong answer.”

He never got to finish the thought. Jack didn’t fire at him. He fired at the weakened bolts holding the gantry. The shot ricocheted with a piercing whine. With a scream of shearing metal, the massive gantry tore loose and came crashing down.

The universe held its breath for a microsecond. Then the gantry fell.

It did not fall cleanly. It tore from the bulkhead with a symphony of metallic agony, a drawn out, ear splitting shriek of tortured steel. The scavenger boss’s grin vanished, replaced by a mask of pure, unadulterated shock. He had just enough time to throw himself backward, a clumsy, desperate dive. His crew were not so lucky. The one to his left looked up, his mouth a perfect ‘o’ of surprise, and was simply erased by the tons of falling metal. The impact was a cataclysm. The deck plates buckled and groaned, sending a shudder through the entire wreck of the ‘Iron Heart’. A thick cloud of rust dust and ancient debris erupted from the point of impact, instantly obscuring everything.

Screams echoed in the sudden, ringing silence. One was a man’s, high and short, abruptly cut off. The other was the continued protest of the ship’s dying structure.

“Salty, now!” Jack yelled, his own voice hoarse in the dust choked air. “Get the conduit!”

The chaos was his only cover. He ducked behind a large, overturned console, the smooth metal cool against his back. He could hear movement from the scavenger’s position, a pained grunt and the scrape of metal on metal. They weren’t all dead. He hadn’t expected them to be.

Salty did not need to be told twice. The furious chittering that had filled the air moments before was gone, replaced by a focused series of sharp, mechanical clicks. He moved with a speed that defied his bulk, skittering up the last few feet of the platform. His multiple limbs, usually so delicate in their manipulations, became powerful tools. Two of his larger forelimbs latched onto the plasma conduit while smaller, more dexterous appendages darted into the junction box, their sharp tips finding release catches and pressure clamps. Sparks showered down as he worked, a miniature fireworks display in the gloom.

A blaster bolt seared the air a foot from Jack’s head, splashing against the bulkhead behind him and leaving a glowing orange crater. He risked a peek. The dust was settling. The scavenger boss, the one with the cybernetic claw, was pushing himself up. The gantry had missed him, but a heavy support beam had pinned his prosthetic arm to the deck. He was roaring in a mixture of pain and fury, his face a contorted mess of rage. Of his three companions, two were clearly gone, little more than mangled shapes beneath the wreckage. The fourth, a skinny man with a wild look in his eyes, was scrambling to his feet, his slug-thrower leveled uncertainly.

“You’re dead!” the boss bellowed, his voice raw. “Both of you are dead!” He fumbled for the pistol at his hip with his good hand.

Jack didn’t give him the chance. He leaned out and fired two quick, precise shots. The first slammed into the skinny scavenger’s shoulder, spinning him around with a yelp and sending his weapon clattering across the floor. The second shot was aimed at the boss’s pistol. It was a risky shot, but Jack’s hand was steady. The bolt struck the weapon’s power pack, and it exploded in a shower of blue sparks, flinging the mangled grip from the man’s hand. He howled, clutching his burned fingers.

“Salty!” Jack shouted, the name a sharp bark.

There was a heavy, resonant *thunk*. Salty had freed the conduit. The glow from within it pulsed more strongly now that it was detached from the ship’s systems. The insectoid turned, the heavy cylinder held securely under his thorax by two pairs of his powerful legs. He was a terrifying sight, his multifaceted eyes glowing in the gloom, the prize held like some captured prey. He let out a series of low clicks, a question. *Which way?*

The way they had come was blocked by a furious, one armed scavenger and his wounded compatriot. Jack’s eyes darted around the ruined engineering bay. Wreckage and torn cables were everywhere. But there, on the far side, was a service corridor, its door half buckled but still open.

“That way!” Jack pointed. “Go!”

Salty moved, his gait surprisingly agile even with his heavy burden. He scurried off the platform and made for the corridor, his legs finding purchase on surfaces a human would have found impassable. Jack laid down another burst of covering fire, forcing the wounded scavenger to duck back behind a twisted stanchion. The boss was still struggling with his pinned arm, his curses echoing through the bay.

Jack backed away, keeping his blaster trained on them, following the path Salty had taken. He reached the corridor just as Salty disappeared around the first bend. Jack risked one last look back. The boss had given up on his arm. His eyes, burning with hatred, met Jack’s across the ruined space. He was reaching for something on his belt with his good hand.

Jack didn’t wait to see what it was. He ducked into the corridor and ran.

The passageway was even darker than the bay. The only light came from the faint, pulsing glow of the conduit Salty carried ahead of him, casting their shadows long and distorted against the walls. The air was thick with the smell of ozone and decay.

“They’ll follow,” Jack panted, his boots ringing on the metal deck.

Salty responded with a series of urgent clicks, his pace not slackening for a moment. He seemed to know these service ways, these forgotten arteries of the dead ship. He took a sharp right, then a left, navigating a maze of identical looking corridors. Behind them, Jack could hear the scrape and clatter of pursuit. The scavenger boss was free, and he was coming for them.

They burst into a wider area, a cargo transfer station. A massive, inert conveyor belt system dominated the center of the room. On the far side, another corridor promised a path forward. But as they started across the open space, a laser blast tore a hole in the deck plating right at Salty’s feet.

Jack spun, dropping to one knee beside a stack of petrified cargo containers. The scavenger boss stood at the entrance to the cargo bay, his face smeared with grime and blood. His cybernetic arm hung limp and mangled at his side, clearly useless. In his other hand, he held a small, wicked looking laser pistol, the one Jack had almost let him grab from his belt. The wounded scavenger was nowhere to be seen, likely left behind. This was personal now.

“Nowhere left to run, junker!” the boss snarled. He fired again. The bolt ricocheted off the container near Jack’s head, showering him with foul smelling dust.

Salty had frozen in the middle of the room, the conduit’s glow making him a perfect target. He was exposed.

“Salty, move!” Jack yelled, firing back. His shot went wide, but it made the scavenger duck for cover.

Salty didn’t run. Instead, he angled his body, placing the dense mass of the plasma conduit between himself and their attacker. Another laser shot impacted the conduit’s copper wiring with a loud *twang*, but the energy was harmlessly absorbed. The part was tougher than it looked. Salty then began to move again, not toward the exit, but sideways, scuttling along the length of the inert conveyor belt, using the conduit as a mobile shield. It was a brilliant, instinctive piece of battlefield geometry.

The scavenger boss popped out of cover, his face a mask of frustration. He fired twice more at Salty, the shots deflecting harmlessly off the conduit. He was so focused on the insectoid that he forgot about Jack.

It was the opening Jack needed. He took careful aim, not at the man, but at the control panel for the conveyor system mounted on the wall just behind him. The panel was old, its casing cracked. Jack squeezed the trigger. The blaster bolt struck the panel dead center. It erupted in a geyser of sparks and smoke.

For a second, nothing happened. Then, with a low groan that vibrated through the deck, the ancient conveyor belt shuddered to life.

It moved slowly at first, a river of rusted metal plates beginning its long-dormant journey. The scavenger boss, standing right beside it, stumbled as the section of floor he was on began to move. His eyes widened in confusion.

Salty, already on the belt, simply had to wait. The belt was carrying him toward the exit. Jack, seeing his chance, scrambled from behind his cover, sprinted across the stationary floor, and took a running leap onto the moving platform. He landed with a roll, coming up next to Salty just as the belt began to pick up speed.

The scavenger boss was less fortunate. He tried to jump off, but his balance was off. He staggered, his good foot slipping on a patch of slick, greasy metal. He fell hard, his head striking the edge of a plate with a sickening crack. He didn't get up. The belt carried his motionless form away from them, back toward the engineering bay they had just fled.

Jack stared for a moment, his heart hammering in his chest. He watched until the body was out of sight. He hadn't killed him. The fall had. It was a small distinction, but it mattered.

Salty clicked softly, nudging Jack’s leg with one of his smaller limbs. The message was clear. *Let's go.*

They rode the conveyor belt to the end of the cargo bay. It deposited them unceremoniously in front of the far corridor. They dismounted, the silence of the ship pressing in on them again, broken only by the rhythmic hum of the newly activated machinery behind them and the faint pulse of the conduit.

The rest of the journey back to the *Mole* was blessedly quiet. Salty seemed to know a direct route, a series of maintenance shafts and crew passageways that were eerily untouched. It was as if they had passed through the heart of the ‘Iron Heart’s’ trauma and emerged on the other side. Within minutes, they saw the familiar circular outline of the *Mole’s* docking ring, a welcome sight in the oppressive darkness.

Jack worked the outer airlock controls, his hands shaking slightly from the adrenaline. The door hissed open, and they scrambled inside. Only when the inner door was open and they were standing on the deck of his own ship did Jack finally allow himself to breathe.

“We did it,” he said, the words feeling inadequate. He slumped against the wall, his blaster hanging heavy in his hand. “We actually did it.”

Salty carefully set the Type 4 Plasma Conduit down on the deck. The meter long cylinder hummed with contained power. It was beautiful. It was their ticket out of debt. He then turned his multifaceted eyes on Jack. The rage was gone. The focused intensity was gone. He emitted a series of soft, questioning clicks, the same gentle sounds Jack was used to. He nudged the blaster in Jack’s hand, then gestured with a limb toward Jack’s side, where a dark stain was spreading across his jacket.

Jack looked down. He hadn’t even noticed. A piece of shrapnel from one of the ricochets must have caught him. It wasn’t deep, just a long, bleeding gash along his ribs. The fight or flight response had completely masked the pain.

“It’s nothing,” Jack said, though the stinging was starting to make itself known. “Just a scratch.”

Salty chittered again, a more insistent sound this time. He scuttled past Jack, heading for the small med-kit stowed in a locker near the cockpit.

Jack watched him go, a sense of profound respect settling over him. He had always valued Salty for his knowledge, his senses, his uncanny ability to find things. But today, he had seen something else. He had seen Salty’s fury. He had seen him use the conduit as a shield. He had seen a warrior’s instinct. The scavenger boss had called him a ‘pet bug’. How wrong he had been. Salty was a partner. In every sense of the word.

Salty returned with the med-kit. He nudged it toward Jack with his head. Jack took it, his lips curling into a tired smile. “Alright, alright, I’ll patch myself up. Thanks, Salty.”

He peeled off his jacket and shirt, hissing as the fabric pulled at the wound. As he began to clean the gash with an antiseptic wipe, he looked at the plasma conduit sitting on the floor. It represented more than just money. It was proof. Proof that they could do this. That they could survive out here in the black, just the two of them against whatever the universe decided to throw at them.

Salty settled down near the conduit, his limbs tucked neatly beneath him. He began to clean his own antennae, a meticulous, calming ritual. The tension of the last hour slowly drained out of the small ship, replaced by a comfortable, shared silence.

After patching his side, Jack made his way to the cockpit and strapped himself into the pilot’s chair. He ran a pre-flight check, his fingers dancing over the familiar controls. All systems were green. The *Mole’s* engines hummed to life, a reassuring thrum that was the sound of safety, the sound of home.

He looked at the nav-screen. The route out of the asteroid field was complex, but manageable. Beyond that, it was a straight shot to the rendezvous with Mr. Silas. They would get their pay. They would refuel, rearm, and restock. And then they would find a new job.

He glanced back from the cockpit into the main hold. Salty was still resting, his multifaceted eyes reflecting the blinking lights of the console. The plasma conduit lay between them, a silent testament to their victory.

“Okay, pal,” Jack said softly, his voice resonating through the small ship. “Let’s go home.”

He eased the throttle forward. The *Mole* shuddered, disengaged its docking clamps from the dead hull of the ‘Iron Heart’, and pushed off into the void. It maneuvered gracefully around the massive, scarred wreck and then accelerated, a tiny spark of life navigating the silent, stone-filled darkness. Behind them, the ‘Iron Heart’ and its dead crew dwindled, becoming just another ghost in the graveyard of space. The job was done.

The asteroids thinned, their jagged shadows shrinking until they were nothing more than scattered teeth in the blackness. Jack kept the *Mole* at a steady, fuel conserving thrust. The ship’s engines were a low hum, a constant companion that vibrated through the deck plates and up into his bones. His side throbbed in time with the rhythm, a dull, persistent ache that the cheap painkiller from the med-kit couldn't quite conquer. Every slight turn of his torso sent a fresh spike of fire along his ribs. He had cleaned and sealed the gash as best he could, but it was deep. Deeper than a scratch. He knew it would need proper synth-flesh grafting when they made port. For now, the sterile bandage and his own stubbornness would have to be enough.

He glanced at the chrono. They were still hours away from the rendezvous point in the Tycho system. Hours of quiet flight through the empty lanes between star systems. He should have felt relieved. The danger was over. The prize was secured. But a different kind of unease was settling in, the quiet, creeping anxiety that always followed a near miss. He kept replaying the fight in his head. The clang of the scavenger’s boot on the hull. The searing heat of the plasma blast that had missed his head by inches. The sickening crunch when Salty’s leg had slammed into the boss’s helmet.

He looked over his shoulder into the hold. Salty was no longer by the conduit. The big insectoid had moved to his designated alcove, a small nook near the engineering access panel that he had claimed as his own. He was still, his posture relaxed, but Jack knew he wasn't sleeping. His multifaceted eyes, like twin clusters of dark jewels, were fixed on Jack. They caught the low light of the cockpit and reflected it back, unblinking and watchful. Jack had the distinct feeling he was being monitored, not with suspicion, but with a kind of clinical concern.

“Just tired, pal,” Jack said, his voice a low murmur. “It’s been a long day.”

Salty responded with a soft click of his mandibles, a sound Jack had come to associate with acknowledgement. The silence stretched again, comfortable but heavy. Jack’s mind drifted. He thought about the credits they would get from Mr. Silas. It would be enough to get the *Mole* a full overhaul, not just the patch jobs he was used to. A new scanner array. Maybe even an upgrade to the shield emitters. And for himself? A few nights in a real bunk on a station with gravity that didn't whine. A few decent meals that weren't nutrient paste squeezed from a tube.

It was a good dream, but it was still a dream. They had to get there first. As if summoned by his anxiety, a light on the console flickered from green to amber. It was a minor alert, almost missable. The secondary coolant pump for the port engine. Jack’s stomach tightened. He had run a full diagnostic. All systems were green. The system must have been lying. Or the damage was subtle, a hairline fracture from a stray vibration that had only now decided to give way.

“Damn it,” he muttered, unbuckling his harness. He stood up slowly, hissing as the movement pulled at the bandage on his side. He shuffled over to the engineering panel, his boots clanging softly on the metal deck. Salty uncurled himself from his alcove and followed, his six legs making barely a whisper of a sound. He stopped a few feet behind Jack, a silent, armored shadow.

Jack keyed in his access code. The panel hissed open, revealing a tangle of pipes, wires, and conduits, all glowing with their own internal light. The air that puffed out was warmer than it should be, with a faint, acrid smell of superheated coolant. The amber light on the main console was not an exaggeration. The pump was failing. It was a slow failure, but if it went completely, the port engine would overheat and automatically shut down. They would be running on a single engine, slow and vulnerable.

He reached into the nest of machinery, his fingers tracing the hot metal of the coolant line. He found the pump assembly. It was vibrating erratically, a shuddering pulse that didn't match the steady hum of the engine. The seal looked intact, but a tiny wisp of vapor, almost invisible, escaped from a joint.

“Okay. Okay, I can bypass it,” he said, more to himself than to Salty. “Route the main coolant through the secondary lines. It’ll put more strain on the primary pump, but it should hold until we get to Silas.”

It was a delicate procedure. He needed to manually close one valve while simultaneously opening another, all while the system was live. One wrong move and he could flood the compartment with boiling coolant or, worse, create a pressure spike that would rupture the entire line. He needed two hands, and his side was screaming in protest every time he leaned in.

He braced himself against the bulkhead, his left hand reaching for the bypass valve. His right hand stretched toward the primary flow gate. His fingers closed around the cold metal of the handle. He took a deep breath, sweat beading on his forehead. He turned his head slightly to get a better angle.

As he moved, a sharp, blinding pain shot through his side. His vision swam. His grip slipped. The heavy valve handle snapped back into place with a loud clang. Jack cried out, stumbling back from the panel. He clutched his side, his breath coming in ragged gasps. The pain was a white hot fire now, and he could feel a wet warmth seeping through the bandage. The wound had reopened.

He leaned against the opposite wall, sliding down to the floor. The ship seemed to tilt around him. Black spots danced in his vision. He was losing it.

He felt a light pressure on his shoulder. He looked up, his vision clearing just enough to see Salty looming over him. The big creature let out a low, guttural thrum, a sound of deep concern. He nudged Jack’s shoulder again, more insistently this time.

“I’m okay,” Jack gasped, though it was a clear lie. “Just… just need a minute.”

Salty ignored him. He turned and looked at the open engineering panel, then back at Jack. He extended one of his slender, multi-jointed forelimbs and gently tapped the bypass valve Jack had been trying to reach. Then he tapped the primary flow gate. He looked back at Jack, his head tilted. The meaning was unmistakable. He had been watching. He understood.

“You… you can’t,” Jack breathed. “You don’t have the dexterity.”

Salty chittered, a sharp, impatient sound. He scuttled to the panel. He braced four of his legs against the deck and bulkhead, anchoring himself firmly. Then, with a delicacy that seemed impossible for a creature of his size and construction, he reached into the compartment with his two forelimbs. One limb, with its sharp, chitinous point, pressed against the small locking pin on the primary gate. The other, broader limb wrapped around the bypass valve.

He looked back at Jack one last time. Jack, still dizzy with pain, could only stare in amazement. He gave a weak, shaky nod.

Salty went to work. With a surgeon’s precision, he depressed the pin and slowly, smoothly, pulled the primary gate closed. The flow of coolant visibly slowed in the transparent section of the pipe. Simultaneously, he began to turn the bypass valve. It was stiff, designed for a human hand and human leverage. Salty’s limb strained, the chitin groaning under the pressure. For a second, Jack thought it wouldn't work. Then, with a sharp squeal of metal on metal, the valve began to turn.

He opened it halfway, then fully, his movements perfectly synchronized with the closing of the other gate. The coolant rerouted, its flow stabilizing in the new channel. The erratic vibration of the failing pump ceased, replaced by the steady, powerful thrum of the primary pump taking on the extra load. The acrid smell began to fade.

Salty retracted his limbs and stepped back from the panel. He turned to Jack and made a soft clicking noise. He nudged the med-kit, which was still on the floor, toward Jack’s leg.

Jack stared, his pain momentarily forgotten. He had seen Salty fight. He had seen him navigate. But this was something else. This was technical proficiency. He had observed, understood, and executed a complex mechanical procedure. The scavenger boss’s words echoed in his mind again. ‘Pet bug’. He let out a weak, breathy laugh. Salty was an engineer.

“Thanks, partner,” Jack said, his voice thick with emotion. “I owe you one.” He grabbed the med-kit and, with Salty watching over him like a concerned parent, began the painful process of redressing his bleeding wound.

The rest of the journey passed in a haze of pain and gratitude. Jack managed to get back to the pilot’s chair, his movements stiff and slow. Salty remained in the main hold, a silent guardian watching over his injured partner and their hard won prize.

Finally, the *Mole* dropped out of hyperspace into the Tycho system. Before them lay Silas Station. It wasn’t a bustling commercial hub like Port ol-Nassar or a grimy free-for-all like the Tartarus Belt. It was a single, massive structure, a spiderweb of dark metal and cold, blue running lights. It was privately owned, operated by Mr. Silas himself, a man who valued privacy and control above all else. Docking here was by invitation only.

“*Mole*, you are cleared for approach to docking bay seven,” a clipped, synthesized voice announced over the comms. “Acknowledge.”

“Acknowledged, Silas Station,” Jack replied, his voice strained. “*Mole* on final approach to bay seven.”

He guided the ship in, his hands sure on the controls despite the fire in his side. The docking clamps engaged with a solid, reassuring thunk. The engines spooled down. For a moment, there was only the sound of the life support and Jack’s own ragged breathing.

“Stay with the conduit,” Jack said to Salty as he unbuckled himself. “Don’t let anyone on board without my say so. Understood?”

Salty chittered his assent, moving to stand directly beside the large, cylindrical piece of salvage. His posture was no longer relaxed. His antennae were erect, and his forelimbs were held slightly away from his body. He looked like a coiled spring, ready to strike. It was a comforting sight.

Jack put his jacket back on, wincing as the fabric brushed his fresh bandage. He took a deep breath, trying to project an aura of calm confidence he did not feel, and hit the switch to cycle the airlock. The outer door hissed open, and a sterile boarding tube extended to meet the *Mole’s* hull.

He walked through the tube into the docking bay. It was cavernous, clean, and unnervingly quiet. The only sound was the low hum of atmospheric processors. Two figures stood waiting for him. They were both human, both dressed in immaculate gray uniforms with no insignias. They were tall, broad, and had the kind of dead-eyed stillness that spoke of extensive military or corporate security training. They were walls, not people.

“Jack,” a voice said. It didn't come from either of the guards. It came from a man who stepped out from behind a stack of cargo containers. Mr. Silas. He was shorter than his guards, dressed in a simple but exquisitely tailored black suit. His hair was silver, his face was lined but sharp, and his eyes were a pale, piercing blue. He held a datapad in one hand.

“Mr. Silas,” Jack said, nodding. He tried to stand a little straighter. “I have your property.”

“So I see,” Silas said, his gaze flicking past Jack toward the open airlock of the *Mole*. “The plasma conduit from the ‘Iron Heart’. You were successful. I confess, I had my doubts. The other parties interested in that wreck were not known for their civility.”

“They weren’t,” Jack agreed. “We ran into some of them. It got messy.” He intentionally left it at that. Let Silas wonder about the details.

“Messiness is a byproduct of your profession, Jack. I am only interested in the result.” He gestured toward the ship. “May I see it?”

“Be my guest,” Jack said, stepping aside.

Silas walked past him, his expensive shoes making no sound on the polished floor. His two guards fell in behind him like shadows. They stopped at the airlock, peering in. Salty, standing guard by the conduit, shifted his weight. A low hiss escaped his mandibles, a clear warning. The guards tensed, their hands moving subtly toward their sidearms.

“It’s alright, Salty,” Jack called out. “He’s with me.”

Salty’s hiss subsided, but he didn’t move. His eyes remained fixed on the three men.

Silas seemed unfazed by the giant alien. He studied the conduit for a long moment, his eyes scanning every inch of its surface. “Bring it out. Place it on the diagnostic sled.”

Jack hesitated. “Payment first, Mr. Silas. That was the deal.”

Silas finally turned his gaze from the conduit to Jack. He smiled, a thin, bloodless expression. “Of course. A man of principle. I respect that.” He tapped his datapad. “Fifty thousand credits, as agreed. The account number I have on file for you is still correct?”

“It is,” Jack confirmed.

“Excellent.” Silas did not transfer the funds. He continued to hold the datapad, his thumb hovering over the screen. “Just a formality, you understand. I need to verify the integrity of the unit. You mentioned it got ‘messy’. I need to ensure my investment wasn't compromised in the process.”

A cold knot formed in Jack’s stomach. This was the part he hated. The part where the client held all the power. They were on Silas’s station, surrounded by his men. The *Mole* was damaged. He was injured. Arguing would be pointless, and potentially fatal.

“Fine,” Jack said through gritted teeth. “Verify it.”

He went back into the *Mole* and, with a series of grunts and pained hisses, managed to get the heavy conduit onto a small cargo lifter. Salty watched his every move, his antennae twitching with what Jack could only interpret as disapproval of the entire situation. He guided the lifter out into the bay and gently lowered the conduit onto the large, flat diagnostic sled Silas had indicated.

Silas and one of his guards approached the sled. The guard produced a small, high frequency scanner and began to run it along the length of the conduit. Silas watched the readings appear on his datapad, his expression unreadable. The scan took several minutes. The silence in the bay was absolute.

Finally, the guard finished and stepped back. Silas stared at his datapad for a long, long time. He sighed, a theatrical sound of disappointment.

“This is unfortunate, Jack,” he said, turning the datapad so Jack could see the screen. A section of the conduit was highlighted in red. A 3D model showed a web of tiny, almost invisible lines. “Micro-fractures. Near the primary emitter housing. Consistent with extreme thermal stress or kinetic shock. A plasma blast at close range, perhaps?”

Jack’s blood ran cold. He knew exactly when that had happened. It was when Salty had used the conduit as a shield. It had saved their lives.

“The unit is still functional,” Jack argued, his voice tight. “Those are superficial. They won’t affect performance.”

“They won’t affect performance *now*,” Silas corrected him smoothly. “But they compromise the unit’s long term stability. The projected operational lifespan is reduced by nearly forty percent. It will require reinforcement and recalibration. An expensive process.” He shook his head slowly. “I’m afraid I cannot pay the full price for damaged goods.”

This was it. The squeeze. Jack had seen it coming, but it still felt like a punch to the gut. A gut that was already on fire.

“That’s not fair,” Jack said, his voice low. “We risked our lives to get that for you.”

“And you will be compensated for your risk,” Silas said, his tone still perfectly reasonable. “But not for delivering a flawed product. I am prepared to offer you thirty thousand. It’s a generous offer, considering the repair costs I will now have to incur.”

Rage, hot and potent, surged through Jack, momentarily eclipsing the pain. Twenty thousand credits. An entire upgrade package for the *Mole*. Gone. He thought of Salty, using his own body to defend the conduit, to defend Jack. And for what? To be shortchanged by this man in a fancy suit.

He took a step forward. “That’s not the deal.”

The two guards tensed again, their posture shifting into something more overtly threatening. Silas didn't even flinch. He just looked at Jack with those cold, calculating eyes.

“The deal was for an intact plasma conduit, Jack. This is not intact. Take the thirty thousand. Or take your damaged goods and leave my station. The choice is yours. But I would advise you to choose wisely. Your ship’s transponder is flagging a coolant leak, and your medical signature suggests you are in considerable pain. You are not in a strong bargaining position.”

Silas knew. Of course he knew. His station had probably been scanning the *Mole* from the moment it arrived. He had all the cards.

Defeated, Jack’s shoulders slumped. He looked past Silas, into his own ship. He saw Salty, still standing there, a silent, powerful warrior who had done everything right. They had won the fight, but they were losing the peace.

“Fine,” Jack spat, the word tasting like ash. “Thirty thousand.”

“A wise decision,” Silas said, without a hint of triumph. It was just business. He tapped his datapad. A second later, Jack’s personal comm chimed, confirming the transfer of funds.

“Now, if you’ll excuse me,” Silas said, gesturing for his men to move the sled. “I have work to do. The medbay is on level three, should you need it. I will even waive the docking fee, as a gesture of goodwill.”

Jack watched them wheel the plasma conduit away, the fruit of their labor, their pain, and their courage, sold at a discount. He turned and walked back to the *Mole*, his body aching and his spirit bruised.

He stepped into the ship. Salty scuttled forward, letting out a series of low clicks and whirs. He nudged Jack’s hand with an antenna. It was a question. *Are you alright?*

“Yeah, pal,” Jack sighed, patting Salty’s armored head. “I’m alright. We got paid. Not as much as we should have, but enough.”

He leaned against the bulkhead, the fight finally going out of him. Enough to repair the ship. Enough to get him patched up. Enough to buy supplies and fuel. Enough to keep going. It would have to be enough.

He looked at his partner, at the being who had fought and bled and thought alongside him. They had survived. They were together. And in the cold, unforgiving black of space, that was worth more than any number of credits.

“Come on,” Jack said, straightening up with a groan. “Let’s get this bucket of bolts to a repair dock. And then, I’m getting the best damn synth-steak on this station.”

Salty chittered, a sound that was almost a laugh. He followed Jack toward the cockpit. The job was done. The price had been paid, in more ways than one. It was time to rest, to heal, and to get ready for whatever came next.

The cockpit of the *Mole* was a symphony of failure. Red lights blinked accusingly on the console, each one a tiny, insistent heartbeat of impending doom. The main coolant pressure gauge was flatlined, its needle resting at zero like a fallen soldier. A high pitched whine emanated from somewhere behind the portside bulkhead, a sound Jack had learned to associate with very expensive repairs. He eased himself into the pilot’s chair, his ribs screaming a protest that was sharp and articulate. The med-gel patch Silas’s guards had slapped on him was a temporary fix, a cheap bandage on a deep wound.

Salty scuttled up beside the command console, his multifaceted eyes scanning the readouts. A series of rapid clicks and whirs filled the small space. He extended a delicate manipulator arm and tapped a sequence of commands onto a secondary screen, rerouting what little auxiliary coolant remained to the primary engine housing. The high pitched whine subsided to a low, disgruntled hum.

*This is bad,* Salty’s posture seemed to say. *Very bad.*

“Tell me something I don’t know, pal,” Jack muttered, his hands moving over the controls with practiced familiarity. He brought the ship’s internal schematics up on the main viewer. A web of blue lines showed the *Mole’s* guts. A large section around the aft engineering bay was flashing a hostile, angry red. The coolant leak Silas had so smugly pointed out was not a leak. It was a full blown rupture. The stray plasma bolt from their little skirmish had done more than cosmetic damage. It had cooked the primary and secondary coolant regulators. They were lucky the whole system hadn’t blown and turned them into a cloud of superheated vapor.

“Alright. Easy does it,” he coaxed the ship, his voice low and soothing, as if talking to a spooked animal. He nudged the thrusters, and the *Mole* groaned its way out of Silas’s private docking bay. The station, a sprawling lattice of metal and light named Port Calypso, loomed around them. It was a typical mid-rim trading post, a place for people to get rich, get broke, or just get by. Jack had spent most of his life in the third category.

He keyed in a docking request for the public repair yard. The response was automated and immediate, a flight path lighting up on his nav-screen. It was a short trip, but with the ship in this state, every second felt borrowed. He kept his touch light on the controls, nursing the ship through the station’s traffic lanes, a slow, wounded beast among sharks. Salty monitored the engine output, his metallic body tense and still, ready to react to the slightest fluctuation. They worked in silence, a well oiled machine even when their ship was falling apart.

The repair yard was a vast, open cavern carved into the station’s superstructure. Ships of all sizes were nested in docking clamps, their hulls open to the void, tended to by mechanics in bulky vacuum suits who drifted around them like pilot fish. A stocky woman with grease smudged on her cheek and a pair of optic enhancers pushed up on her forehead directed them into an empty berth. The magnetic clamps engaged with a heavy thud, and Jack killed the engines. The silence that followed was profound, broken only by the faint, systemic ticking of the ship cooling.

“Grease,” Jack said, as the airlock hissed open and the woman stomped aboard. Her name was Anya, but no one had called her that in a decade.

“Jack,” she grunted, her eyes immediately sweeping the cockpit, taking in the scorch marks on the bulkhead and the flickering console. She didn't waste time on pleasantries. “Heard a rumor you were picking a fight with the Orion Syndicate out by the asteroid belt. I see the rumor was understated.”

Jack grimaced. News traveled fast in the black. “Just a minor disagreement over shipping rights.”

Grease snorted, a harsh, metallic sound. She walked past him, her heavy boots clanging on the deck plating, and headed straight for the engineering section. Salty gave her a wide berth. The two of them had a long history of mutual, professional respect, which meant they mostly ignored each other. Grease was one of the few humans who didn't treat Salty like a pet or a curiosity. To her, he was just another part of the ship’s hardware. A very complicated, mobile part.

Jack followed her, his body protesting every step. “How bad is it?”

Grease was already pulling a diagnostic probe from her belt and plugging it into a port near the coolant regulators. She squinted at the readout on her wrist comm. “Minor disagreement, huh? Looks more like you tried to use a plasma cannon as a welcome mat. Your primary regulator is slag. The secondary is fused solid. You’ve got microfractures all through the coolant lines. And your aft shield emitter is putting out all the defensive power of a wet piece of paper.” She looked up at him, her enhanced eyes glowing a soft blue. “You’re lucky to be alive. You’re even luckier you didn’t vent your atmosphere.”

“So you can fix it?” Jack asked, ignoring the lecture.

“I can fix anything, Jack. You know that. The question is, can you afford it?” She tapped a few commands into her comm, did some quick calculations. She looked at him again, her expression unreadable. “Twenty five thousand credits. And that’s the friends and family rate, which you are neither.”

Jack felt the air go out of him. Twenty five thousand. That left them with five thousand from Silas’s insult of a payment. Five thousand to live on. To eat. To refuel. To re-arm. His grand plan of a nice synth-steak and a few days of rest was evaporating like spilled water on a hot engine block.

“That’s…” he started, but the words died in his throat. It was highway robbery. But it was also the only offer he was going to get. Grease was the best, and she knew it.

“It’s the price,” she said flatly. “I’ll need to fabricate a new regulator from scratch. The syndicate parts aren’t standard issue. Take it or leave it. But if you leave it, you’re not getting off this station in that ship.”

Defeated for the second time in as many hours, Jack nodded. “Do it.”

“Payment up front,” she said, holding out her wrist comm.

Jack sighed and tapped his own comm against hers. He watched the number in his account plummet. The chime that confirmed the transfer sounded like a death knell for his finances.

“Good,” Grease said, all business again. “It’ll take me a cycle, maybe two. Stay out of my way.” She turned and got to work, pulling open a maintenance hatch with a grunt of effort. Jack was dismissed.

He and Salty walked back to the cockpit. Salty nudged his hand again, a series of soft, questioning clicks. *Now what?*

“Now, I go to the medbay,” Jack said, his voice strained. “That steak is looking less likely by the minute, pal. But I’m not spending the next two days crippled.” He looked at his partner. “You stay with the ship. Keep an eye on Grease. Make sure she doesn’t replace any of your favorite capacitors with cheap knockoffs.”

Salty chittered, a flicker of amusement. He settled himself near the main airlock, a silent, six legged guardian. He would watch. He always did.

The walk to the medbay was a tour of Port Calypso’s stratified society. Grease’s repair bay was in the grimy, industrial underbelly of the station, a place of loud noises, strange smells, and hard-working people. The air tasted of ozone and hot metal. As he ascended on a public lift tube, the environment changed. The corridors became wider, cleaner. The lighting softened. The air smelled of filtered oxygen and faint, antiseptic chemicals. Level three was the medical and administrative nexus. It was quiet, sterile, and soulless.

The medbay was not a place of healing hands and comforting words. It was a sterile white room lined with automated diagnostic and treatment pods. A single, bored looking attendant sat behind a desk, barely looking up from his datapad.

“Need treatment,” Jack said, his voice flat.

“Credit chit,” the attendant replied, equally flat.

Jack swiped his chit. A green light blinked.

“Pod three,” the attendant said, gesturing vaguely down the line.

Jack walked over to the indicated pod and lay down on the cool, synthetic surface. A scanner descended from the ceiling, bathing him in a blue light. A synthesized voice, calm and detached, spoke from a hidden speaker.

“Analysis complete. Multiple contusions to the torso. Three fractured ribs. Minor internal hemorrhaging. Prescribing class four analgesic and subdermal regenerator. Estimated cost: two thousand credits. Do you accept?”

“Do I have a choice?” Jack muttered to himself. “Accept,” he said louder.

A mechanical arm extended from the pod’s side, a hypospray at its tip. It pressed against his neck, and he felt a cold sting, followed by a spreading warmth. Another arm, this one tipped with a soft, glowing emitter, passed over his torso. He could feel a faint tingling as the regenerator went to work, knitting his bones and sealing his damaged tissues. The sharp, biting pain in his side dulled to a distant, manageable ache.

“Treatment complete,” the voice announced. “Please remit payment.”

Jack staggered out of the pod and tapped his comm at the payment terminal. Two thousand more credits vanished into the station’s coffers. He was now down to three thousand. Three thousand credits to his name. The synth-steak was officially a fantasy. It was going to be nutrient paste and recycled water for the foreseeable future.

He felt better physically, but his mood was black. He had bled for that conduit. Salty had almost been killed. And for what? For a pittance that had been swallowed whole by the basic necessities of survival. This was the life. You take two steps forward, and the universe kicks you three steps back.

He met Salty back at the *Mole*. Grease was deep in the ship’s guts, surrounded by tools and spare parts, the air thick with the smell of soldering. She ignored them completely.

“Change of plans, pal,” Jack said quietly to Salty. “The steak is off. We’re on a budget. A very, very tight budget.”

Salty’s antennae drooped slightly. He let out a low, mournful whir.

“Yeah, I know,” Jack sighed. “But we can at least get out of this grease pit for a bit. There’s a dive bar on level one, The Rusty Gantry. Their synth-ale is cheap, and the nutrient paste tastes slightly less like cardboard than most. It’s better than sitting here.”

Salty seemed to agree. He scuttled after Jack, and they left the repair bay, two broke freelancers looking for a cheap drink.

The Rusty Gantry was everything its name suggested. The lighting was dim, the air was thick with smoke from various alien substances, and the floor was sticky. The clientele was a mix of grizzled spacers, low level corporate drones, and shady figures who kept to the shadows. It was Jack’s kind of place. They found a booth in a dark corner, Salty folding himself neatly under the table, mostly out of sight. Jack ordered two mugs of the house synth-ale and a bowl of gray nutrient paste.

He took a long swallow of the ale. It was bitter and cold, and it cut through some of the grime in his throat. He stared into the murky depths of his mug, watching the bubbles rise and pop. For the first time since leaving Silas’s dock, he allowed himself to feel the full weight of their situation. They were broke, their ship was in pieces, and they were stuck on this station for at least a day. And they had made enemies. The Orion Syndicate was not known for its forgiving nature. They wouldn’t just write off a stolen high tech component.

He was so lost in his thoughts that he didn’t notice the figure until they were standing right beside his table, casting a long shadow over him.

“Jack Carver,” a voice said. It was not a question.

Jack looked up slowly. The man was tall and unnaturally thin, dressed in a sharp, dark gray suit that was far too clean for a place like The Rusty Gantry. His face was a mask of cold professionalism, with pale eyes that seemed to see right through him. He had the quiet, coiled stillness of a predator.

“I don’t know you,” Jack said, his hand instinctively moving to rest on the table, near where he would keep a blaster if he had been foolish enough to bring one into a public bar.

“No, you don’t,” the man said. “But you know my employers. You recently came into possession of some of their property.”

Jack’s blood ran cold. The Orion Syndicate. He had been expecting them, but not this soon. Not this publicly.

“That was a business transaction,” Jack said, his voice level. “It’s over.”

The man smiled, a thin, bloodless stretching of his lips. “Nothing is ever over, Mr. Carver. It simply changes. My employers are not unreasonable people. They understand that things get lost. But they are very interested in where lost things end up.”

Salty made a low, guttural clicking sound from under the table. A warning.

“I sold it,” Jack said. “To a man named Silas. On this station.” He saw no point in lying. This man already knew. This was a test.

“Yes. Silas,” the man said, his eyes narrowing slightly. “We are aware. He is a collector of rare technologies. What you may not be aware of is what he is building. That conduit you so kindly delivered was not for a simple power generator. That level of damage, the specific microfractures you inflicted, they were not a flaw. They were a feature.”

Jack stared at him. “What are you talking about?”

“Silas is not building something to provide power,” the man explained, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “He is building something to deny it. A weapon. A localized plasma cascade generator. It can create a targeted, unstable energy storm, capable of disabling any ship, any station defense grid, in a matter of seconds. The damaged conduit you provided is the key. It’s the focusing element that makes the cascade unpredictable, and therefore, impossible to shield against.”

Jack felt a knot tighten in his stomach. Silas hadn’t just swindled him. He had used him. Used his desperation, his fight, his very specific failure, as a component in a weapon of mass disruption. He had been a pawn from the very beginning.

“Why are you telling me this?” Jack asked, his mind racing.

“Because Silas is protected. His station is a fortress. His personal security is unmatched. My employers cannot get close to him,” the man said. “But you can. You are already on his station. You have already done business with him. You are, for the moment, not a threat in his eyes.”

The nutrient paste sat untouched on the table. Jack had lost his appetite. “What do you want?”

The man placed a small, metallic datachip on the table. It was black and featureless. “My employers want you to undo your mistake. They want you to get back inside Silas’s private sector and sabotage the device he is building. The conduit you delivered is the heart of it. Destroy it. Ruin his work. In return, your… misunderstanding with the Syndicate will be forgiven. More than forgiven. You will be rewarded. Handsomely.”

Jack looked from the chip to the man’s cold eyes. “And if I say no?”

The man’s smile returned, and this time it held no humor at all. “Then your misunderstanding with the Syndicate becomes a blood debt. And they always collect. They have a very long reach, Mr. Carver. There is no corner of the galaxy you could run to where they would not find you. And your… companion.”

The veiled threat hung in the air, more potent than any weapon. They would not just come for him. They would come for Salty.

The man straightened up. “The chip contains technical details, access codes our agents have managed to acquire, and a secure contact channel. You have until your ship is repaired to make your choice. But like Mr. Silas, I would advise you to choose wisely.”

Without another word, the man turned and melted back into the shadows of the bar, leaving Jack alone with the datachip, the cheap ale, and the ruin of his day. The brief respite was over. They had escaped one fire only to be thrown into a furnace. He was caught between Silas, a man with a superweapon, and the Orion Syndicate, a criminal empire with a grudge.

He looked down at the datachip, a small piece of black plastic that held either his salvation or his death sentence. Salty nudged his leg from under the table, a low, worried click.

*What now?*

Jack picked up the chip, its smooth surface cool against his skin. His hand closed around it, a fist of weary resignation.

“Yeah, pal,” he whispered, staring into the darkness where the man had disappeared. “What now?” The job wasn't done. It had just begun.

The air in the bar, once a noisy refuge, now felt thin and suffocating. The shadows the Syndicate man had disappeared into seemed to pulse with a life of their own, full of unseen eyes and unheard threats. Jack sat frozen for a long moment, the weight of the datachip in his palm a physical anchor pulling him down into a new level of trouble he hadn't known existed. He was a small-time operator, a hauler of questionable goods. He ran from problems. He didn’t run toward them, especially when they involved interstellar criminal empires and madmen with reality-bending weapons.

Salty made another soft clicking sound, a gentle inquiry that cut through Jack’s paralysis. He looked down at the small, chitinous creature. Salty’s multifaceted eyes shimmered with a worried light. In them, Jack saw not just a pet or a partner, but the one thing in his miserable, drifting life that he had managed to do right. He had rescued Salty from a grimy exotic pet market on Cygnus X1, a creature bred for its novelty, destined for a cage. On the *Stardust Drifter*, Salty was free. He was family. And the Syndicate man had put a target on his back.

The choice was gone before it had ever truly formed. There was no choice.

“Alright, pal,” Jack breathed out, the words a ragged puff of air. He pushed himself up from the table, his legs stiff. The untouched nutrient paste looked like a grey monument to his own stupidity. He tossed a few low denomination credits onto the table, more than enough to cover the ale. He didn’t want to owe anyone here anything.

He walked out of the bar, Salty trotting silently at his heels, a four legged shadow. The main concourse of Silas’s station was a river of bodies, a cacophony of a dozen languages and the electronic chatter of vendor stalls. Just an hour ago it had felt vibrant, a place of opportunity. Now it felt like a prison yard. Every face seemed to be watching him. Every shadow seemed to hold a Syndicate enforcer or one of Silas’s corporate thugs. Paranoia was a cold snake coiling in his gut.

He kept his head down, his worn jacket pulled tight. He navigated the crowds with the practiced ease of someone used to being overlooked, melting into the flow, just another piece of human debris circulating through the station’s arteries. They made their way back toward the docking bays, to the lower levels where the air smelled of ozone and recycled coolant.

The *Stardust Drifter* was exactly where he’d left her, a battered but beloved hunk of metal in a row of sleeker, newer ships. A greasy mechanic in oil stained coveralls was just wiping his hands on a rag near the primary plasma conduit he’d been hired to patch.

“She’s almost there, Carver,” the mechanic grunted without looking up. “Had to re-seat the entire injector assembly. Another few hours to run the final diagnostics. Should have you out of here before the next cycle.”

“Good. Thanks,” Jack said, his voice flat. A few hours. That was his window. The Syndicate had been precise.

He climbed the boarding ramp, Salty right behind him. The moment the inner door hissed shut, sealing them inside, Jack felt a fraction of the tension leave his shoulders. This was his sanctuary. The familiar scent of worn upholstery, engine oil, and Salty’s faint, earthy musk was a comfort. The cockpit was cramped, the cargo bay was a mess, and the galley was barely functional, but it was home.

He slumped into the pilot’s chair and stared out the viewport at the uninspiring grey wall of the docking bay. Salty hopped onto the console next to him, his antennae twitching. He nudged the datachip in Jack’s still clenched fist.

“I know, I know,” Jack muttered. He opened his hand and looked at the small black rectangle. Salvation or a death sentence. He had to know which. He slotted the chip into the console’s auxiliary port.

The screen, normally displaying ship status or navigation charts, flickered and filled with encrypted data. A simple decryption key provided by the Syndicate agent’s verbal instructions unscrambled the files. What spilled across the monitor was a treasure trove of corporate espionage.

There were schematics, detailed blueprints of Silas’s private research sector. They were layered with station infrastructure plans, showing power conduits, data lines, and, most importantly, environmental ducting. Ventilation shafts. Patrol routes for Silas’s security team were mapped out, their timings listed with chilling accuracy. There were passcodes, not for the main doors, but for secondary systems, maintenance panels, and diagnostic terminals. The Syndicate’s reach was long indeed.

But the centerpiece of the data was the device itself. They called it the ‘Cascade Engine’. It was a brutal, elegant piece of engineering. A spherical chamber, surrounded by superconducting magnets and emitter arrays. And at its very heart, a diagram of the conduit he had delivered. His conduit. The data confirmed what the agent had said. The unique quantum signature of the materials from his failed mining venture was the focusing lens. It took a chaotic energy field and collapsed it into a single, unpredictable, unstoppable wave. A wave that could, according to the Syndicate’s projections, unravel the fabric of spacetime on a localized level. It wouldn’t just blow up a ship. It would erase it from existence, leaving behind a wound in reality that would bleed radiation for centuries.

Jack felt sick. He had delivered the trigger for a doomsday weapon.

He zoomed in on the schematics, his mind no longer a swirl of panic but a cold, calculating machine. This was his trade, after all. Finding a way in, and a way out. The main access was a no go. Armored doors, biometric scanners, and a platoon of guards. But the schematics showed a network of maintenance tunnels running beneath the research sector. Specifically, a primary coolant duct that ran directly under the core chamber housing the Cascade Engine. Its access point was three levels down, in a little used service corridor behind a decommissioned waste reclamation unit. It was a ghost passage, forgotten by all but the station’s original architects and, apparently, the Syndicate’s spies.

The goal wasn’t destruction. Not in the explosive sense. The chip was clear on that. An explosion would be traced back to him instantly. Silas would lock down the station, and the Syndicate would deny everything, leaving him to be spaced. No, the plan was more subtle. Sabotage. The conduit was held in place by a series of high powered magnetic clamps, each drawing power from a dedicated capacitor. The data suggested that if the polarity of one of those capacitors was reversed, the next time the engine was brought to full power for its final calibration, the containment field would collapse asymmetrically. The conduit would be instantly crushed into dust, and the resulting energy backlash would fry every delicate component in the engine. It would be a catastrophic, but internal, failure. A billion credit science experiment gone wrong. Deniable.

He looked at Salty. “It’s bad, pal. Real bad.”

Salty clicked, a low thrumming sound. He seemed to understand the gravity, if not the details.

“But I think we can do it,” Jack continued, more to himself than to the creature. “It’s a tight squeeze. And if we get caught…” He didn’t finish the sentence. He didn’t need to.

He stood up and began to prepare. He didn’t own body armor or plasma rifles. His tools were for breaking and entering, not for open combat. He pulled a small, worn bag from a storage locker. Inside, he placed a plasma torch with a low intensity focusing tip, a set of circuit jammers, a handheld scanner to detect energy fields, and a multi-tool with a hundred different attachments. He added a small coil of high tensile microfiber rope and a magnetic grapnel. Finally, he grabbed a small, cylindrical device. A localized audio dampener. It wouldn’t silence a gunshot, but it could muffle the sound of a torch cutting through a metal grate.

He looked over at Salty, who was watching his every move. “You’re on point, little guy. You go first. You’re small, quiet. You listen for anything I can’t hear and you sniff out anything that doesn’t smell right.”

Salty bobbed his head, a series of quick, sharp movements. He was ready.

Jack took a deep breath, the recycled air of his ship tasting stale. He clipped the bag to his belt, pulled a dark knit cap down over his head, and gave the cockpit one last look. “Let’s go earn our freedom,” he whispered.

They slipped out of the ship, back into the station’s underbelly. The mechanic was gone, a blinking green light on the diagnostic panel the only sign of his work. The clock was ticking.

Finding the service corridor was easy with the Syndicate’s map. It was exactly where the file said it would be, behind a grimy panel labeled ‘Hazardous Waste Disposal, Sector 7G’. The air behind the panel was stale and cold. A single, flickering light illuminated a narrow passage that descended into darkness.

“Here we go,” Jack muttered, swinging his legs into the opening. He landed with a soft thud on the metal floor below. Salty dropped down silently beside him.

The tunnel was cramped. Pipes lined the walls, weeping condensation that slicked the floor. The only sounds were the distant, rhythmic hum of the station’s life support and the drip, drip, drip of water. Jack switched on a low beam torch, its light cutting a nervous path through the gloom. Salty trotted ahead, his claws making barely a whisper of a sound.

They followed the map on Jack’s wrist-mounted datapad for what felt like an eternity. Left at a junction marked with faded blue paint. Down a rusted ladder. Through a narrow crawlspace that forced Jack onto his hands and knees. Salty moved with an easy grace, a native of these tight, dark places.

After twenty minutes, they arrived at their target. A large, circular duct, three meters in diameter, marked with warnings about extreme temperature coolant. The schematics said it was on a bypass loop for the next two hours for routine maintenance. A stroke of luck, if you could call any of this lucky.

“Okay, this is it,” Jack whispered. He pulled out the audio dampener and placed it on the duct. A low hum filled the air as it created a small field of silence. He then took out the plasma torch. The tiny, intensely hot flame made no sound as it sliced through the bolts holding a service grate in place.

He pulled the heavy grate free and set it aside. A blast of frigid air washed over them. Inside, the duct was a cavern of polished steel, large enough for Jack to stand upright. He checked his scanner. No coolant flow. The Syndicate’s intel was good.

“Your turn, pal,” he said to Salty. “Get in there. Find a spot to hide near the next junction. Let me know if you hear anything.”

Salty gave a short click and scrambled into the duct, disappearing into the cold darkness. Jack waited a moment, then clipped his bag securely and pulled himself inside, replacing the grate behind him. He couldn’t seal it completely, but in the dark, it would look undisturbed to a casual glance.

Inside the duct, the cold was biting. Every breath plumed in front of his face. The polished floor was slick, and the echo of his own movements was unnervingly loud. He moved slowly, his light off, trusting the faint glow from distant service hatches.

He had gone about fifty meters when his datapad, linked to a tiny receiver he had given Salty, vibrated. A single, sharp pulse. The signal for ‘stop’.

Jack froze, pressing himself against the curved wall of the duct. He held his breath, straining his ears. At first, he heard nothing but the ever-present hum of the station. Then, he caught it. Voices. Muffled, but clear, coming from a grate up ahead.

“...don’t know why Silas is pushing the calibration forward,” one voice said. “The preliminary diagnostics aren’t even finished.”

“You know how he gets,” a second voice replied. “He’s convinced the board is going to pull his funding. He wants a successful full power test to show them. Wants to do it before the end of this cycle.”

Jack’s blood ran cold. Before the end of this cycle. That was now. His timeline hadn’t just shrunk. It had vanished. If Silas powered up the engine while he was in there, the sabotage wouldn’t matter. The energy backlash would be the least of his worries. He, Salty, and a good chunk of this sector would be atomized.

The voices faded as the guards moved on. Jack waited, his heart hammering against his ribs. The datapad vibrated again. Two pulses. All clear.

He scrambled forward, his movements now frantic. He reached the grate the voices had come from. Peeking through the slats, he saw it. The chamber.

It was a cathedral of bad intentions, a vast, circular room with a ceiling that soared three stories high. Walkways and control stations ringed the upper levels. And in the center, suspended by a web of conduits and magnetic emitters, was the Cascade Engine. It was larger than he had imagined, a perfect sphere of polished black alloy, humming with latent power. Lights played across its surface, a slow, rhythmic pulse like a sleeping beast.

And there, visible through a reinforced viewport in the sphere’s side, was the conduit. His conduit. It glowed with a soft, internal energy, the source of the entire station’s current predicament.

There was no one in the chamber itself, but he could see technicians moving on the upper levels, their faces illuminated by glowing control panels. They were prepping for the test.

The access point he needed was directly below the engine. A small panel that led to the capacitor bank for the lower magnetic clamps. He found the corresponding grate in the floor of the duct. This one was smaller, and electronically locked.

“No time for subtlety,” he muttered. He pulled out his multi-tool, found the right attachment, and jammed it into the lock’s interface. He bypassed the security handshake, sending a surge of power that fried the mechanism with a silent spark. The lock clicked open.

He squeezed through the opening, dropping silently to the floor of a small maintenance crawlspace directly beneath the humming engine. The air here was hot, thick with the smell of ozone. A series of thick, armored cables snaked from the engine’s underbelly into a row of junction boxes. The capacitors.

He found the one labeled ‘Lower Clamp 3’. According to the schematic, this was the one. Reversing its polarity required re-routing the internal power flow. It was delicate work. His fingers, numb with cold just moments before, were now slick with sweat. He opened the panel. A complex web of fiber-optic relays and power couplings greeted him.

He referenced the diagram on his datapad, his eyes darting between the screen and the panel. Disconnect the primary feed. Engage the secondary bypass. Cross-connect the emitter flow. It was like performing surgery on a live bomb.

Suddenly, a voice boomed from the chamber above, amplified by the station’s comm system. “All personnel, clear the core chamber. Final calibration sequence initiated. T-minus ten minutes to full power activation.”

Ten minutes.

Jack’s hands flew. He worked with a desperate precision, sweat beading on his forehead and dripping into his eyes. He ignored it. He felt Salty, a small, reassuring presence, watching from the duct above.

He slid the final relay into its new, incorrect position. The small indicator light on the capacitor, which had been green, flickered and turned amber. It was done. It was armed. The next time the system tried to draw full power, it would tear itself apart.

Now he just had to not be here when it happened.

He scrambled back toward the duct, pulling himself up. Just as his feet cleared the crawlspace, a klaxon began to blare. Not the loud, station-wide alarm, but a localized, high-pitched shriek. Red lights began to flash within the maintenance space.

“Energy fluctuation detected in lower capacitor bank three!” a voice yelled from above. “Security to the sub-level! Now!”

He had been too slow. Or the system was more sensitive than the Syndicate had known.

There was no time for the quiet way back. He slammed the grate shut, not bothering to lock it, and ran. He sprinted through the freezing duct, his boots skidding on the slick floor. Salty ran ahead, a blur of motion in the darkness.

Behind them, he could hear the clang of boots on metal as security guards entered the duct system. Shouts echoed down the polished steel tube.

They reached the exit grate. Jack used the plasma torch, forgetting the dampener, and sliced through the cheap hinges in seconds. He burst back out into the service corridor, a wild man emerging from the walls.

“Go!” he yelled.

They didn't look back. They raced through the maze of forgotten tunnels, following the path back to the docking bay. The sounds of pursuit faded behind them, lost in the labyrinth. They burst out from behind the waste disposal panel, back into the relative normalcy of the lower decks. People gave them strange looks, a sweaty man in dark clothes and a strange creature running as if their lives depended on it. They weren't wrong.

He saw the *Stardust Drifter* and the mechanic standing by the ramp, holding out a datapad.

“All done,” the mechanic said, a grin on his face. “She’s purring like a kitten. That’ll be twelve hundred credits.”

Jack didn’t slow down. He slapped a credit chip into the man’s hand without checking the balance. “Keep the change.”

He and Salty ran up the ramp. Jack hit the button to close it, then sprinted to the cockpit. He threw himself into the pilot’s chair, his hands flying across the console, muscle memory taking over. Power up, pre-flight checks, undocking clamps release.

“Docking control, this is *Stardust Drifter* requesting immediate departure,” he said into the comm, his voice still ragged.

“You’re cleared, *Drifter*,” came the bored reply. “Mind the outbound freighter from Bay 12.”

Jack didn’t need to be told twice. He pushed the thrusters forward, and the ship lifted from its cradle. He guided it out of the docking bay, the massive station filling his viewport. Somewhere deep inside that metal sphere, a bomb of his own making was ticking.

As soon as they were clear of the station’s local traffic control, he pulled up the Syndicate’s secure channel. He typed a single, pre-arranged phrase: *The conduit is broken*.

He didn’t have to wait long. A reply appeared. *Debt satisfied. A bonus has been transferred to your account. Advise you depart the system. Immediately.*

Jack took a shuddering breath and pushed the ship’s sublight engines to their maximum. The station shrank behind them, a monument to his terrible choices. Salty hopped onto his lap, a series of soft, calming clicks vibrating through his chest. Jack stroked the creature’s smooth carapace, his hand still trembling.

They had done it. They had survived. They were free of the Syndicate. But as he stared into the star-dusted blackness ahead, he knew he had just traded one enemy for another. Silas was no fool. He would figure out what had happened. He would know it was sabotage. And he would hunt the man who had delivered the final, crucial component and then disappeared just as it was all about to begin.

The job wasn't done. The running had just begun.
