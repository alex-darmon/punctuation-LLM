Alistair grabbed the thick safety rope attached to Gregory’s harness. “I will not let you fall, my friend.”

Gregory gave a grim nod, a set of specialized wrenches already in his hand. “Just make sure you pull me back in when I’m done.”

The Professor eased back on the throttles. The Kestrel’s frantic forward momentum bled away, the high-pitched scream of the engines lowering to a deep, protesting groan. The vessel felt sluggish, buffeted by the powerful winds.

Gregory unlatched the main observation hatch. A hurricane of cold air blasted into the cabin, whipping papers from the navigation table and tearing at their clothes. With Alistair bracing himself, the rope wrapped tightly around a reinforced stanchion, Gregory crawled out onto the narrow catwalk that ran along the Kestrel’s flank.

The world outside was a dizzying blur of motion. The wind roared in his ears, a physical force that threatened to peel him from his precarious perch. Below, the grey, choppy sea stretched to the horizon, a thousand-foot drop that promised instant oblivion. He clung to the handrail, his knuckles aching with the strain, and began to inch his way towards the sputtering port engine.

The engine housing was hot to the touch, even through his thick gloves. Smoke and steam vented from the seams. He could see the problem immediately. The primary intake grille, a fine mesh designed to filter the air, was coated in a black, glassy crust. Shards of obsidian, fused together by the engine’s heat, had formed a near solid sheet, starving the turbine of air.

He braced himself against the fuselage, his legs shaking with the effort of resisting the wind. He pulled a heavy, clawed wrench from his belt and began to attack the blockage. With each blow, sharp fragments of the volcanic glass broke free and were instantly whipped away into the slipstream. It was like chipping away at stone.

Inside, Alistair watched, his heart in his throat. He kept the safety line taut, ready to haul back with all his strength at the slightest sign of trouble. The Professor, his face beaded with sweat, kept his eyes glued to his instruments, making constant, minute adjustments to the ailerons and rudder, fighting to keep the Kestrel on an even keel.

A particularly violent gust of wind slammed into the air-vessel. The Kestrel lurched sideways. Gregory lost his footing, his heavy boots skidding on the metal catwalk. He cried out as he was thrown against the engine nacelle, his grip on the handrail slipping. For one horrifying second, he dangled over the abyss, held only by the safety line.

“Gregory!” Alistair screamed, digging his heels in and pulling with all his might. The rope bit into his hands.

Gregory swung wildly, his tools clattering against the hull. He managed to get one hand back on the rail, his muscles screaming in protest. He hauled himself back onto the catwalk, his chest heaving. He gave a quick, shaky thumbs up towards the cockpit window before turning back to his task, his movements now filled with a renewed, desperate fury. He hammered at the glassy crust, ignoring the pain in his arms, ignoring the terrifying drop below. He had a city to save.

With a final, savage blow, a large section of the blockage cracked and shattered. The rest of the fused glass imploded inwards, sucked into the suddenly freed intake. The engine, which had been coughing and sputtering, let out a deafening roar as air flooded its combustion chamber. It surged with renewed power.

The Kestrel lurched forward violently, the sudden thrust from the port engine nearly throwing Gregory from his feet again. He clung on for dear life as the Professor fought to balance the power, slowly bringing the starboard engine up to match it.

“He’s done it!” Alistair yelled in triumph. “He’s done it!” He began to haul on the rope, pulling a weary, trembling Gregory back towards the hatch. The big mechanic tumbled back inside, collapsing onto the deck, his face pale but a grin of success spreading across it.

“Never… a dull moment… with you, Alistair,” he gasped, as Alistair slapped him on the back.

The Professor pushed the throttles forward. The Kestrel, both engines now singing in harmony, surged ahead, clawing back its lost speed. They were back in the race. But they had lost nearly forty precious minutes.

As the sun began to dip towards the western horizon, casting long shadows across the rolling green fields of France below, the three of them gathered around the navigation table. The immediate danger had passed, but the greater one loomed ever closer.

“We have a target, but London is a metropolis of millions,” Alistair said, pointing at the chart. “Where would he place such a device? It would need to be positioned for maximum effect.”

The Professor stroked his chin, his mind racing. “He would need height. A sonic wave of that magnitude would be affected by buildings. It would create sonic shadows, areas of less destruction. To level the city, as he claims, he would need to broadcast the wave from a high, central point. Like ripples in a pond, but with sound.”

“A church steeple? The dome of Saint Paul’s Cathedral?” Gregory suggested, pouring them all a cup of strong, hot tea from a thermos.

“Possible,” the Professor conceded. “But Blackwood is not just a destroyer. He is a creature of immense ego and theatricality. He despises the Empire and its symbols. He would not just destroy London. He would want to do it in a way that was, to his twisted mind, poetic.”

Alistair’s eyes went distant. He pictured the map in Blackwood’s control room, the red line piercing the city’s heart. He remembered the villain’s arrogant pronouncements, his hatred for the chimes of Big Ben, the sound he claimed regulated the lives of slaves.

A terrible realization dawned on him. “The Clock Tower,” he whispered.

The Professor and Gregory looked at him.

“The Clock Tower at the Palace of Westminster,” Alistair said, his voice growing stronger. “It’s one of the most famous symbols of the Empire. It’s tall. It’s central. And it contains the great bell, Big Ben.”

The Professor’s eyes widened. “The bell… of course! He wouldn’t just place a device on the tower. He would use the bell itself! The bell is a massive, perfectly cast bronze resonator. If he were to attach his sonic generator to it, he could use the bell as a colossal amplifier. He could tune his device to the bell’s own resonant frequency, and when activated, it would broadcast a wave of such power… it would be the death knell of London, quite literally.”

“He would turn the city’s most iconic sound into its own executioner,” Gregory breathed, the color draining from his face. “That’s just the sort of evil, clever trick he would pull.”

“Then that is where we must go,” Alistair declared, his fist closing on the table. “Professor, can you get us there without being seen? The Royal Air Navy will have patrols over the city.”

“The approach will be treacherous,” the Professor admitted. “But I know the river. There are old, disused wharves downriver from the Tower Bridge, remnants of a bygone shipping era. I can bring the Kestrel down in the industrial haze and shadow. We could moor there undetected, at least for a short time. From there, we would have to proceed on foot.”

Night had fallen completely by the time they crossed the English Channel. The string of lights along the coast of Kent was a welcome, yet somber sight. This was what they were fighting for. Every pinprick of light was a home, a family, a life that was now in mortal peril.

As they flew deeper into England, the sky ahead began to glow with a faint, orange luminescence. It was the reflected light of London, a sprawling beacon of civilization. From their height, it looked peaceful, majestic. A sleeping giant, unaware of the dagger aimed at its heart.

“We are thirty minutes out,” the Professor announced, his voice a low murmur. He began the Kestrel’s slow, careful descent, dipping below the main air-lanes.

Alistair checked his Webley revolver, ensuring it was loaded. He strapped a climbing harness over his clothes and packed a satchel with rope, a set of Gregory’s modified cutting tools, and two of the Professor’s more experimental devices: a handheld galvanic disruptor and a small, potent explosive charge designed for precise demolition. Gregory, meanwhile, was sharpening a rather large, menacing looking blade he simply called ‘The Persuader’.

“We have one chance at this,” Alistair said, his voice low and steady. He looked at his two companions. The brilliant, aging inventor and the steadfast, loyal mechanic. An unlikely trio to stand between a madman and the annihilation of a great city. “Whatever happens down there, it has been an honor.”

“The honor is all ours, Alistair,” the Professor replied, a faint smile on his lips. “Now let us go and make sure there is a city left for us to save.”

Gregory simply nodded, his jaw set. “Let’s go knock his clock off.”

The Kestrel descended into the thick, smoky air above the eastern sprawl of the city. The Professor navigated by the familiar landmarks of the Thames, a winding black ribbon dotted with the lights of bridges and ships. He found the derelict wharf he was seeking, a place of crumbling brick warehouses and rotting piers. With incredible skill, he brought the air-vessel to a silent hover just feet above a muddy, debris-strewn courtyard, hidden from the river and the street by the looming skeletons of forgotten industry.

With a soft hiss of hydraulics, the mooring lines were deployed, and the Kestrel settled into the shadows. The engines powered down, and an eerie silence fell, broken only by the distant lap of the river and the low hum of the city.

They had arrived. But as Alistair stepped out into the damp, cold London air, he knew their race was far from over. It had just entered its final, most desperate leg. Somewhere, in the heart of the city, under the face of the world’s most famous clock, a madman was preparing to strike midnight for an entire civilization. And the clock was ticking.

The air was thick with the ghosts of industry. It smelled of cold river mud, damp brick, and the faint, acrid tang of coal smoke that seemed to be a permanent part of the London atmosphere. Alistair drew it into his lungs, a familiar poison that tasted of home. He held the Webley steady, scanning the deep shadows that clung to the corners of the courtyard. The Kestrel was a great black shape behind them, a silent predator at rest, utterly invisible against the soot stained wall of a warehouse whose windows were all blind eyes.

“Professor, our position,” Alistair said, his voice a low command that did not carry more than a few feet.

The Professor, huddled over a small brass and leather case, unfolded a beautifully detailed map of the city’s labyrinthine underworld. A small, self contained glow lamp, one of his own inventions, cast a soft, controlled circle of light upon the parchment. “We are here,” he whispered, his finger tracing a line from a depiction of their wharf to a nearby street. “Limehouse. The entrance we need is approximately two hundred yards north east. An old storm drain access point. According to the schematics from the Metropolitan Board of Works, it provides the most direct route to the primary sewer trunk line running west towards Westminster.”

Gregory hefted his own satchel, the tools inside clinking softly. He sniffed the air and wrinkled his nose. “Sewers. Right then. Knew this night was getting too glamorous.”

“It will keep us off the streets, Gregory,” Alistair reminded him. “Mor-Vane’s men will be watching the main thoroughfares. Down there, we have the advantage of darkness and surprise.”

“And rats the size of terriers, I’ll wager,” Gregory muttered, but he squared his broad shoulders. He was ready.

They moved as one, a tight unit of three, melting into the warren of derelict buildings. Alistair led, his feet making no sound on the slick cobblestones. The Professor followed, surprisingly nimble for his age, clutching his precious map case. Gregory brought up the rear, his head on a constant swivel, his large frame a comforting, solid presence in the oppressive dark. The ambient glow of London was muted here, a distant, hazy promise of a world that was still awake, still alive. They passed skeletal cranes and piles of rotting crates, monuments to a commerce long dead. The silence was unnerving. It felt as if the city itself was holding its breath.

They found the access point exactly where the Professor’s map indicated, tucked away in a narrow alley between two towering brick behemoths. It was a heavy, circular plate of cast iron, rusted firmly into the stone. “Allow me,” Gregory said, setting his bag down. He took out a long, flattened crowbar and a small vial of clear, viscous liquid.

“A potent acid,” the Professor explained quietly as Gregory carefully dripped the fluid around the rim of the cover. “It will dissolve the rust without creating any significant noise or vapor.”

They waited for a minute as the chemical did its work, a faint sizzle the only sound in the alley. Then, Gregory wedged the tip of the crowbar into the gap. He braced his feet, his muscles bunching under his thick coat. With a low groan of tortured metal, the cover shifted. He worked it back and forth, slowly, deliberately, avoiding any sudden clang that might echo through the empty streets. Finally, with a soft scrape, the cover was pushed aside, revealing a black, circular maw that seemed to swallow the faint light. A wave of cool, foul air washed over them.

Gregory peered down. “Smells worse than I imagined. And I’ve got a vivid imagination.”

“The price of stealth,” Alistair said. He uncoiled a rope from his pack and secured it to the base of a nearby, and thankfully solid, iron bollard. He went first, disappearing into the darkness without a word. A moment later, a soft tug on the rope signaled he was down. The Professor, more cautious, followed. Gregory came last, pulling the heavy cover back into place above them, plunging them into absolute, suffocating blackness. For a moment, there was nothing. No light, no sound save for a steady, rhythmic dripping somewhere nearby.

Then, a small flame sputtered to life. It was the Professor’s glow lamp, its light now seeming impossibly bright. It revealed a circular brick chamber and an iron ladder, slick with slime, descending another ten feet to a narrow walkway. Below that, a channel of dark, sluggish water flowed into a much larger tunnel. They were in.

The Professor consulted his map again, orienting himself by the direction of the water’s flow. “This way. We must follow this feeder tunnel until it joins the main artery. Stay close, and be mindful of your footing. Some of these walkways have not been repaired since the Great Stink.”

They proceeded in single file, the single lamp casting their elongated shadows against the curved brick walls. The air was heavy and cold, thick with the stench of decay and effluent. The only sounds were the slosh of their boots through shallow puddles, the constant drip, drip, drip of water from the ceiling, and the distant, unseen rush of the main sewer. It was a disorienting, subterranean world, a hidden city beneath the city.

They had been walking for what felt like an hour when Alistair, in the lead, suddenly raised a hand, freezing them in place. He pressed himself against the wall, extinguishing the small electric torch he had been using. The Professor immediately doused his own lamp, and they were once again in total darkness.

“What is it?” Gregory whispered, his voice a low growl.

“Ahead,” Alistair breathed back. “Light. And voices.”

Slowly, carefully, Alistair crept forward. He peered around a bend in the tunnel. About fifty yards ahead, the narrow walkway opened into a much larger junction, a cavernous space where several massive tunnels converged. In the center of the junction, a temporary looking platform had been erected over the churning water. On it sat a strange, brass apparatus, a web of wires and vacuum tubes humming with power. Two men in heavy coats stood guard. They were armed with advanced looking rifles, their polished steel barrels a stark contrast to the grime of the sewer.

“His men,” Alistair reported back in a whisper. “Guarding some sort of listening post, I think.”

“The scoundrel,” the Professor hissed. “He’s wired the sewers. A network of acoustic and pressure sensors. He would have known the moment we entered the system if we had used a more common access point. We were fortunate to have chosen this disused branch.”

“Two of them,” Gregory said, his hand already reaching for the hilt of The Persuader. “We can take them.”

“Silently,” Alistair stressed. “They may have a way to signal an alarm. Professor, that device you gave me. The galvanic disruptor. Will it work on their equipment?”

“At that range, yes,” the Professor confirmed. “It should overload any delicate circuitry. Communications, sensors, anything of that nature. It will be a momentary surge. They might think it a simple malfunction.”

“Good. Gregory, you are with me. Professor, stay here. And be ready.”

Alistair unholstered the disruptor. It was a compact device, a series of brass coils around a crystal core, with a pistol grip. He took aim, his arm braced against the brickwork. Gregory was a coiled spring beside him, his massive blade held in a reverse grip, its polished surface drinking the faint, ambient light.

“On my mark,” Alistair whispered. He took a breath, let it out, and squeezed the trigger.

There was no sound, but a faint blue shimmer pulsed from the device. Down the tunnel, the lights on the enemy apparatus flickered violently. A shower of sparks erupted from a control panel. One of the guards cursed, turning to fiddle with a dial. The other took a step towards the edge of the platform, peering into the darkness of their tunnel. “What was that?” he called out, his voice echoing.

That was their moment. Alistair and Gregory moved as one, a surge of silent motion. They were ghosts in the darkness, their boots splashing through the muck. The guard who was looking their way barely had time to register their approach before Alistair was on him. A hard, precise strike to the temple with the butt of his Webley sent the man crumpling to the walkway without a sound.

The second guard, still distracted by the malfunctioning equipment, turned at the last second. His eyes widened, his mouth opening to shout a warning. But the warning never came. Gregory’s arm shot out, his hand clamping over the man’s mouth while his other arm locked around his chest, lifting him clean off his feet. There was a brief, muffled struggle, a sharp crack, and then the body went limp. Gregory laid the man down gently, his expression grim. It was a brutal business, but a necessary one.

The entire engagement had lasted less than ten seconds. The Professor hurried forward, his eyes wide. “Excellent work, gentlemen. Positively surgical.”

He immediately turned his attention to the damaged device, his fingers probing the smoking circuitry. “As I suspected. A relay station. It amplifies signals from sensors embedded throughout the main sewer lines and transmits them to a central hub. Undoubtedly at the tower itself. Mor-Vane has turned Bazalgette’s masterpiece into his own private nervous system.” He pulled a wire here, disconnected a vacuum tube there. “This station is now deaf and dumb. But there will be others. We cannot risk the main routes.”

He pulled out his map once more, tracing a new path with his finger. “We must divert. Through the old Fleet river conduit. It is older, smaller, and more treacherous. But it is not on any modern schematic. It will be unmonitored.”

Their new path took them into tunnels that were claustrophobically narrow. The brickwork was crumbling, and in some places, the walkway had fallen away entirely, forcing them to edge along narrow ledges, the dark, fetid water churning just inches below their boots. The air grew thicker, more stagnant. Once, a tide of rats, a chittering, furry wave, swept past them, fleeing some unknown disturbance. Gregory stamped his foot, and they parted around him like water around a stone.

Time was a fluid, uncertain thing in the darkness. They had no view of the moon or stars, no sense of the world above. Their only measure was the ticking of the Professor’s pocket watch and the growing weariness in their bones. The strain was beginning to show on the old inventor. His breath came in ragged gasps, and he stumbled on a loose brick.

Gregory’s hand shot out, steadying him. “Easy does it, Professor. Almost there.”

“Thank you, Gregory,” the Professor panted, leaning against the wall for a moment. “My mind is still willing, but the flesh, it seems, is filing a complaint.”

Alistair looked at him with concern. “We can rest for a moment.”

“No,” the Professor said, straightening up, his jaw set with renewed determination. “There is no time. Midnight waits for no man, not even a tired old one.”

Just then, a deep, resonant sound echoed through the tunnels. It was not a sound of the sewer. It was a bell. Bong. Bong. Bong. Eleven chimes. It was eleven o’clock. A grim silence fell over them. One hour.

“Come on,” Alistair urged, his voice tight. “We have to move faster.”

They pushed on, their pace quickening, their exhaustion forgotten in the face of the stark deadline. The Professor’s alternate route proved as treacherous as he had warned. At one point, they came to a complete stop. A massive, iron sluice gate, green with corrosion and age, blocked the tunnel. It was a relic of a bygone era, its lifting mechanism a tangle of rusted chains and gears.

“Blast,” Alistair muttered, shining his torch on the immovable obstacle. “My explosive charge would be too much. It could bring the whole tunnel down on us.”

Gregory stepped forward, a glint in his eye. He ran a hand over the rusted gear housing. “No need to blow it up, lad. Just need to persuade it.” He dropped his satchel and produced a large wrench and a can of lubricating oil. “This old girl just needs a bit of mechanical sympathy.”

For the next twenty minutes, which felt like an eternity, Gregory worked on the ancient mechanism. He oiled the frozen gears, tapped at pins with a small hammer, and put his back into turning a huge, rust-fused wheel. The Professor provided light and quiet technical advice, pointing out the load bearing points and the likely sources of the seizure. Alistair stood guard, his revolver in hand, listening to the groans of the metal, praying that each sound was not the precursor to a collapse.

Finally, with a tremendous, shuddering screech that set Alistair’s teeth on edge, the main gear turned. The massive gate began to rise, slowly, agonizingly, with Gregory straining at the wheel like a titan from mythology. It lifted just enough, perhaps four feet, creating a gap at the bottom.

“That’s all she’s got in her!” Gregory gasped, sweat beading on his forehead. “Go! Now!”

Alistair slid under first, followed by the Professor. Gregory let go of the wheel. The gate slammed back down with a deafening boom that echoed like a cannon shot through the subterranean world. He scrambled under just before it hit, rolling to his feet, breathing heavily.

“So much for stealth,” he grumbled, wiping his greasy hands on his coat.

“It cannot be helped,” Alistair said. “Anyone who heard that will take time to investigate. We must use that time.”

They moved on, and soon, the character of the tunnels began to change again. The air grew fresher, and the distant rumble of the city above became more pronounced. The Professor, checking his map for the final time, pointed upwards. “Here. This ladder should lead to a sub-basement of the Palace of Westminster itself.”

They found the ladder, clean and dry, ascending into a square of lesser darkness above. Alistair went up, pushing on the manhole cover at the top. It lifted easily. He poked his head through, scanning the area before climbing out. He was in a small, stone walled chamber filled with a forest of pipes and humming with the sound of modern machinery. It smelled of ozone and hot metal. He helped the others up, and Gregory eased the cover back into place. They were out of the sewers.

But the humming Alistair had noticed was deeper than just the building’s utilities. It was a powerful, rhythmic thrumming that seemed to vibrate in their very bones, a low, oppressive drone that was both sound and feeling.

The Professor’s face went pale in the dim light. He held up a small, handheld device from his pocket, a meter with a quivering needle. “The vibration… the harmonic frequency… it’s a temporal resonator,” he said, his voice a horrified whisper. “Heavily amplified. Mor-Vane has already activated the primary stage of the device.”

As if on cue, another sound cut through the air. It was not an echo from a distant part of the city. It was directly above them, a sound of immense weight and power. The first chime of the great bell of the Elizabeth Tower. Bong. A quarter hour strike. It was eleven forty five.

“Fifteen minutes,” Alistair said, his voice dangerously quiet. He looked up, as if he could see through the stone floors, through the labyrinth of offices and chambers, to the iconic clock face that was now a doomsday clock. “He’s in the tower. Let’s go.”

They found a maintenance door and stepped out into a deserted stone corridor, lit by bare electric bulbs. The thrumming was louder here, a palpable presence in the air. The race was not over. They had only reached the final, vertical battlefield. Fifteen minutes to climb the most famous clock tower in the world and defuse a device that could erase London from history. The clock was no longer ticking. It was tolling.

The corridor was a tight, stone-lined artery deep in the heart of the ancient building. The thrumming was no longer a distant threat; it was a physical assault. It pressed in on them, a solid wall of sound that made their teeth ache and their vision swim at the edges. The air, thick and heavy, tasted of static electricity and something else, a coppery tang like an old coin held on the tongue. It was the taste of time itself being warped.

“This way,” Alistair commanded, his voice tight but clear above the drone. He moved with a predatory focus, his gaze sweeping the length of the corridor. The bare bulbs overhead flickered in sympathy with the resonator’s pulse, casting their shadows in a frantic, stuttering dance.

The Professor held his meter aloft. The needle was no longer quivering; it was pinned hard against the far right of its dial, vibrating so furiously it was a blur. “The energy is being focused upwards,” he stated, his voice strained. “Directly into the tower’s foundation. He has turned the entire structure into a temporal tuning fork.”

“Less talk of forks, Professor,” Gregory grumbled, his big hands clenched into fists. “More finding the stairs.”

They reached an intersection of corridors, a sterile junction of stone and white-painted brick. A small, brass sign was affixed to the wall, its letters tarnished with age. ‘Elizabeth Tower,  Maintenance Access’. Below it, a heavy oak door bound with iron stood sentinel. It looked as if it had not been opened since the reign of Queen Victoria.

Alistair tried the handle. It was, as expected, locked solid. “Gregory.”

Gregory cracked his knuckles, a sound like logs splitting. He produced a thin, wicked looking piece of steel from his coat pocket, a custom made tension wrench. “An old Chubb lock. A good one. But time is one thing we don’t have to pick it.” He put the tool away and stepped back, eyeing the hinges. “A matter of leverage, then.” He wedged his fingers into the crack between the door and the frame. He set his feet, his massive frame coiled like a spring. With a grunt that was more animal than human, he heaved.

Wood groaned in protest. Old splinters flew. The iron hinges, set deep into the stone, whined under the immense strain. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, with a percussive crack that echoed down the hall, the top hinge ripped free from the doorjamb. A second heave, and the bottom one followed. The door sagged inwards, held only by the lock’s bolt. Gregory gave it a final, contemptuous shove with his shoulder. It burst open, slamming against the stone wall inside.

They were in. The base of the tower was a spiral of narrow, stone steps, climbing up into darkness. The thrumming was a deafening roar here, a physical force that tried to push them back. They started to climb, Alistair in the lead, his hand resting on the silver head of his cane. The Professor followed, Gregory taking the rear, his bulk filling the narrow stairwell.

They had ascended perhaps fifty feet when Alistair stopped dead. He raised a hand, silencing their heavy breathing. From the darkness above, a new sound emerged. It was a rhythmic, wet scraping, accompanied by a low, electronic hum that was dissonant to the resonator’s drone.

Two figures emerged from the gloom. They were men, dressed in the dark uniforms of Parliamentary security guards, but they moved all wrong. Their steps were not fluid. They descended with a stuttering, unnatural gait, as if they were frames in a reel of film, with some of the intervening frames missing. One moment a foot was on a step, the next it was two steps lower, with no discernible motion in between.

“Chrono-troopers,” the Professor whispered, his face ashen. “He’s armed them with localized time displacement fields. Be careful. They are not entirely in our present.”

The guards raised their batons. They were not standard issue truncheons. They glowed with a faint, blue light, crackling with the same energy that filled the air. One of them lunged, its movement a disorienting blur. It swung its baton at Alistair’s head.

Alistair moved with a speed that was entirely his own. He sidestepped, the baton hissing past his ear. He felt a peculiar coldness as it passed, a sensation of lost moments. His cane flashed out, the silver wolf’s head connecting with the guard’s wrist. There was a sound like shattering glass, and the guard cried out, dropping the baton. It clattered down the stone steps, its blue light fading.

The second guard swung at Gregory. The big man did not try to dodge. He brought his forearm up, catching the blow on his thick coat. A ripple of blue energy spread across the fabric. Gregory grunted, his face contorting not in pain, but in a strange, slow motion confusion.

“It stole a second from me,” he growled, shaking his head as if to clear it. “Felt it go.”

He lunged forward, grabbing the guard by the front of his uniform. The guard’s body flickered, becoming momentarily transparent. Gregory’s hands passed through him. The guard reappeared a step lower, off balance. Before he could recover, Alistair was on him. He brought his cane down in a sharp, brutal arc across the back of the man’s neck. The guard crumpled to the steps and did not move.

The first guard, clutching his broken wrist, tried to scramble back up the stairs. Gregory’s patience had run out. He grabbed the man by the ankle and hauled him back. The guard was lifted effortlessly from his feet and slammed against the unforgiving stone wall. He went limp, joining his partner in unconsciousness.

“Efficient,” Alistair noted, already moving upwards. “But it cost us time.”

They continued their ascent, the air growing colder as they climbed. The stone walls were slick with a strange condensation that did not feel like water. The thrumming grew in complexity, lower frequencies joined by a high pitched, keening whine that scraped at their nerves.

They reached a small landing, a circular chamber where the stairs paused before continuing their relentless spiral. And then they stopped.

“What is it?” Gregory asked, breathing heavily behind the Professor.

“We’ve been here before,” Alistair said, his voice low. He pointed with his cane to a fresh scuff mark on the stone floor. “I made that mark, checking for loose footing on our way up.”

The Professor consulted his device, his brow furrowed in concentration. “A temporal eddy. A feedback loop. The resonator is creating localized pockets where time repeats itself. We are climbing the same flight of stairs over and over again.”

“So we’re trapped?” Gregory’s voice was a low rumble of frustration.

“Not necessarily,” the Professor said, his eyes scanning the space around them. “These loops have a pulse, a rhythm. We passed through it once to get here. To escape, we cannot simply run through it again. We must break the cycle.” He closed his eyes, his head tilted as if listening to music only he could hear. “There. A null point. A seam between the repetitions. It lasts less than a second.”

“How do we hit a target that small?” Alistair asked.

“We must move in sync with it. But also against it,” the Professor muttered, thinking. “The loop resets on a downbeat. The pulse is one, two, three, four, RESET. One, two, three, four, RESET. We must move on the half beat, between four and the reset. All of us. At the same instant.”

“You want us to dance our way up the stairs?” Gregory scoffed.

“In a manner of speaking, yes,” the Professor said, deadly serious. “It is the only way. I will count. We must take a single step, onto the next flight of stairs, at the exact same moment. If one of us is out of sync, the loop will catch us and we will find ourselves back on this landing.”

Alistair nodded, his face grim. “We will do it. Professor, get ready to count. Gregory, on me.”

The Professor raised his hand, the tip of his finger tapping out the invisible rhythm in the air. The drone of the resonator seemed to fall into the same pattern, a monstrous metronome counting down their final minutes.

“Ready,” the Professor said. “And… one… two… three…” He paused, his eyes squeezed shut. “FOUR…”

A heartbeat of absolute silence seemed to descend on the stairwell, a vacuum in the roaring torrent of sound.

“NOW!”

In perfect unison, the three men stepped forward. For a terrifying instant, Alistair felt a pulling sensation, a nauseating lurch as if the world was trying to snap back into a familiar position. He saw the landing below them flicker at the edge of his vision. Then the feeling passed. They were on the next flight of stairs. The air felt different, sharper. The landing below was empty. They had broken through.

They did not pause to celebrate. They scrambled upwards, their lungs burning, their legs aching. The stairs seemed endless, a helical torment designed to exhaust them. They passed the silent, motionless clock weights, immense stacks of iron disks hanging in a deep shaft. They climbed past small, slit-like windows that offered brief, dizzying glimpses of the London skyline, a sea of glittering lights under a starless, overcast sky.

Finally, they emerged into a vast chamber, a place of ordered chaos. They were in the clock mechanism room. Giant gears, some taller than a man, meshed with intricate precision. Levers and cams moved in a slow, ponderous ballet, driven by the steady pull of the weights below. The ticking of the great clock was a thunderous, metallic heartbeat that shook the floor. Tick. Tock. Each sound was a sledgehammer blow. And through it all, the oppressive, bone-jarring hum of the temporal resonator was a constant, maddening presence.

A huge pendulum, over thirteen feet long, swung in a majestic, inexorable arc, its polished brass bob a gleaming disc of gold. This was the heart of the great clock, the timekeeper for the nation.

“Mor-Vane is above us,” Alistair shouted over the noise. “In the belfry!”

A door on the far side of the room, leading to the final set of stairs, was their goal. But their path was blocked. The gears, which should have turned with smooth regularity, were spasming. They would spin furiously for a few seconds, then shudder to a halt, then turn slowly, then reverse direction.

“He’s tampered with the escapement mechanism!” the Professor cried, pointing. “He’s feeding temporal energy directly into the clockwork! It’s unstable!”

As he spoke, a massive cogwheel spun without warning. Its iron teeth gnashed the air inches from Gregory’s face. He stumbled back, cursing.

“There’s a pattern,” Gregory said, his eyes narrowed. He was no clockmaker, but he understood machines. He saw the logic in the chaos. “It’s a sequence. Fast, slow, reverse, stop. It’s repeating. Look at the third pinion, there. It’s the driver for this whole section. When it stops, we have about three seconds to get past.”

They watched the mechanical madness for one full cycle. The gears whirred, ground, reversed, and then, for a brief moment, fell silent and still.

“Now!” Gregory yelled.

They dashed forward, ducking under one enormous, stationary wheel and weaving between the teeth of another. The Professor, less agile, stumbled. A smaller gear began to turn, its spokes threatening to catch his coat. Gregory, right behind him, grabbed the Professor’s collar and bodily hauled him forward, clear of the danger, just as the entire mechanism roared back to life with a deafening shriek of protesting metal.

They reached the far door and burst through it, finding another, much steeper and narrower set of iron stairs. This was the final climb. The air was suddenly bitingly cold, smelling of rain and ozone. They could hear the wind whistling above them.

They emerged into the belfry. It was an open chamber, exposed to the elements on all four sides through great, arched openings. The wind howled through the space, whipping at their clothes. In the center of the room hung the colossal bells of the quarter chimes, and looming over them all, the vast, brooding shape of Big Ben itself, the Great Bell. Thirteen and a half tons of silent, waiting bronze.

And standing before it, his back to them, was a tall, slender figure in a long, dark coat. Mor-Vane.

He had not placed his device next to the bell. He had integrated it. Crystalline conduits, pulsing with a sickening purple light, snaked around the bell’s immense form, converging on a complex apparatus of spinning gyroscopes and brass armatures fixed to its crown. The entire bell had been transformed into the crucial component of his machine. The resonator hum was loudest here, a sound that was so powerful it was almost silence, pressing on their eardrums until they felt they would burst.

Mor-Vane turned slowly. His face was pale and hawkish, dominated by intelligent, cold eyes that glittered with manic triumph. He was not surprised to see them. He looked amused.

“Alistair,” he said, his voice calm and cultured, yet carrying easily over the wind and the hum. “And the Professor. And your… associate. Right on schedule. I do so admire punctuality.”

“It’s over, Mor-Vane,” Alistair said, stepping forward. “Deactivate the device.”

“Over?” Mor-Vane laughed, a dry, rasping sound. “My dear Alistair, it has just begun. In less than three minutes, this bell will strike midnight. Its chime, amplified and focused by my resonator, will not merely be heard across London. It will propagate backwards through time. A single, pure, temporal note that will erase the city at its foundation. Not destruction. Deletion. A paradox that will cascade through the timeline, wiping this grubby, chaotic century from existence, and allowing me to prune the timeline as I see fit.”

“You are insane,” the Professor gasped.

“Insanity is repeating the same mistakes and expecting different results,” Mor-Vane countered, a flicker of irritation in his eyes. “History is nothing but a series of such mistakes. I am providing the solution.” He raised his left hand. A complex gauntlet of brass and crystal covered his forearm, glowing with the same purple energy as the device. “I’m afraid I cannot allow you to interfere.”

Suddenly, the world lurched. Mor-Vane, who had been standing fifteen feet away, was now directly in front of Alistair, his hand outstretched. Alistair reacted on pure instinct, throwing himself to the side. Mor-Vane’s fingers brushed his coat. Alistair felt an agonizing cold, a sense of years being leeched away from him in an instant. He rolled and came up to his feet, his heart hammering.

“His gauntlet allows him short, personal time jumps,” the Professor shouted. “He can displace himself by a few seconds at will!”

As the first of the Westminster Quarters began to chime from the smaller bells, a beautiful, haunting melody that was now a herald of doom, the fight began.

Mor-Vane flickered, appearing behind Gregory and striking him in the back with the gauntlet. Gregory cried out and stumbled, his body spasming as if struck by lightning. He aged visibly for a second, his hair flashing grey, his face wrinkling, before snapping back to normal. He collapsed to one knee, disoriented and weak.

“Professor, the device!” Alistair yelled, turning his full attention to Mor-Vane. “How do we stop it?”

The Professor was already scrambling towards the Great Bell, his meter in hand. “The energy is being channeled through a primary focusing crystal at the crown! If I can disrupt the harmonic frequency, I might be able to force a feedback loop!”

Alistair charged Mor-Vane. The villain simply vanished, reappearing by the arched opening, silhouetted against the stormy night sky. “You cannot fight what you cannot touch, Alistair. You are an analog man in a digital age.”

Alistair didn't waste his breath with a reply. He watched Mor-Vane’s eyes, his posture. The man was arrogant. He would telegraph his moves. Mor-Vane flickered again, aiming to appear at Alistair’s flank. But Alistair was already moving, not to where Mor-Vane was, but to where he was going to be. He swung his cane, not at a man, but at a space.

The silver wolf’s head connected solidly with Mor-Vane’s ribs the very instant he materialized. The villain grunted in genuine pain and surprise, stumbling back. For the first time, his composure was broken.

Meanwhile, the Professor had reached the bell. “Gregory! I need your help! The crystal is protected by a pressure casing. I cannot break it!”

Gregory, shaking off the effects of the temporal shock, staggered to his feet. He saw the casing the Professor was pointing to, a thick dome of reinforced brass. The second part of the quarter-hour chimes rang out, louder and more urgent.

He joined the Professor, grabbing the edges of the casing. “Stand back, Professor.” He dug his heels in and pulled. The muscles in his back and arms bulged, the veins standing out on his neck. Metal screeched in protest, but it held.

Alistair and Mor-Vane were a blur of motion. Mor-Vane jumped, struck, and jumped again. Alistair, forced onto the defensive, was a master of parries and dodges, his cane a silver extension of his will. He was taking glancing blows, each one feeling like a touch of ice, sapping his strength, slowing his reactions. He was losing.

The third chime sequence echoed across the sky. The final, longest melody was about to begin.

“It’s not moving!” Gregory roared in frustration, pulling on the casing with all his might.

“The resonance!” the Professor yelled. “It’s reinforcing the structural integrity! We have to time it! Pull on the moment of silence between the chimes!”

The fourth and final sequence of the Westminster Quarters rang out, the prelude to the midnight hour. Gregory waited. The last note faded. In the fractional second of silence before the first strike of midnight, he gave one last, monumental heave.

With a deafening crack, the brass casing tore free. Gregory was thrown backwards, the heavy dome clattering across the floor. The primary crystal was exposed, a fist-sized diamond of purple light, pulsing violently.

The first great chime of midnight began. BONG.

The sound wave was a physical blow. The entire tower shuddered. The crystal flared, the hum rising to an unbearable shriek.

Mor-Vane laughed, seeing his victory at hand. “Too late!”

Alistair, seeing his chance, did not attack Mor-Vane. He lunged for the discarded brass casing, grabbing it.

BONG. The second strike.

The Professor was fumbling in his satchel. “I need to introduce a counter-frequency!” He pulled out a device that looked like a large tuning fork with wires attached. “I have to touch it to the crystal!”

BONG. The third strike. Mor-Vane, seeing what the Professor was doing, time-jumped, appearing between the Professor and the bell, his gauntlet raised to strike the old man down.

But Alistair had anticipated him. As Mor-Vane materialized, Alistair threw the heavy brass casing with all his remaining strength. It spun through the air, not at Mor-Vane, but at the Great Bell itself.

BONG. The fourth strike. The casing struck the lip of the bell, a fraction of a second before the hammer. It didn't stop the hammer, but it introduced a discordant clang into the chime, a foul, ugly note.

The effect on the device was instantaneous. The pure temporal note it needed was corrupted. The crystal flashed violently, and a wave of raw energy erupted from it, not outwards, but inwards. A feedback loop.

BONG. The fifth strike, now horribly out of tune. Mor-Vane screamed as the feedback from his own device washed over him. He flickered uncontrollably, his body aging and regressing, a horrifying strobe effect of youth and decrepitude, before his gauntlet exploded in a shower of sparks. He was thrown against the far wall and collapsed in a heap.

BONG. The sixth strike. The Professor, seizing the moment, scrambled forward and touched his tuning fork to the convulsing crystal. There was a high pitched whine, and the purple light sputtered, flickered, and died.

The remaining six chimes of midnight rang out, their pure, sonorous tones returning as the temporal interference vanished. Bong. Bong. Bong. Bong. Bong. BONG.

The twelfth strike faded into the night. The terrible thrumming was gone. The only sounds were the howling of the wind and the ragged gasps of the three men.

Gregory slowly got to his feet, rubbing his shoulder. The Professor leaned against the now silent Great Bell, his face slick with sweat. Alistair walked over to the unconscious form of Mor-Vane, nudging him with the toe of his boot. He was alive, but his connection to time had been violently severed.

Below them, London glittered, oblivious. The rain had begun to fall, washing the grime from the air.

Alistair looked out through the archway, at the sprawling, infinite lights of the city he had just saved. “So much for pruning the timeline,” he said, his voice quiet.

The clock had struck midnight. And London was still there.

The silence that followed the final bell was heavier than the chimes had been. It was a dense, muffling quiet, filled with the ghost of a hum that no longer existed. Rain pattered against the stone of the belfry, a soft percussion that seemed a world away from the violence of the last few minutes.

The Professor was the first to speak, his voice thin and reedy. “The counter frequency held. It collapsed the resonant field into a single, unstable point. Into him.” He gestured with his tuning fork towards Mor-Vane’s crumpled form. “He tried to channel the entirety of London’s timeline through that crystal. When the note was corrupted, the channel reversed. It all flooded back into the nearest anchor. Him.”

Alistair looked down at his former mentor. Mor-Vane’s face, usually set in a mask of arrogant certainty, was slack. But it was more than unconsciousness. A flicker, almost too fast to see, passed over his features. For an instant, the face was that of a smooth skinned young man, eyes wide with discovery. Then it was a wizened ancient, skin like cracked parchment, a deathly rattle in his throat. It settled back to the familiar, middle aged man he was, but the echo of the other faces remained, a disturbing afterimage.

“What’s wrong with him?” Gregory asked, his voice a low rumble. He had retrieved his coat and was shrugging it on, wincing as the movement pulled at his bruised shoulder.

“He is adrift,” the Professor said, carefully packing his tuning fork device back into his leather satchel. “Imagine your life is a single thread, anchored at birth and death. Mor-Vane’s gauntlet allowed him to move along that thread at will, and even to tug on the threads of others. When the feedback loop struck him, it didn’t just break the gauntlet. It unspooled his thread. He is everywhere in his own timeline at once. Conscious of none of it.”

Alistair nudged the body again. No response. “So he’s trapped. A prisoner in his own mind.” There was a grim satisfaction in the thought, but it was hollow.

“For now,” the Professor corrected, his expression grave. “But such a state is not stable. The paradoxes of his own existence are warring within him. Eventually, he will either cease to be, creating a vacuum that could unravel the local timeline, or he will… break. Violently. And we cannot be certain what form that will take.”

The three men stood for a long moment, the weight of this new problem settling upon them. They had saved the city from a controlled demolition of its history. Now they faced the possibility of an uncontrolled implosion, centered on the man lying at their feet.

“We can’t leave him here,” Gregory stated, the pragmatist cutting through the temporal theory. “The coppers will find him in the morning. How do we explain this?” He gestured around the belfry, at the shattered dome, the scorched patch on the wall, the inert and bizarrely complex crystal device.

“He’s right,” Alistair said, his mind already working. “We can’t involve the authorities. And we can’t just dump him in the Thames.” He looked at the Professor. “Your workshop. It’s the only place secure enough.”

The Professor nodded slowly, his gaze distant. “Yes. My workshop. It will have to do. We will need to be careful. Very careful.”

Getting Mor-Vane down from the belfry was a graceless and exhausting task. He was a dead weight, heavier than he looked, and his occasional, disconcerting flickers between ages were unnerving to the touch. One moment Alistair would be gripping a firm, muscular arm, the next it would feel frail and bony, the skin loose and papery. They half carried, half dragged him down the seemingly endless spiral staircase, the stone steps worn smooth by centuries of footfalls. The air grew warmer and less exposed as they descended, the howling of the wind replaced by the steady, rhythmic ticking of the great clock’s mechanism. Gears the size of wagon wheels turned with ponderous, unstoppable force, their oiled surfaces gleaming in the light of Gregory’s lantern.

“The Temporal Concordance,” Alistair said, breaking the strained silence of their labor. “That’s what he called his little society. They won’t let this go. Mor-Vane was one of their chief architects.”

“I have heard the name,” the Professor said, pausing to catch his breath on a small landing. “Whispers in certain circles. They are not mere opportunists. They are ideologues. They believe time is a disease, a chaotic, branching mess that must be sterilized. Pruned, as you said. They see themselves as surgeons, cutting away the messy, unpredictable parts of history to create a clean, logical progression.”

“A progression that leads to them being in charge, I imagine,” Gregory grunted, adjusting his grip on Mor-Vane’s shoulders.

“Naturally,” Alistair said with a dry, humorless smile. “They believe only they have the intellectual fortitude and moral clarity to make such decisions. To decide which cities live, which empires fall, which billions of lives are simply… edited out. For the greater good, of course.” He had once believed in that mission, or a version of it. The idea of bringing order to the chaos of human existence had appealed to his younger, more arrogant self. But he had seen what that order cost. He had been sent to ‘correct’ an anomaly once, a small village in the Pyrenees that was not supposed to exist past the 14th century. The correction had been absolute. He had left the Concordance the next day.

“They are dangerous because they are so certain,” the Professor murmured, resuming the descent. “Certainty is the most powerful and destructive force in the universe. It allows a man to commit any atrocity with a clear conscience.”

They finally reached the ground floor, stumbling out of a small, unassuming door at the base of the tower into a narrow, cobbled alley. The rain had softened to a fine mist, hanging in the air and blurring the edges of the gaslights. London was sleeping, or at least, the respectable parts of it were. A lone hansom cab clattered by on the main thoroughfare, the horse’s breath pluming in the damp air.

“Now what?” Gregory asked, looking up and down the empty street. “We can’t very well hail a cab.”

The Professor was already walking, turning deeper into the labyrinth of backstreets and alleys. “Follow me. My workshop is not far. We must stay out of sight.”

They followed, a strange procession in the London night. Two exhausted men carrying a third, who was flickering through his own lifespan like a faulty cinematograph. They moved through a city of shadows and secrets, past darkened storefronts and shuttered windows. The smell of wet soot, coal smoke, and the river hung heavy in the air. For Alistair, every shadow seemed to hold a threat. He kept glancing over his shoulder, expecting to see the tailored suits and impassive faces of Concordance agents. They were patient. They were thorough. They would already know Mor-Vane had failed. They would be looking for him. For all of them.

After twenty minutes of walking, the Professor stopped before a narrow, three story building squeezed between a tobacconist and a dusty bookbinder’s shop. The ground floor was a cluttered storefront, the window displaying a bizarre assortment of sextants, astrolabes, and intricate brass clockwork mechanisms that seemed to serve no purpose. The sign above, barely legible in the gloom, read: “A. H. Quibble, Horologist & Philosophical Instrument Maker.”

The Professor produced a key and opened a side door that led into a dark, narrow hallway. The air inside was thick with the scent of brass polish, ozone, and old paper. He led them up another two flights of stairs to the top floor, which opened into a single, vast room.

It was the most wonderfully chaotic space Alistair had ever seen. The entire room was the Professor’s workshop, a hoarder’s paradise of science and mystery. Books were stacked to the ceiling. Diagrams and star charts were pinned to every available inch of wall space. Wires snaked across the floor, connecting humming dynamos to crackling glass spheres. A huge telescope pointed out of a domed skylight, currently slick with rain. In the center of the room was a heavy oak table, cleared of clutter as if in anticipation of their arrival.

“Place him there,” the Professor instructed, already shedding his coat and moving towards a large cabinet.

They laid Mor-Vane on the table. In the steady, warm light of the workshop’s gas lamps, his condition seemed even more pronounced. His hair would silver and recede, then grow thick and dark again. Wrinkles would web across his face and then vanish, all in the space of a few heartbeats. He began to murmur, his voice a low, incoherent jumble.

“...the final sequence is initiated… Mama, is the tea ready?… the curvature is unstable, we must account for the… I see it, Alistair, a perfect, clean timeline… I’m cold…”

Alistair felt a chill that had nothing to do with the night air. He was hearing fragments from Mor-Vane’s entire life, spoken at once. His first words, his last words, and everything in between, all tangled together.

The Professor returned from the cabinet carrying a device that looked like a physician’s stethoscope, but with a brass bell at the end instead of a diaphragm, and a series of delicate, humming wires connecting it to a glass gauge. He placed the bell on Mor-Vane’s chest. The needle in the gauge began to twitch, then swing wildly from side to side.

“Worse than I thought,” the Professor breathed, his face pale. “His personal temporal signature is in a state of quantum decoherence. He’s not just unspooled, he’s… fraying. The integrity of his existence is failing.”

“What does that mean, Professor?” Gregory asked, his hand resting on the grip of the pistol tucked into his waistband. “In plain English.”

“It means,” the Professor said, looking up at them, his eyes wide with a terrible understanding, “that he is becoming a hole in the world. A tiny, localized chronological black hole. Right now, it is contained within his own biological and quantum state. But if it breaches that containment, if he fully unravels… it could begin to erase things. Objects. People. Memories. It would spread outwards from him like a stain.”

Alistair stared at the man on the table. The man who had recruited him, who had taught him to see time as a weapon, who he had come to despise. Now, he was something far more dangerous than a mere villain. He was a bomb that could erase reality itself.

“Can you stop it?” Alistair asked, his voice tight.

The Professor ran a hand through his already wild grey hair. “Stop it? No. Good heavens, no. That would be like trying to re-assemble a shattered vase in the middle of a hurricane. The pieces are all there, but they are in motion, scattered across seventy years of spacetime. No, we cannot fix him.” He walked over to a dusty corner of the workshop, covered by a large canvas sheet. “But we might be able to contain him.”

He pulled the sheet away, revealing a contraption of brass, copper, and thick, green glass. It was a framework, roughly the size of a coffin, with intricate coils of copper wire wrapped around a series of powerful electromagnets. At its heart was a glass cylinder, large enough to hold a person, frosted with age and disuse.

“A chronostatic chamber,” the Professor announced, tapping one of the copper coils. “An old project of mine, from before I understood the true dangers. In theory, it generates a field of null-time. A bubble where time, for all intents and purposes, does not pass. If we place him inside and activate it, it should freeze him in his current state of temporal flux. It will not cure him, but it will put him, and the danger he represents, on pause. Indefinitely.”

Gregory looked from the murmuring, flickering man on the table to the coffin-like device. “So we put him in the box, turn it on, and he just stays there? Forever?”

“Precisely,” the Professor said. “A fly in amber. A very, very dangerous fly.”

“There’s a problem, I assume,” Alistair said, sensing the hesitation in the Professor’s voice. “It’s never that simple.”

“There is,” the Professor admitted with a sigh. “The chamber requires an immense amount of energy to initiate the stasis field. Far more than my small private generator can provide. It needs a massive, direct surge of power to bridge the initial quantum gap and establish the null-time bubble. Once established, it is self sustaining, but the ignition… that is the problem.”

He trailed off, looking out the rain-streaked window at the city below. Alistair followed his gaze, seeing the flickering gaslights, the occasional bright electric lamp of a wealthy residence. Power. The city was full of it, but it was diffuse, scattered. They needed a source. A massive, concentrated source.

Alistair’s mind raced. He thought of the city’s new electrical substations, heavily guarded. He thought of the generators for the underground railway. Then another image came to him, an image of Mor-Vane’s own technology. The crystal. The device in the belfry. It was designed to channel and focus enormous amounts of energy. Temporal energy, yes, but energy nonetheless.

“The crystal,” Alistair said, turning back to the Professor. “Mor-Vane’s device. You said the resonant field collapsed. But is the power source still there?”

The Professor’s eyes lit up with a sudden, fierce intelligence. “Alistair, you magnificent rogue! Of course! The primary crystal is a temporal capacitor. It drew its power from the background chronon radiation of the entire city, storing it. It failed to discharge properly. The energy is still in there, contained but unstable. It’s a bomb waiting to go off, but if we could tap it… if we could create a controlled discharge…”

“We could power your chamber,” Alistair finished.

“It would be extraordinarily dangerous,” the Professor cautioned, already pacing and muttering to himself, pulling diagrams from dusty shelves. “The energy is not electrical, not in the conventional sense. We would need to build a converter, a regulator… and we would have to go back up there. To the belfry.”

The thought of returning to the clock tower was not a pleasant one. But looking at the man on the table, whose skin was now flickering so fast he appeared almost translucent, Alistair knew they had no choice. They had stopped Mor-Vane’s plan. Now they had to clean up his mess.

“Gregory,” Alistair said, his voice firm, taking charge. “We’ll need tools. A lot of them. And rope. The Professor will tell you what he needs.” He turned to the old man, who was now frantically sketching on a piece of parchment. “Professor, draw me a list. We have to work fast. The Concordance won’t be idle. And I have a feeling our friend on the table isn't going to wait for us forever.”

As if on cue, a teacup sitting on a shelf a few feet from the table rattled, shimmered for a second, and then vanished. Not broken, not shattered. It was simply gone. Erased.

The three men looked at the empty space where it had been, then at each other. The rain continued to fall outside, washing the London streets, but in the cluttered workshop, a new and silent storm was gathering. Their night was far from over. As Alistair watched the Professor’s frantic sketching, a sudden, sharp knock echoed from the door downstairs.

It was not the knock of a late night reveller or a lost constable. It was precise, deliberate, and repeated three times.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Alistair froze, his hand instinctively going to the revolver hidden beneath his coat. Gregory moved to the top of the stairs, peering down into the darkness. The Professor stopped sketching, the charcoal stick poised over the parchment, his face a mask of sudden fear.

The silence of the workshop was absolute. The only sound was the incoherent muttering of the man on the table, and the quiet, insistent patter of the rain.

The knock came again, louder this time, more demanding. An unmistakable summons. Someone knew they were there.

Alistair held up a hand, a silent command for absolute stillness. Gregory, poised at the landing, flattened himself against the wall, his considerable bulk seeming to shrink into the shadows. The Professor, who had been on the verge of a new torrent of technical jargon, snapped his mouth shut with an audible click of teeth. His eyes, wide and terrified behind his spectacles, darted from the door to Alistair.

The knock was not just a sound. It was a declaration. It spoke of authority, of patience, and of an utter lack of concern for the hour. This was not a social call. It was a summons from an entity that did not expect to be kept waiting.

From the street below, a voice cut through the drumming of the rain, muffled by the thick oak of the door but clear in its cold precision. “Professor Alcuin, we know you are in there. So is the man known as Alistair Finch. By the authority of the Queen’s Concordance for the Regulation of Unsanctioned Sciences, you are ordered to open this door.”

Alistair’s blood ran cold. The Concordance. He had hoped they would be slower, still picking through the wreckage at the belfry, trying to make sense of the chaos. He had underestimated them. They were efficient. They were hounds, and they had the scent.

“They cannot have seen us enter,” Gregory whispered, his voice a low rumble.

“They didn’t need to,” Alistair breathed back, his mind racing. “They tracked the temporal disturbance. The energy spike from the clock tower would have been a beacon. And our friend on the table… he’s a walking anomaly. They likely followed his trail right to the Professor’s doorstep.”

The man on the table chose that moment to prove Alistair’s point. He let out a low, guttural moan, and a stack of books on a nearby shelf trembled violently. The leather bindings seemed to age and crack in an instant, turning to dust that drifted down and vanished before it hit the floor. The space they had occupied was now utterly empty.

“We cannot fight them,” the Professor hissed, his face pale as parchment. “They are not constables, Alistair. They have… methods. Instruments.”

“Then we don’t fight,” Alistair said, his eyes scanning the cluttered workshop. “We run.”

A heavy thud echoed from downstairs. They were starting on the door. They were not going to wait for an invitation.

Alistair’s gaze fell upon a boarded up section of the far wall, half hidden behind a towering cabinet of mineral specimens. It looked like an old window, bricked over decades ago. “Professor, what was there?”

The Professor followed his gaze, his mind momentarily pulled from his panic. “A window. It overlooked the old tanner’s yard. It was sealed up long before my time. The brickwork will be solid.”

“Gregory.” Alistair’s voice was sharp.

Gregory needed no further instruction. He moved to the cabinet, and with a grunt of effort that barely seemed to tax him, he shoved the colossal piece of furniture aside. It scraped across the floorboards with a deafening groan. Behind it was a rectangle of grimy, uneven brickwork set into the plaster.

Another splintering crash from downstairs. The door was beginning to give way.

“The list, Professor. And the tools,” Alistair commanded.

The Professor, jolted back into action, snatched the parchment from his desk and began grabbing items with a manic energy. He threw strange looking calipers, coils of copper wire, a set of finely geared wrenches, and a small, leather wrapped box into a sturdy canvas satchel. “The regulator components are crucial! The dampening field coils, we must have them!”

Gregory, meanwhile, had retrieved a heavy sledgehammer from a corner. He swung it without ceremony. The impact was titanic, sending a spiderweb of cracks through the old brick and mortar. Dust and grit filled the air. He swung again, and a chunk of brick fell away, revealing the wet, black darkness of the night outside.

“Faster, Gregory!”

The big man grunted, his muscles bunching under his coat as he rained blows upon the wall. The opening widened. Rain and wind immediately blasted into the workshop, sending loose papers flying.

A final, tremendous crash from below announced the demise of the front door. Heavy footsteps thudded on the floorboards. “Upstairs! Secure the area!” a voice shouted.

Gregory smashed a last hole large enough for a man to squeeze through. “Go!” he urged, stepping back.

“Professor, you first,” Alistair said, grabbing the old man’s arm. He practically shoved the Professor toward the opening. The old academic hesitated, looking at the two story drop into the muddy yard below.

“There is no time!” Alistair barked. He grabbed the coil of rope they had set aside and quickly looped it around a heavy, iron leg of the Professor’s workbench. He tossed the other end out into the night. “Go!”

The Professor, seeing the armed, dark coated figures of the Concordance agents starting up the stairs, scrambled through the hole with surprising agility. Alistair handed him the satchel of tools. He then turned to Gregory, who was hefting the sledgehammer, his eyes fixed on the doorway to the workshop.

“Gregory, no. We don’t have time for heroics. We need you.”

Gregory looked from the approaching agents to Alistair, his expression grim. He dropped the hammer with a clang and squeezed his large frame through the opening. Alistair was right behind him, his revolver now in his hand. He took one last look at the man on the table, whose form was now a constant, shimmering blur. They had to come back for him. They had to.

He slid through the hole just as the first Concordance agent burst into the room. The agent raised a strange, brass weapon, but Alistair was already gone, his hands grabbing the rope as his feet found purchase on the slippery brickwork of the outer wall.

Below, Gregory was helping the Professor to his feet in the muddy yard. Alistair slid down the rope, his boots splashing into a puddle of foul smelling water. He didn’t bother to retrieve the rope. They had to move. The yard was a maze of discarded barrels and rotting crates, offering temporary cover. From the broken window above, a beam of light sliced through the rain.

“This way,” Alistair whispered, pulling them into the deeper shadows of a narrow alley that smelled of gin and decay.

The journey to the St. Giles clock tower was a nightmare of stealth and endurance. Alistair led them through a warren of backstreets and courtyards he knew from a past life he rarely spoke of. The rain was their ally, masking their footsteps and blurring their forms in the gaslight. Twice they had to press themselves into darkened doorways as patrols of Concordance agents marched past, their faces grim under their tall hats, their long coats shedding water. They were not like the police. They moved with a soldier’s discipline, their eyes scanning every shadow.

The Professor, unused to such exertion, was flagging badly. His breath came in ragged gasps, and he stumbled frequently on the slick cobblestones. Gregory, despite carrying the heavy tool bag, moved with a surprising grace, often reaching out a steadying hand to keep the old man from falling.

Finally, they reached the edge of the public square. In its center, looming like a skeletal finger pointing at the angry sky, was the clock tower. Its base was swarming with Concordance agents. A perimeter had been established, with lanterns hung on temporary posts, casting a sterile, white light on the scene. The main entrance was guarded by at least four men. There was no getting in that way.

“They’ve sealed it tighter than a drum,” Gregory murmured, his voice low.

“Of course they have,” Alistair replied, his eyes tracing the lines of the tower. “But they are guarding the ground. They don’t expect an attack from above.” He pointed to an adjacent building, a grim looking counting house with a steeply pitched roof. “There. If we can get to that roof, we can cross to the tower’s first parapet. The storm damage should provide us cover and an entry point.”

Getting into the counting house was the easy part. A locked side door yielded to a few moments of Alistair’s focused attention with a set of slender metal picks. The building was dark and silent, smelling of old paper and dust. They climbed flight after flight of stairs until they reached a small maintenance hatch that led onto the roof.

The wind hit them with the force of a physical blow. The rain was a horizontal torrent, stinging their faces. The roof slates were slick with water and moss, treacherous and slick. Far below, the Concordance lanterns seemed small and distant.

“The rope, Gregory,” Alistair shouted over the howl of the wind.

Gregory uncoiled the second length of rope he had insisted on bringing. With the practiced ease of a sailor, he hurled a weighted end across the ten foot gap to the clock tower’s parapet. The grappling hook clattered against stone, scraped, and then caught firm on a decorative gargoyle. Gregory tested it, putting his full weight on the line. It held.

“I will go first and secure a second line,” Gregory said. It was not a suggestion.

He moved across the rope hand over hand, his immense strength making the perilous crossing look almost easy. He disappeared into the shadows of the tower. A few moments later, a second rope snaked back towards them.

“Professor. Your turn,” Alistair said, securing the new rope around the old man’s waist. “Just hold on. We have you.”

The Professor’s crossing was a terrifying ordeal. He was not a man of strength or nerve, and he dangled and spun wildly in the wind, his feet scrabbling for purchase on the sheer stone wall. Between them, Alistair and Gregory managed to haul him across the gap, finally pulling him over the parapet onto a narrow, windswept walkway. He collapsed there, shaking and soaked.

Alistair followed, moving with a cat’s grace, and untied the Professor. They had made it. They were inside the perimeter. They found a large, arched window where the stained glass had been blown out, and slipped into the cavernous darkness of the tower’s interior.

The ascent was eerie. The air was thick with the smell of ozone and burnt metal. Debris littered the winding stone staircase. At one point, they passed the giant pendulum, now motionless and hanging at a skewed angle. The silence was absolute, the familiar ticking of the great clock utterly gone. It felt like walking through the skeleton of a dead giant.

Finally, they reached the top. The belfry was a scene of utter devastation. The four great arches that opened to the city were shattered, and the wind howled through them, a mournful dirge. The great bell itself had a hairline crack running its length. The intricate machinery of the clock face was a mangled heap of gears and springs.

And in the very center, where Mor-Vane’s device had been, sat the crystal.

It was about the size of a man’s head, a multi faceted stone of breathtaking clarity. But it was not inert. A soft, violet light pulsed within its core, a slow and sickly heartbeat. The very air around it seemed to shimmer and warp, and a low, resonant hum vibrated through the stone floor. It was beautiful and terrible, a captured moment of cosmic violence.

“Incredible,” the Professor whispered, his earlier fear forgotten, replaced by a scholar’s awe. He stumbled forward, pulling his diagrams from the now soaked satchel. “The matrix is intact, but the containment field is degrading. You see the chronon bleed at the edges? It’s… it’s erasing the ambient particulate matter. The energy must be siphoned, and quickly.”

He laid out his plans on a section of relatively dry floor. He pointed to a series of brass fittings on the crystal’s cradle. “Gregory, I need you to brace the primary housing. It will vibrate violently once we begin the transfer. Alistair, these connection points. They must be attached in a precise sequence. The temporal flow must be routed through the converter before it enters the accumulator. If you connect them out of order, the feedback loop will be catastrophic. It will not explode. It will simply… un-happen. And us with it.”

Alistair knelt, studying the diagram, committing the sequence to memory. The device they were building was a strange contraption of wires, vacuum tubes, and geared brass that looked like it belonged in a madman’s laboratory. The ‘accumulator’ was the leather wrapped box, now opened to reveal a core of tightly wound copper coils around a small, dark crystal.

Gregory moved to the central device, planting his feet and taking hold of the heavy iron cradle. “I am ready.”

“Begin with the tertiary conduit, Alistair,” the Professor instructed, his voice tense. “Connect it to the regulator’s input valve.”

Alistair reached forward, his hands surprisingly steady. The air near the crystal was cold, and it made the hairs on his arms stand up. As his fingers worked to attach the first clamp, a flicker of movement caught his eye. For a split second, he saw the belfry not as it was, but as it had been. Intact, clean, with a younger looking Mor-Vane standing right where the Professor was, a triumphant smile on his face. The vision vanished, leaving Alistair with a lingering sense of vertigo.

“The secondary flow next!” the Professor urged. “Careful, the resonance will increase.”

As Alistair attached the second connection, the hum of the crystal rose in pitch. The violet light within it began to pulse faster. Gregory grunted, his knuckles white as he strained to hold the cradle steady against the increasing vibrations.

“Now, Alistair! The primary channel! This is the most dangerous part! The full energy stream will be diverted!”

Alistair took a deep breath, his fingers closing around the final, thick cable. He could feel the raw power thrumming through the metal, an energy that was not heat or electricity, but something far stranger, a current of pure time. He moved to connect it to the central port on the converter.

“Hold! In the name of the Concordance!”

The shout came from the stairwell. Three agents stood there, their dark coats flapping in the wind, their strange, brass weapons leveled at them.

Alistair didn’t hesitate. He dropped the cable and drew his revolver in one fluid motion. “Gregory, hold it steady! Professor, guide him!”

He fired, the gunshot shockingly loud in the enclosed space. The bullet sparked off the stone wall near the lead agent’s head, forcing them to duck back.

“The connection, Alistair! It must be made!” the Professor shrieked.

Another agent leaned out and fired his weapon. There was no bang, only a strange thrumming sound. A bolt of shimmering, distorted air shot past Alistair’s head and hit a pile of broken gears behind him. The gears did not shatter. They simply dissolved, fading into nothingness with a faint hiss.

Alistair fired again, aiming for the center of the doorway. One of the agents cried out and stumbled back.

“Gregory! Can you reach the cable?” Alistair yelled, ducking as another temporal bolt erased a chunk of the floor near his feet.

Gregory, still straining to hold the vibrating cradle, let go with one hand. He stretched, his fingers just brushing the heavy cable. He couldn’t quite grasp it.

“Now!” the Professor yelled, pointing at a sequence of dials on the converter. “The field is peaking! It will breach!”

Alistair knew they had one chance. He took a breath, aimed carefully, and fired his third shot not at the agents, but at the cracked great bell hanging above them.

The bullet struck the fissure with a sharp ping. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, with a deep, groaning protest of tortured metal, the crack widened. The ancient bell, weakened by Mor-Vane’s experiment and finished by Alistair’s bullet, tore free from its mounting. It did not fall straight down. It swung out like a monstrous pendulum, right towards the stairwell.

The Concordance agents stared in horror for a split second before scrambling to get out of the way. The bell smashed into the side of the doorway, shattering stone and blocking the entrance completely.

In that moment of chaos, Gregory finally grabbed the cable. “Where, Professor?” he roared over the din.

“The central port! Push it in!”

With a final, desperate lunge, Gregory shoved the plug into the socket.

The effect was instantaneous and absolute. A wave of pure, white light erupted from the crystal, utterly silent and blindingly bright. The hum ceased. The vibrations stopped. All sound was extinguished. Alistair felt a bizarre sensation, as if he were being pulled apart and reassembled in the same instant.

Then, as quickly as it began, it was over. The light vanished. The belfry was plunged back into a deeper darkness, the storm outside the only source of light and sound. The great crystal was now inert, a simple piece of quartz, its inner fire gone. But the leather wrapped box, the accumulator, glowed with a soft, contained violet light.

They had done it.

“We have to go,” Alistair said, his voice hoarse, his ears ringing. From behind the wall of the fallen bell, he could hear shouting and the sound of men trying to clear the rubble.

They grabbed the glowing accumulator and scrambled back to the broken archway. The escape down was even more frantic than the climb up. They made their way back across the rooftops, the precious box held tight in the Professor’s hands, and melted back into the city’s rain slicked shadows.

It felt like an eternity later when they finally stumbled back to the alley behind the Professor’s workshop. Exhausted, soaked, but triumphant.

The back of the building was as they had left it, a gaping hole in the wall. But the front was another story. The door was a wreck of splintered wood, hanging from a single hinge. The Concordance had been and, apparently, gone.

“They must think we are still trapped in the tower,” Gregory grunted.

“Let’s hope so,” Alistair said. He helped the Professor through the hole and climbed in after Gregory.

They hurried up the dark stairs, their hearts pounding with a mixture of dread and anticipation. They had the power source. Now they could save the man.

Alistair was the first into the workshop. He stopped dead. The room was ransacked. Books and papers were strewn everywhere. Glassware was shattered. The Concordance had torn the place apart looking for them.

His eyes shot to the long table in the center of the room.

It was empty.

The man was gone. There was no sign of a struggle, no trail of blood. Just an empty table in a ruined room. Gregory and the Professor came up behind him, their victorious expressions turning to ash.

“No,” the Professor whispered. “Did he… did he erase himself completely?”

“No,” Alistair said, his voice dangerously quiet. He walked slowly to the table, his eyes scanning its surface. The man was gone, but he had not vanished into nothing. Where his head had rested, there was a small, dark scorch mark on the wood, as if from a focused heat. And resting in the center of that mark, gleaming in the dim light, was a single, ornate silver cufflink, engraved with the initials M.V.

Mor-Vane’s cufflink.

Alistair stared at it, the terrible implications crashing down on him. The man on the table had not been a random victim caught in the temporal backlash. He was Mor-Vane himself, caught in his own trap, his body unravelling through time. And the Concordance had not just been hunting them. They had been hunting their own rogue agent.

They had the cure, but the Concordance now had the disease. And a man as brilliant and unstable as Mor-Vane, armed with the secrets of time itself, was now in the hands of the one organization ruthless enough to use him as a weapon.

Their night was not over. It had just begun.

Alistair reached out a hand that did not tremble and plucked the silver cufflink from the scorch mark. It was cool to the touch, heavy with an obscene finality. M.V. Mor-Vane. The man who had haunted his past, the architect of so much of his own personal misery, had been right under his nose, dissolving into temporal dust. The irony was a physical sickness that rose in his throat.

“They took him,” Gregory said, his voice a low rumble of disbelief. “They came in here, tore the place apart, and they just… they took him.”

“They have him,” the Professor corrected, his voice thin and reedy. He sank onto the edge of a splintered crate, his face pale in the gloom. The violet glow of the accumulator in his lap cast strange shadows on his features, making him look like a phantom. “They have him, and we have this.” He patted the strange metal box. “The cure for a man who is now in the custody of his own jailers. What a perfectly cruel joke.”

Alistair closed his fist around the cufflink, the sharp edges of the engraving digging into his palm. “It’s not a joke, Professor. It’s a declaration of war.”

“War?” Gregory scoffed, kicking at a pile of ruined books. “Alistair, look at us. We are three men in a wrecked workshop. They are the Concordance. They have soldiers, vehicles, the entire city grid under their thumb. We just blew up their bell tower. They think we are buried under the rubble, which is the only thing keeping us alive right now. This is not war. This is a rout.”

“He’s right, Alistair,” the Professor sighed, his shoulders slumping with the weight of their failure. “Our mission was to save the man on that table. We failed. He is beyond our help now. Our only sane option is to take the accumulator and disappear. Vanish. Live to fight another day.”

“There is no other day,” Alistair said, his voice dropping to a near whisper, yet it cut through the room with chilling clarity. He turned to face them, his eyes dark and intense. “Don’t you understand what just happened? The Concordance didn’t capture a prisoner. They recovered an asset. A broken one, yes. But they are the only people in the world with the resources to potentially stabilize him. To study him. To weaponize the very temporal decay that is tearing him apart.”

He opened his hand, showing them the cufflink again. “This man, Mor-Vane, he has seen the beginning and the end of things. He has secrets in his unravelling mind that could rewrite history, not just for a person, but for everyone. For everything. If the Concordance learns to control that, to aim it… they won’t just be policing the timeline. They will become gods. Cruel, stupid, bureaucratic gods.”

A heavy silence filled the workshop, broken only by the patter of rain against the boarded up windows. The implications of Alistair’s words settled over them like a shroud. They hadn't just lost a patient. They had potentially handed the ultimate weapon to their worst enemy.

Gregory ran a hand over his tired face. “So what’s the plan, then? We march up to the Concordance headquarters and ask for him back politely?”

“No,” Alistair said. “They won’t have taken him there. Not yet. He’s too volatile. Too valuable. They’ll have taken him to a secure, off-the-books location. A place to assess what they have before moving him to a primary research facility.” He looked directly at the Professor. “You worked with their precursors. You know their protocols, their mindset. Where would they take a high value, unstable temporal asset?”

The Professor looked down at the glowing accumulator, then up at Alistair’s unyielding gaze. For a long moment, he seemed to shrink, to retreat into his age and his fear. But then, a flicker of the old fire returned to his eyes. The man who had dared to build this workshop, to defy the Concordance in the first place, was still in there.

“There were… contingency sites,” he said slowly, dredging up memories he had long tried to bury. “Black sites. Places for things that couldn't be entered into the official record. There was one they favored for its isolation and its pre-existing infrastructure. It was an old hydraulic pumping station, from back when the industrial canals were the city’s lifeblood. Section Gamma Seven. They called it the Lockbox.”

“The Lockbox,” Gregory repeated, testing the name. “Sounds charming.”

“It’s on the west side of the river, deep in the derelict quarter,” the Professor continued, his voice gaining strength. “It’s a fortress of brick and iron. Self contained power, reinforced walls. Only one road in, with the canal protecting its other three sides. It would be the logical place.”

“Then that’s where we are going,” Alistair stated, a finality in his tone that allowed for no argument.

“Alistair, this is madness,” Gregory protested, though his voice lacked its earlier conviction. “We’re exhausted. We’re injured. We have no equipment, no plan.”

“We have the accumulator,” Alistair said, nodding toward the device in the Professor’s lap. “That’s our key. And we have the element of surprise. They think we’re dead. They’ll be focused on securing Mor-Vane, not on repelling an assault. This is our only chance. If we wait, they will move him, and we will lose him forever.”

He walked over to a battered cabinet in the corner, one of the few pieces of furniture still standing. He pulled it open, revealing a small, locked metal box. He retrieved a key from his pocket and opened it. Inside lay a heavy, oil-black revolver and a box of cartridges. It was a relic, but a well cared for one.

Gregory’s eyes widened slightly. “I didn’t know you had that.”

“Some things are best kept off the books,” Alistair said, echoing the Professor’s earlier words. He checked the cylinder, then began methodically loading the cartridges. The metallic clicks were the only sounds in the room. He tucked the revolver into the back of his waistband.

He then turned to the Professor. “Professor, we need to patch ourselves up. Anything left in your medical supplies?”

The Professor, galvanized by Alistair’s resolve, nodded. “Yes. Yes, of course.” He carefully set the accumulator on a clear patch of floor, its violet light pulsing like a strange, mechanical heart. He hurried to a small, hidden panel behind a bookcase and retrieved a surprisingly well stocked first aid kit.

For the next hour, they worked in a grim, focused silence. The Professor cleaned and bandaged the gash on Gregory’s arm and the various cuts and scrapes they had all accumulated. Gregory, in turn, found a length of sturdy canvas and fashioned a crude but effective satchel for the accumulator, designed to shield its telltale glow. Alistair, meanwhile, salvaged what he could from the wreckage. A set of heavy wire cutters, a coil of rope, two small but powerful magnetic clamps from a dismantled piece of equipment. It was a pathetic arsenal, but it was all they had.

As he worked, Alistair’s mind raced. Saving Mor-Vane. The thought was a bitter pill. He had every reason to want the man to suffer, to simply cease to exist. But his personal feelings were a luxury he could not afford. Mor-Vane was no longer just a man. He was a key, a locus of temporal energy that could not be allowed to fall into the Concordance’s hands. They didn’t need to save the man. They needed to secure the weapon. And if that meant stabilizing Mor-Vane with the accumulator, only to deal with him later… then so be it.

“The rain is letting up,” Gregory observed, peering through a crack in the boarded window. “But the streets will be crawling with patrols. They’ll be looking for any sign of us, just in case.”

“We’re not taking the streets,” Alistair said, shouldering the coil of rope. “Professor, the old service tunnels. Do they still run under the workshop?”

The Professor nodded. “The main sewer access is two blocks down, but there’s a storm drain spur that connects to our cellar. It will be foul, but it will take us most of the way to the river without seeing daylight.”

“Good.” Alistair looked at his two companions. The aging academic and the gruff mechanic. An unlikely trio to challenge a monolithic power. Yet, here they were. “Once we get him, we bring him back here. We use the accumulator, we stabilize him. Then… we figure out the rest. Agreed?”

Gregory gave a short, sharp nod. The Professor, his face set with a grim determination he hadn't possessed hours ago, did the same.

“Let’s go,” Alistair said.

Gregory hoisted the satchel containing the accumulator, its light now reduced to a faint, barely perceptible thrumming against the canvas. The Professor grabbed a small bag with the remaining medical supplies and a few specialized tools. Alistair led the way down the stairs, the weight of the revolver a cold, hard presence against his back.

The cellar was damp and smelled of earth and mildew. As the Professor had said, a heavy iron grate in the corner covered the entrance to the storm drain. Gregory put his considerable strength to it, and with a groan of rusted metal, he lifted it free. A wave of foul, cold air washed over them.

“Ladies first,” Gregory grunted, gesturing into the blackness.

One by one, they descended the iron rungs of the ladder into the tight, brick-lined tunnel. The darkness was absolute, a thick, cloying presence that swallowed the beam of the small hand-cranked torch Alistair produced. The only other light was the faint, rhythmic violet pulse from Gregory’s satchel, a ghostly beat in the oppressive dark.

The journey was a nightmare. The tunnel was slick with slime, and the water, cold and foul, rose to their ankles, then their knees. The air was thick with the stench of decay. They moved in single file, Alistair in the lead, his torch beam cutting a small, wavering circle in the infinite black. They spoke only in whispers, their own footsteps and the drip of water from the ceiling echoing unnaturally in the confined space.

Twice, they froze as they heard the heavy rumble of Concordance vehicles passing on the street above them. The sound was a terrifying reminder of the world they had left behind, and the power that was hunting them. In those moments, Alistair would extinguish the torch, and they would stand in perfect, heart-pounding blackness, feeling the vibrations through the soles of their boots, the faint purple glow of their prize the only thing visible in the entire world.

It felt like days had passed when the Professor finally whispered, “Here. This junction.” He pointed a trembling finger at a smaller, intersecting tunnel. “This will lead us toward the old canal sluice gates. It should surface about two hundred yards from the pumping station.”

They changed course, the new tunnel even narrower than the last. They had to stoop to avoid hitting their heads on the low ceiling. Finally, Alistair saw it: a faint, gray light ahead. It was a vertical maintenance shaft, ending in a heavy iron manhole cover.

Gregory braced himself beneath it and pushed. It didn't budge. “Sealed. Or rusted shut.”

“Let me see,” Alistair said. He passed the torch to the Professor and examined the edges of the cover. He could see the thick, corroded bolts. “Wire cutters.”

Gregory handed them over. For ten agonizingly long minutes, Alistair worked, the screech and snap of the metal cutters echoing far too loudly in the shaft. Every sound was a potential death sentence. Finally, with a last, loud crack, the final bolt gave way. Together, he and Gregory pushed. The heavy cover shifted, then tilted, allowing a sliver of gray, pre-dawn light and a blast of cold, clean air to pour into the shaft.

They pushed it aside and cautiously, Alistair poked his head out. They were in a weed-choked alley between two derelict warehouses. The air smelled of rain, river water, and rust. And in the distance, perhaps two hundred yards away as the Professor had predicted, stood the Lockbox.

It was a monstrous building of dark red brick, stained black with a century of soot and grime. It rose four stories high, its windows bricked over, its roof a complicated mess of vents and iron structures. A high, wrought iron fence topped with razor wire surrounded the compound. A single, reinforced gate provided the only visible entrance. They could see two armored Concordance transports parked inside the perimeter, and the unmistakable silhouettes of armed guards patrolling the fence line.

They slipped out of the manhole, replacing the cover as best they could, and huddled in the shadow of the warehouse wall.

“Fortress is right,” Gregory muttered, his eyes scanning the defenses. “They could hold off an army in there.”

“But they’re not expecting one,” Alistair countered, his gaze fixed on the building. “They’re expecting silence. They think we are a pile of ash and bone in a collapsed tower.” He looked at the pumping station, not as a soldier would, but as a thief. He noted the thick power conduits running up the western wall, the large outflow pipe that led directly to the canal, the cluster of ventilation fans on the roof.

“Professor,” he said quietly. “The outflow pipe. Is it grated?”

“Heavily,” the Professor replied without hesitation. “An iron cross-hatch. Quarter-inch steel. You couldn’t cut through it.”

“But the ventilation systems on the roof?”

“Less secure. They were only ever meant to keep out birds and weather. Maintenance hatches, access panels. Much more vulnerable.”

Alistair nodded, a plan beginning to form in his mind, desperate and thin as a spider’s thread. “The conduits on the west wall. They’ll get us to the roof. Gregory, I’ll need you to handle the guards at the fence’s weakest point, over by the canal where the ground is soft. Create a momentary diversion. Just long enough for the Professor and me to get over.”

Gregory stared at him. “A diversion? Alistair, there are four of them on patrol. What kind of diversion am I supposed to create? Should I juggle?”

“A blackout,” Alistair said, a grim smile touching his lips. He pointed to a junction box on a pole just outside the fence. “That box controls the perimeter lighting. If you can short it out, it will plunge the yard into darkness for a few crucial seconds. The guards will converge on the box. That’s our window.”

The plan was insane. It relied on split second timing, luck, and the Concordance guards reacting exactly as predicted. It would probably get them all killed.

Gregory looked from the junction box to the patrolling guards, then to the sheer brick wall of the pumping station. He looked at Alistair’s determined face and the Professor’s terrified but resolute expression. He let out a long, slow breath.

“A blackout it is, then,” he said, his voice a low growl of acceptance. “Just try not to fall off the roof before I get there.”

The sky was beginning to bleed from black to a bruised purple. Their night was almost over. Their true test was about to begin. Alistair gripped the cool metal of the magnetic clamps in his pocket, his gaze locked on the dark fortress that held Mor-Vane. They had the cure. Now they just had to break into hell to deliver it.
