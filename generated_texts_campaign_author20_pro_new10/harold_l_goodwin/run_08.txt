CHAPTER IV
The Sound in the Fog

Gable, the old marina watchman, had no trouble confirming their suspicion about the newcomer, Mr. Vance. He’d made a special trip to the town clerk’s office after his shift, reporting back that the so-called geologist was renting the old boatwright’s shed down by the wharf.

"No doubt he's our man," Leo said, pacing the worn floorboards of their makeshift clubhouse. Then, because they hadn't yet gotten the full story from the watchman, he pressed him for details about the strange thumping from the abandoned cannery.

"Heard it a dozen times, easy, not counting the night you boys came runnin'," Gable said, chewing on an unlit pipe. "Funny thing about the night the fog was thickest, though."

"What was funny?" Finn asked.

"Heard a splash."

"A splash isn't funny," Leo pointed out.

"Nope. But it wasn't coming from the cannery. It was out past the jetty. Heard the thumping, same as always, then a big ker-plunk, like someone dropped a safe in the channel. Then nothin'. The thumping just stopped, clean as a whistle."

This was a new piece of the puzzle. Leo and Finn exchanged a baffled look.

"You've seen Vance out there?" Finn asked.

"Sure have. Seen him plenty. Not that he sees me. I got no wish to interrupt a man admiring the scenery, not me. But I saw him. He’d stand on the dunes lookin' at the cannery, just lookin'."

"What was he doing?" Leo demanded.

"Nothin'. That's the thing. Just standin' there. Hands in his pockets. Like he was listenin' to a concert. Once saw two other fellows with him, carrying long poles. Bet they was ghost-huntin' with them fancy antennas."

"Did you see where they came from, or where they went?"

"Not me. I got my rounds to make, and the cannery ain't on 'em. Like I say, strange noises and me don't get along, none to speak of."

Leo mulled over the information. "Is this thumping always at ten o'clock?"

"No. Usually closer to midnight. Sometimes later."

Leo turned to Finn. "What does that sound like to you?"

"Nothing," Finn replied. "Not a thing. You say you saw two other men with poles?"

"That's right. Dressed in dark clothes. Most peculiar thing I ever saw was them wading into the surf, holdin' the poles up like they was divining for water."

Leo felt a chill that had nothing to do with the sea breeze. The calm, matter-of-fact way the watchman described these bizarre events was unnerving. "You mean you saw men wading into the ocean in the middle of the night?" the boy asked incredulously.

"Like I said. Right into the breakers, up to their waists. The water was churnin' white around 'em. Then they’d stop and hold the poles real still. Must be they was measurin' the tide. Don't know why else they'd be so keen on gettin' wet. And that Vance fella just watchin' from the dunes every time. Up and down the beach they went."

Finn asked, "Where were you when this was happening?"

"On the pier, feelin' foolish, but not so foolish I was gonna look away."

A man of quiet nerve, Leo thought. Didn't understand it, but had the sense to watch it unfold. "Mr. Gable, you said Vance was just watching. Are you sure he wasn't signaling them?"

"Couldn't say. He had his hands in his pockets. No flashlight, no noise. Just listenin' like the rest of us."

"Has anyone else seen these men with poles?" Finn wanted to know.

"Sure enough. Sal from the diner saw them once, thought they were fishermen with fancy new rods." The watchman paused, then looked straight at Leo. "Why are you boys so wound up about this geologist?"

Leo hesitated. "He just seems interested in the same things we are," he said carefully. "And he's new. It struck us as odd."

Gable's next comment made Leo's carefully constructed theory feel like a house of cards in a gale. "Oh, he's interested, all right. He asked me about the thumping himself, day he got here. Wondered if it was some old pump kicking on." The watchman added, "I was the one who told him it only happens at night."

Leo looked at Finn, his frustration plain. "Thank you, Mr. Gable," he said glumly. "You've given us a lot to chew on."

The boys watched the old watchman amble back toward the marina, a steady and reliable figure against the darkening sky. There was no questioning what he had seen, only what it all meant. Clearly, Leo thought, he had seen men on the beach. But what were they actually doing?

"Don't look so defeated," Finn offered. "Vance asking about the sound doesn't mean he's innocent. Maybe he was just establishing his cover, making sure everyone knew he was clueless."

"What do you suppose the splash was?" Leo asked.

"Probably just a bit of the old pier falling in. That place is a wreck. No reason to think it's connected."

"We'll stick to the plan," Leo resolved. "It's the only real move we have."

By the time the last of the evening fog began to roll in off the water, the boys were settled in their hiding spot, a hollow in the dunes that gave them a perfect, hidden view of the path to the cannery. They had armed themselves with a thermos of hot cocoa and a shared pair of binoculars, but the damp chill seeped into their jackets anyway. They lay in silence, watching the path, but it remained empty.

During a long stretch of quiet, Finn whispered, "You think we missed him? He could have come out before sunset."

"I doubt it. Where would he hide for three hours? It makes no sense. He'd come just before he needed to, to set up whatever it is he does."

The minutes ticked by. Nine thirty became ten o'clock, the time the thumping had started on the night they first investigated. The air remained still, broken only by the cry of a distant gull. At ten fifteen, Leo finally sighed in frustration.

"He's either not coming, or he's already inside. Let's go check the cannery itself."

As they crept along the sandy track, keeping to the shadows, Leo explained his new idea to Finn. "The sound can't be coming from inside the building. It's too regular, too deep. It's got to be projected somehow. Like a directional speaker, maybe using low frequencies you feel more than hear."

"A thump projector," Finn agreed. "But where would they set it up?"

That, Leo admitted, was the million-dollar question. "All we can do is get close and listen. Try to pinpoint the source."

"Both ears," Finn corrected.

It was ten twenty when they reached the rusted fence surrounding the cannery. The building loomed before them, a dark shape against the slightly less dark sky. The air was heavy with the smell of salt and decay. By silent consent they moved toward a breach in the fence near the loading docks. If the sound started, they would be right on top of it, in a prime position to see or hear any activity.

"Ready?" Finn whispered. "If it starts, we find the loudest wall and stick to it."

Leo nodded, his heart hammering against his ribs. Despite his belief that this was all a trick caused by clever men and their machines, he didn't much like the idea of being so close to the source. The deep, vibrating boom had felt wrong the first time, unsettling in a way he couldn't explain. But, he told himself, Finn was right. They had to follow their logic to the end.

"Wait until we're sure it's steady," Leo whispered back.

A voice from the darkness behind them made them both jump. "You boys better clear out. This ain't no place to be after dark."

They spun around to see a local fisherman heading home from the docks, and in that split second a low hum vibrated through the soles of their shoes. A gasp came from the fisherman.

The fisherman’s face, already weathered and creased by a lifetime at sea, seemed to collapse in on itself. His eyes were wide, fixed on the dark cannery beyond the boys. "It's starting," he breathed, the words catching in his throat. "The Beat." The hum grew louder, a deep resonant frequency that traveled up their legs and settled in their chests. It was a physical presence, making the air feel thick and heavy.

"What beat?" Leo asked, his voice a little shaky. He tried to keep his tone clinical, analytical, but the pure animal fear in the fisherman's eyes was contagious.

"You boys don't know the stories?" the man said, taking a stumbling step backward. "You ain't from here, are you?" He didn't wait for an answer. "They say it's the heart of the cannery. Old Man Hemlock's heart, the one they never found after the accident. Beating in the dark. Calling for his crew."

Finn looked at Leo, his own eyes wide. "A heart?"

Before Leo could scoff, the first thump hit them.

It was not a sound. It was a punch. A wave of pressure expanded from the cannery, so powerful it made the chain link fence shudder. Leo felt the air forced from his lungs in a sharp grunt. The hum vanished, replaced by a profound, vibrating silence. Then the hum returned, building for a few seconds.

THUMP.

Again. A physical blow that seemed to bend the air itself. The fisherman cried out, a thin, terrified sound, and turned to run. "Get away from here!" he shouted over his shoulder, his heavy boots kicking up sand as he scrambled back toward the distant lights of the town. "It'll swallow you whole!"

Leo and Finn were frozen, not by fear, but by a strange combination of terror and awe. The rhythm was exactly as they remembered it. A low, rising hum, then the percussive blast of pressure, then a few seconds of echoing silence. Hum. THUMP. Hum. THUMP.

"His heart hammered against his ribs" had been a figure of speech just minutes ago. Now Leo felt it literally. With every beat from the cannery, his own heart seemed to slam against his sternum in a panicked, sympathetic rhythm.

"Leo," Finn whispered, his voice trembling. "What is that?"

Leo forced himself to think, to push past the primal fear the fisherman had so easily surrendered to. A heart. A ghost. It was nonsense. But this… this was more than a speaker. "It's a low frequency acoustic device," he said, the words sounding absurdly academic in the vibrating darkness. "A powerful one. More powerful than I thought." He swallowed, his throat dry. "It works by creating a pressure wave. That's what we're feeling. Not sound, but displaced air."

"It feels like it's coming from inside my own head," Finn said, pressing his hands to his temples.

"That's the point. It's disorienting. It's meant to scare people away." Leo's mind was racing. "My theory was right. This is a scarecrow. A very, very effective one." He took a deep breath as the hum began to build again, bracing himself.

THUMP.

The impact rattled his teeth. "Okay," he said, once the wave passed. "The plan is the same. We find the loudest wall."

"Loudest wall?" Finn croaked. "It's everywhere. It feels like the ground is doing it."

Finn was right. It was impossible to tell the direction. The wave seemed to originate from the very center of their being and expand outward. It was a perfect, omnidirectional assault. Leo’s confidence in his simple plan began to fray. "Okay," he said, thinking fast. "New plan. It can't be omnidirectional. That's not how physics works. There has to be a source. One source, or… or multiple sources." The thought clicked into place. "That's it. It's not one big projector. It's a network. A system of them, all timed to go off at once. To create a standing wave, or a zone of constructive interference right here."

"A what?"

"They're making it feel like it's everywhere by putting it in several places," Leo simplified, already moving. "Come on. We have to get inside the fence."

They squeezed through the familiar breach, the rusted metal cold and rough under their hands. Inside the cannery grounds, the effect was even more intense. The decaying wooden walls of the loading dock groaned with each pulse. Loose sheets of corrugated metal vibrated with a high, buzzing shriek that rode on the edge of the silence after each thump.

Hum. THUMP. Zzzzzzz. Silence.

Hum. THUMP. Zzzzzzz. Silence.

It was a monstrous, mechanical heartbeat. They crept along the base of the main building, their hands flat against the splintery wood. Here, the thumping was less a punch and more a deep, resonant shudder that traveled up their arms.

"It's weaker here," Leo noted, his voice tight. "The main force of the wave is being projected outward, toward the town. To keep people away."

"So where do we go?" Finn asked, huddling close.

"We need to get higher. If it's a network, the control unit and the cables have to be somewhere." Leo scanned the looming structure. His eyes fell on the external metal staircase that led up to the second floor offices and then continued to the flat roof of the main processing building. "Up there. On the roof. It's the perfect place to mount this kind of equipment. Clear line of sight."

"Climb that thing? Now?" Finn’s voice was barely a whisper. The staircase was a skeleton of rust, and with each THUMP, it rang like a tuning fork.

"It's the only way," Leo insisted, already moving toward it. He was operating on pure adrenaline now, his scientific curiosity overriding his fear. This was the greatest puzzle he had ever faced, and he was not going to be scared away from the solution.

He placed a foot on the first step. The metal was slick with sea spray and grit. "Stay close," he said, and began to climb.

The ascent was terrifying. With every step, the metal groaned in protest. With every thump from the unseen machine, the entire structure shook violently, threatening to tear itself away from the wall. Leo kept his eyes fixed on the step above him, not daring to look down. Finn followed so closely behind that his panicked breaths were hot on Leo's neck. They passed the second floor landing, a dark doorway that gaped like a mouth, and kept going up, toward the sky.

The thumping grew stronger the higher they climbed, the pressure more direct. It felt less like a wave and more like a solid, repeating impact against his back. They were getting closer.

Finally, Leo's hand found the edge of the roof. He hauled himself over the top, landing with a soft thud on the tarred surface. He scrambled back and helped Finn up. They lay flat on their stomachs for a moment, catching their breath, the tar paper rough against their cheeks. The rhythmic assault was deafening here, a physical torment that made it hard to think.

Leo lifted his head and looked around. The roof was a flat expanse of black, littered with debris and old ventilation shafts. And something else. Near the center of the roof, he saw it. A cluster of strange machines. There were four of them, arranged in a wide square. They were not speakers, not in any conventional sense. Each device consisted of a large, thick metal drum, about the size of an oil barrel, lying on its side. A complex web of thick black cables snaked out from the back of each one, converging at a central metal box covered in blinking lights. Just before the next thump, Leo saw the flat, circular face of each drum glow with a faint purple light, and then, with the THUMP, a visible distortion rippled the air in front of them.

"Thump projectors," Finn breathed, staring in wonder.

"More like pressure cannons," Leo whispered back. "Look at them. They're custom built."

He was right. They were crudely powerful, a brute force application of some advanced principle. The welding was thick and uneven, the power cables industrial grade, clamped directly to the metal drums. This was not something you could buy in a store. This was the work of a very clever, very determined, and probably very dangerous person.

The cables from the four cannons all led to the central control box. But there was another, much thicker cable, a bundle of them wrapped in heavy black insulation, that led from the box toward the back of the roof, where it disappeared down a wide ventilation shaft.

"What's that for?" Finn pointed.

"Power," Leo guessed. "And maybe more. The sound might be generated down there." He began to crawl toward the ventilation shaft, the thumping so intense now that his vision was blurring with each impact. He had to know. He had to see what was at the other end of that cable.

As they neared the edge of the shaft, a new sound reached them, a sound that was almost completely masked by the rhythmic pounding. It was the whine of machinery. And voices.

Leo froze, holding up a hand to stop Finn. He strained his ears, trying to parse the words in the brief, silent intervals between thumps.

"...winch is holding steady." A man's voice, rough and low.

Hum. THUMP.

"...another hour at this rate. The tide's on our side." A second voice, calmer, more in control.

Hum. THUMP.

"...corrosion on the casing is worse than we thought. Hope the contents are stable."

Hum. THUMP.

"...won't know until we get it open. Just get it up. The longer we're here, the more chance some stupid kid or nosy fisherman..."

The voice trailed off as Leo and Finn exchanged a horrified look. They weren't just scaring people away for fun. They were hiding something. They were using the cannery as a base of operations for something big, something they were pulling up from the water. The winch. The casing.

Leo peered cautiously over the edge of the ventilation shaft. It was a straight drop of thirty feet into the vast, cavernous space of the main processing floor. The floor was gone in the center of the room, leaving a gaping, square hole filled with dark, churning water: an indoor tide pool, connected directly to the sea through a submerged channel. Positioned over this hole was a massive, jury-rigged winch, its motor whining. A thick steel cable descended from the winch into the black water.

And standing around it were three men. They were dressed in dark work clothes, their faces illuminated in the stark, swinging light of a single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling. One man was at the controls of the winch. The other two were watching the cable, their expressions tense.

The puzzle pieces slammed into place in Leo's mind with the force of one of the cannery's beats. The thumping wasn't just a scarecrow. It was a cover. Its powerful vibrations and noise would mask the sound of their winch motor and their activities from anyone on the outside. It was a brilliant, terrifyingly effective plan.

"We need to go," Finn hissed, tugging on Leo's jacket. "Now."

Leo knew he was right. They had seen too much. These were not pranksters. These were serious men engaged in a serious, clandestine operation. He began to back away from the shaft, moving as quietly as he could.

But he was not quiet enough.

His shoe scuffed against a loose piece of gravel on the tar paper. The tiny sound was nothing compared to the rhythmic pounding, but in the heightened tension of the moment, it was as loud as a gunshot.

Down below, one of the men looked up. His eyes narrowed, sweeping the dark upper reaches of the processing hall. He couldn't see them, not from that angle, but he had heard something. He tapped the shoulder of the man next to him and pointed upward.

Leo's blood ran cold. He and Finn scrambled backward, crab walking away from the shaft.

"Up there!" the voice from below shouted, his words echoing in the vast space. "Check the roof!"

They heard the clang of a foot on metal. One of the men was coming up the outside staircase.

"Run," Leo choked out, and they scrambled to their feet.

The cannery roof, which had seemed so empty moments before, now felt like a naked, exposed stage. The only way down was the staircase they had come up, and one of the men was on his way to meet them.

"This way!" Finn yelled, pointing toward the far side of the building, the side that faced the ocean cliffs. "There's an old fire escape!"

They sprinted across the roof, their feet pounding on the tar. The thumping was relentless, a monstrous soundtrack to their flight. They reached the fire escape, a rickety latticework of iron even more rusted than the main staircase.

"Go!" Leo urged, pushing Finn toward it.

Finn swung a leg over the parapet and got his feet onto the platform. It swayed precariously, groaning in protest. He started down, moving as fast as he dared.

Leo was about to follow when a large figure appeared at the head of the main staircase. It was one of the men, silhouetted against the faint glow of the town. He was big, and he was moving fast.

"Hey!" the man yelled. "You kids! Stop right there!"

Leo didn't hesitate. He threw himself over the edge and onto the fire escape, his hands grabbing the cold, slick railing. The whole structure shuddered. Below him, Finn was already halfway down.

"It's not going to hold both of us!" Finn shouted up, his voice thin with panic.

A powerful jolt shook the fire escape. The man was on it. Leo looked up and saw him descending, his heavy boots making the metal scream. He was taking the steps two at a time.

They were trapped. The man was above them. The ground was still a terrifying twenty feet below. And to their left was nothing but a sheer drop to the rocks and churning sea.

"Jump!" Leo yelled to Finn. "It's the only way!" The last ten feet of the ladder had rusted away completely years ago, leaving a gap to the ground below. It was a risky leap, but better than being caught.

Finn looked down, then back at Leo. He nodded, and with a cry, he let go, dropping the last ten feet and landing in a crouch on the damp sand. He stumbled but stayed on his feet. "Leo, jump!"

Leo was about to follow, but the man was almost on him. He could hear his heavy breathing now, even over the thumping. He had one last, desperate idea. He looked at the bolts holding the fire escape to the brick wall. They were thick with rust, more red-brown powder than solid metal.

With the next THUMP, as the entire building vibrated, Leo threw all his weight against the rusted railing. He felt a grinding, groaning shift. The man above him cursed as the platform lurched.

Leo did it again, kicking out at the spot where the metal was bolted to the brick. The rust flaked away. A bolt shifted.

"What are you doing, you crazy kid?" the man snarled, reaching for him.

Leo ignored him. He waited for the hum to build, for the pressure to swell. Just before the next beat, he gave one final, desperate kick.

THUMP.

The percussive wave hit at the exact moment his foot did. The corroded metal, stressed to its absolute limit, gave way with a screech of tearing iron. The entire lower section of the fire escape ripped away from the wall.

For a heart-stopping second, Leo and the man were falling together, connected by a collapsing cage of metal. The man yelled in surprise and rage. Leo pushed off the wall, away from the falling debris, and twisted his body. He hit the sand hard, the impact knocking the wind out of him, but he was clear.

The heavy iron ladder and the man crashed onto the beach a few feet away with a deafening clang of metal on rock. The man cried out in pain, his leg trapped and twisted beneath the wreckage.

"Leo!" Finn was pulling at his arm. "Come on!"

Leo scrambled to his feet, his side screaming in protest. He glanced back. The man was trying to pull himself free, his face a mask of fury. From the front of the building, they could hear shouting. The other two men were coming.

They ran. They fled into the darkness of the dunes, not daring to look back. The rhythmic thumping of the cannery pursued them, each beat a reminder of the real, human danger they had uncovered. It was no ghost. It was something much, much worse.

Their feet pounded on the soft, shifting sand of the dunes, the frantic rhythm a counterpoint to the steady, deep THUMP that still seemed to echo in their bones. Leo’s side was a hot knot of pain, each breath a sharp intake of fire. Finn, a pace ahead, kept glancing back, his face pale and strained in the thin moonlight. They didn't stop running until the cannery was just a dark block against the star-dusted sky and the sounds of their pursuers had been swallowed completely by the roar of the surf and the whisper of the wind through the dune grass.

They collapsed behind a high, steep dune, the kind they had sledded down on flattened cardboard boxes in winters past. The memory felt like it belonged to another boy, in another lifetime. Leo rolled onto his back, gasping for air, the sand cold and damp beneath his thin jacket. The pain in his side pulsed in time with the phantom thumping in his head.

"Are they… are they gone?" Finn whispered, his voice hoarse. He was peering over the crest of the dune, a nervous silhouette against the night.

"I think so," Leo breathed, closing his eyes. "For now."

They lay in silence for a long time, listening. The world was reduced to the crash of waves, the lonely cry of a distant gull, and their own ragged breathing. The immediate, heart-pounding terror began to recede, replaced by a cold, heavy dread.

"What was that, Leo?" Finn finally asked, sliding down the dune to sit beside him. "What was all that?"

"I don't know," Leo admitted, though it was only a half truth. He knew it was something big. Something worth injuring a man, maybe even killing two boys, to protect. "They weren't canning fish."

"No kidding," Finn said, his voice trembling slightly. "Did you see their faces? They weren't just angry we were trespassing. They were… vicious."

Leo pushed himself up to a sitting position, wincing as the muscles in his side protested. He pulled up the edge of his shirt. A dark, angry bruise was already blooming across his ribs, a souvenir from his landing on the beach. "That guy. The one on the ladder. He was going to throw me off."

The reality of it hit them both. This wasn't a game. They had stumbled into a hornet's nest, and they had hit it with a stick.

"We have to go home," Finn said, his voice small. "We have to tell my dad."

Leo shook his head. "And say what? That we were breaking into the old cannery, got chased by scary men, and I wrecked the fire escape? They'll ground us for a year. Or worse, they won't believe the part about the men. They'll think we made it up to cover for the vandalism."

"So what do we do?" Finn's voice cracked. "We just… forget about it? Pretend it never happened?"

The thumping. The rhythmic, industrial beat. It was the key. "That sound," Leo said, looking back toward the dark shape of the cannery. "It wasn't a ghost. It was a machine. A big one." He remembered the glimpse he'd gotten through a grimy window before the chase began. Just a flash. A brightly lit room, people moving with purpose, and something that was definitely not a fish packing line. "I saw something inside. Through a window. It was like a workshop. Bright lights. And… paper. Stacks of it."

"Paper?"

"Yeah. Big sheets. And I smelled something. Not fish. Chemicals. Like paint or… ink."

They looked at each other. The pieces were starting to form a picture, one that was blurry and terrifying. A secret workshop. Men willing to do violence to protect it. A giant, thumping machine. Ink.

"We can't go home," Leo said, his mind racing past the fear and into the cold, clear space of a problem that needed solving. "And we can't go to the police. Not yet. They'll ask questions we can't answer, and we don't have any proof. It's just our word against theirs."

"So we hide," Finn said, relieved. "Good. Where do we go?"

"No," Leo said, and Finn's face fell. "We don't hide. We get proof."

"Are you insane?" Finn scrambled to his feet, gesturing wildly at the cannery. "They just tried to kill us! One of them has his leg crushed under a fire escape because of you! They're not going to give us a second chance!"

"Which is why we have to be smarter," Leo insisted, his voice low and intense. He got to his feet, ignoring the throb in his side. "Finn, think about it. Whatever they're doing in there, it's a huge secret. People who keep secrets like that, they're afraid of one thing more than anything else. Light. We just have to shine a little light on it."

"They'll squash us like bugs, Leo!"

"Not if they don't see us coming." Leo's eyes were fixed on the cannery, a plan already forming. "We know this town. We know every inch of this coastline. They don't. They're strangers. That's our advantage."

He led Finn away from the dunes, not towards the town and its warm, safe houses, but further down the coast, towards the jagged cliffs that bordered the north side of the bay. Tucked away at the base of the cliffs, hidden by a curtain of sea-weathered boulders, was the entrance to a small cave. They called it The Pirate's Lair when they were eight. It was damp and smelled of salt and seaweed, but it was dry and, most importantly, invisible from the beach.

Inside, they huddled together for warmth, the adrenaline finally giving way to a bone deep exhaustion. Finn was quiet, withdrawn. Leo knew he had pushed his friend too far, but he couldn't see another way. To run and hide felt like a different kind of death. It felt like letting the darkness win.

"My dad used to work at the cannery," Leo said softly into the darkness of the cave. "Before it closed down. He used to tell me about it. He said there was a whole network of tunnels under it. Drains. For the fish guts and the water."

Finn didn't respond. He had his knees pulled up to his chest.

"One of the main ones lets out near here," Leo continued, trying to draw him in. "A big concrete pipe, right into the sea. It's only above water at low tide. The tide's going out now. We could…"

"No," Finn said, his voice flat. "No, Leo. I'm not going back there. You can't ask me to."

"We just have to look, Finn. One look. So we can tell the police exactly what's happening. So they have to believe us."

"And what if they catch us this time? There won't be a fire escape to jump from. We'll be trapped in a sewer pipe."

The logic was sound. It was a crazy, stupid plan. But the alternative, doing nothing, felt even crazier. Leo thought of the man's face on the ladder, the pure, predatory rage in his eyes. People like that didn't belong in their town. They were a poison.

"Okay," Leo said, his voice softer. "Okay. We'll wait here until morning. We'll figure it out then."

He didn't sleep. He sat at the mouth of the cave, watching the tide slowly retreat, exposing a slick, dark expanse of sand and rock. He watched the moon trace its path across the sky. And he watched the cannery. From this angle, he could see a few high windows, all dark except one. A single window, high up on the third floor, glowed with a faint, pulsing light. It beat in time with the thumping he could still feel in his chest. THUMP… glow. THUMP… glow. A monstrous, mechanical heartbeat in the center of the town's decay.

When the first hints of grey lightened the eastern sky, Finn stirred. He looked out at the sea, then at Leo, then at the cannery. His face was set with a weary resignation.

"The drainpipe?" he asked.

Leo nodded.

They found it an hour later, a massive concrete circle half hidden by a rockfall. A rusty iron grate covered the opening, but the hinges had long since corroded. With a shared effort that strained Leo's bruised ribs to the breaking point, they managed to pry it open just enough to squeeze through.

The air inside was foul, a thick, cloying mixture of ancient fish rot, brine, and mildew. It was pitch black. Leo pulled a small keychain flashlight from his pocket, a prize from a cereal box years ago. Its beam was weak, but it was enough. The pipe was wide enough to walk in, hunched over. Their footsteps echoed, unnaturally loud in the confined space. Water dripped from the ceiling, and slick, slimy moss coated the curved walls.

"This is the stupidest thing we've ever done," Finn whispered, his voice bouncing off the concrete.

"Probably," Leo whispered back.

They walked for what felt like an eternity, the pipe sloping gradually upward. Soon, the sounds of the sea faded, replaced by a low, resonant humming. It grew louder and louder, vibrating through the soles of their sneakers, through the very concrete they walked on. And then, beneath the hum, they heard it. THUMP. It was right above them now. A heavy, percussive impact that made the air shudder. THUMP.

Leo spotted a small, square shape in the ceiling of the pipe. An access grate. A ladder of rusted iron rungs was set into the wall beside it. This was it. He looked at Finn, who just shook his head, his eyes wide with fear. Leo squeezed his shoulder, then began to climb.

The rungs were slick and cold. The grate was heavy, but it wasn't locked. He pushed it up an inch. The humming and thumping were deafening. He could feel the vibrations in his teeth. He smelled the ink again, sharp and chemical, and something else. Hot metal and oil. He lifted the grate another inch and peered through the crack.

He was looking up into the heart of the machine.

It was a vast, open room, the entire top floor of the cannery. All the old fish processing equipment had been torn out. In the center of the room, dominating everything, was the source of the noise. It wasn't a single machine. It was a press. A printing press, but unlike any he had ever seen. It was a colossal, custom built monster of steel and gears, easily twenty feet high. A massive flywheel spun with a hypnotic hum, and at its heart, a huge metal plate slammed down every few seconds onto a steel bed. THUMP.

Men moved around the machine with a practiced, factory like efficiency. They fed huge sheets of pale green paper into one end. Other men collected the sheets that came out the other, now covered in intricate green and black designs. They stacked them on long tables where more people were working under bright, unforgiving fluorescent lights. They were cutting the sheets, stacking the results. Stacks of money. Not just stacks. Bricks. Pallets of it.

Leo felt his breath catch in his throat. He was looking at millions, maybe hundreds of millions of dollars. He saw the man who had been on the fire escape. His leg was heavily bandaged and he was leaning on a crutch, directing two other men as they loaded bricks of cash into wooden crates. The same kind of crates used for shipping fish.

He let the grate fall back into place with a soft clink, his hands shaking. He scrambled back down the ladder, his mind reeling.

"Leo? What is it? What did you see?" Finn hissed.

"Money," Leo breathed, his back against the slimy wall. "They're printing money. So much money."

The scale of it was impossible. It wasn't just a few crooks. This was an operation. An industry.

"We have to go," Finn urged, pulling on his arm. "Now. We've seen it. Let's go."

But Leo's eyes were drawn back to the ladder. He had to see more. He had to understand. "Wait," he whispered. "Just one more look. I need to see who's in charge."

Against his better judgment, against Finn's frantic, silent pleas, he climbed the ladder again. He was more careful this time, lifting the grate just a fraction of an inch. He scanned the room, past the grunting workers and the thumping press. He saw the man with the limp barking an order. And then he saw who the man was talking to.

A man in a clean, well pressed suit was inspecting a sheet of the freshly printed bills with a jeweler's loupe. He nodded, said something Leo couldn't hear, and then he turned. The bright overhead lights fell on his face.

It was Mr. Abernathy.

Mr. Abernathy from the town council. Mr. Abernathy who gave speeches on Founder's Day about civic pride. Mr. Abernathy who had presented Leo with his third place ribbon in the science fair last year. His kindly, public face was gone. In its place was a cold, calculating expression Leo had never seen before. A manager's expression.

The world seemed to tilt. This wasn't just a crime. It was a betrayal. The poison was not just in the cannery. It was in the town itself.

Abernathy looked up, his eyes sweeping the ceiling. For a heart stopping second, Leo thought their eyes met. He felt a jolt of pure panic, a certainty that he had been seen. He dropped the grate. It landed with a loud CLANG that echoed in the drainpipe like a gunshot.

"He saw me!" Leo gasped, sliding down the ladder so fast he almost fell.

Below them, in the main room, the thumping stopped. The sudden silence was more terrifying than the noise had been. They could hear shouted voices. The sound of heavy boots on a concrete floor.

"What did you do?" Finn cried, his voice an octave of pure terror.

"Run," Leo said, grabbing his friend's arm and pulling him down the pipe. "Just run."

They plunged back into the darkness of the pipe, their footsteps slapping in the shallow, foul water. They didn't need the flashlight now. The way was straight. Behind them, they heard a metallic scraping sound. A shaft of brilliant light pierced the darkness. The men were coming. They were in the pipe with them.

They ran with burning lungs and pounding hearts, the echoes of their own flight mixing with the shouts of their pursuers. The small circle of grey light that was the entrance to the pipe seemed a thousand miles away. They were running for their lives, no longer boys exploring a haunted cannery, but witnesses who knew a secret that was worth more than money. A secret that was worth killing for. The thumping of the press was gone, but a new, more frantic rhythm had taken its place: the sound of their own feet, fleeing the darkness that had taken root in the heart of their town.

The circle of light ahead was their only hope, a distant, mocking moon in the suffocating blackness. The foul water, ankle deep and cold, splashed up their shins, soaking the cuffs of their jeans and chilling them to the bone. Leo’s heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drumbeat that seemed louder than their own frantic footsteps. Behind them, the shouts grew clearer, bouncing off the curved concrete walls, distorted and monstrous. The beam from their pursuers’ flashlight cut a sharp, wobbling cone through the darkness, dancing ahead of them, then falling behind as the man carrying it stumbled.

“I can’t,” Finn wheezed, his voice a ragged sob. “Leo, I can’t breathe.”

He staggered, his hand slipping from Leo’s grasp. Leo whipped around, grabbing Finn’s shirt and yanking him forward. Finn’s face, barely visible, was a pale mask of terror. His eyes were wide, unseeing.

“Yes, you can,” Leo commanded, his own voice raw and desperate. “You have to. They will not just catch us, Finn. They will do what they did to Mr. Henderson.”

The thought of the old watchman, of the rumors they had dismissed as town gossip, was a fresh jolt of adrenaline. They were not just trespassing. They were witnesses. Mr. Abernathy’s face, cold and hard under the factory lights, swam in his vision. The kindly man from the town council. It was a lie. Everything was a lie.

“My fault,” Leo gasped, pulling Finn along. “It’s my fault. So I have to get you out.”

The shouts behind them were closer now. They could hear the heavy, rhythmic splashing of two, maybe three sets of boots. They were bigger men, their strides longer. They were gaining. The light at the end of the pipe seemed no closer, an impossible goal.

Suddenly, Leo saw it. To their left, a dark, gaping hole. A smaller pipe, an intersecting tributary no more than four feet in diameter, branching off from the main artery. It was a choice. A terrible, instant choice. The main pipe was a straight shot to the creek, to a known exit. But their pursuers knew that too. This smaller pipe was a gamble. It could be a dead end. It could be a trap.

“This way,” Leo hissed, making the decision in a split second. He veered left, dragging Finn with him into the even more profound darkness of the side tunnel.

“Leo, no,” Finn cried, trying to pull back. “The light is that way.”

“They’ll be waiting for us,” Leo grunted, forcing his way into the narrower passage. The air here was thicker, the smell of rot and stagnant water more intense. They had to crouch now, their backs scraping against the slimy, curved ceiling. “This is our only chance.”

They plunged into the side pipe. Behind them, they heard the men’s footsteps thunder past the junction. For a moment, a glorious, heart stopping moment, it seemed to have worked. Then the footsteps faltered. A confused shout echoed back to them. The beam of light reappeared, sweeping back and forth, probing the darkness. It found the opening of their tunnel.

“In here,” a gruff voice yelled.

The chase was on again, but the dynamic had changed. In the tighter space, they were forced to a hunched, scrambling run. The water was deeper, sloshing around their knees. Twice, Finn slipped and went down, his hands plunging into the unseen filth on the pipe’s floor. Twice, Leo hauled him back to his feet, his own muscles screaming in protest. They could hear the men behind them, cursing as they squeezed into the smaller pipe. The men were bigger, less agile. The tight space that hindered the boys was a greater obstacle for them. It was their only advantage.

The pipe began to slope upwards. Their desperate scramble became a grueling climb against a small but steady current of brackish water. Leo’s sneakers, slick with grime, found little purchase. He used his hands, his fingers clawing at the rough concrete, his knuckles scraping raw. He could hear Finn’s ragged, weeping breaths just behind him.

“Almost there,” Leo lied, though he had no idea where they were going. He just knew they had to keep moving up, away from the water, away from the men.

Then his outstretched hand hit something hard and metallic. Iron. He looked up. A faint lattice of grey light filtered down from above. A ladder. Another ladder, leading to another grate. Hope, fierce and sharp, cut through his panic.

“Up,” he ordered, his voice barely a whisper. “Finn, climb.”

He pushed himself onto the first rung, his waterlogged clothes weighing him down. The iron was cold and slick with condensation. He climbed, rung by painful rung, his body shaking with exhaustion. He could hear Finn following, his sobs quieter now, replaced by a grim, focused effort. Below, the flashlight beam bobbed erratically. The men were still coming, but they were slower on the incline.

Leo reached the top, his head pressing against the cold iron of the grate. He pushed. It didn't budge. He pushed again, putting his shoulders into it, grunting with the effort. It was heavier than the last one. Panic seized him again. A dead end. They were trapped.

“Help me,” he whispered down to Finn.

Finn climbed up beside him, his small frame squeezed onto the narrow ladder. Together, they put their shoulders to the grate. They pushed in a desperate, unified heave. For a second, nothing. Then, with a groan of rusted metal, the grate shifted. A sliver of night air, clean and cold and smelling of pine needles, washed over them. It was the most beautiful thing Leo had ever smelled.

They pushed again, harder, and the grate lifted a few inches. It was enough. Leo hooked his scraped fingers over the edge of the opening and hauled himself up and out, rolling onto damp, cool grass. He gasped in the night air, his lungs burning. A moment later, Finn squirmed through the gap, collapsing beside him in a shivering heap.

They lay there for what felt like an eternity, listening. The world was quiet. There was only the sound of crickets and the whisper of wind in the trees. They were in a small clearing, surrounded by a thick stand of pines. They had emerged at the edge of town, where the manicured lawns gave way to the untamed woods.

Below them, from the open drain, they heard a muffled curse. A metallic scrape as one of the men reached the ladder.

“Don’t make a sound,” Leo breathed.

He reached over and slowly, carefully, pulled the heavy grate back into place. It settled with a soft thud. They were out. They were free. But they were not safe.

They crawled away from the grate on their hands and knees, their bodies trembling, until they were hidden in the deeper shadows of the pine trees. Only then did they dare to sit up. They were caked in mud and filth, shivering uncontrollably in their wet clothes.

Finn turned to Leo, his face a mess of tears and grime in the dim moonlight. The terror in his eyes had been replaced by a cold, hard anger.

“This is your fault,” he said, his voice shaking with fury. “All of it. ‘Just one more look,’ you said. You and your stupid curiosity. They saw you, Leo. Abernathy saw you. He knows your face.”

Leo flinched as if struck. The words hit him harder than any physical blow. Because they were true. It was his fault. His need to know, his refusal to listen to Finn’s caution, had led them here.

“I know,” Leo said, his own voice hollow. He couldn’t meet Finn’s eyes. He looked down at his trembling, filthy hands. “I know, Finn. I’m sorry.”

“Sorry?” Finn’s voice rose, cracking. “Sorry doesn’t help us. They’re going to be looking for us. What are we going to do? We can’t go home. My mom… my dad… they’ll be worried sick.”

The thought of his own parents sent a fresh wave of sickness through Leo. His mother, waiting up with a glass of milk. His father, pretending to read the paper but really just waiting to hear the front door open. He had betrayed their trust just as surely as Abernathy had betrayed the town’s.

“We can’t go home,” Leo repeated, the words tasting like ash. “That’s the first place they’ll look. Abernathy… he knows me from the science fair. He knows my name. He knows where I live. If we go home, we put our families in danger.”

The reality of their situation settled over them, colder and heavier than the wet clothes clinging to their skin. They were two thirteen year old boys, hunted by powerful men in their own hometown. The police were not an option. Abernathy was on the town council. Who knew how many officers were in his pocket? They had no one to turn to. They were utterly, terrifyingly alone.

They sat in silence, the woods growing darker and more menacing around them. Every snap of a twig, every rustle of leaves, sent a jolt of fear through them. They imagined Abernathy’s men emerging from every drain in town, fanning out, their flashlights cutting through the night.

“So what do we do?” Finn finally asked, his anger spent, leaving only a small, tired voice behind. “We’ll freeze to death out here.”

Leo’s mind raced, sifting through desperate, impossible options. They needed a place to hide. A place to get warm. A place where no one would think to look for them. And they needed an adult. Someone they could trust. But who? Every familiar, friendly face in town was now a potential mask, hiding a secret as dark as Abernathy’s.

Then, an idea surfaced. A strange, risky idea born of local legend and childish whispers. Old Man Hemlock.

He lived in a cabin deep in these same woods. The town considered him a crazy recluse. Kids told ghost stories about him. They said he was a veteran who had come back from some war changed, that he hated people, that he’d shoot trespassers on sight. But Leo had seen him a few times, from a distance, while hiking with his dad. He’d seen a tall, lean man with a weathered face and watchful eyes, gathering herbs or just standing and looking at the trees. He never came to town events. He wasn’t part of the Founder’s Day parades or the bake sales. He was separate. An outsider.

“I know a place,” Leo said slowly. “Maybe. It’s a long shot.”

“What place?” Finn asked, suspicious.

“Old Man Hemlock’s cabin.”

Finn stared at him as if he’d lost his mind. “Hemlock? The crazy hermit? Leo, he’ll murder us. He probably eats kids for breakfast.”

“He doesn’t eat kids for breakfast,” Leo snapped, a spark of his old self returning. “That’s just stupid stories. He’s just… separate. He’s not part of the town. He won’t be one of Abernathy’s friends. It’s our only chance, Finn. We need help from someone who isn’t connected to all this.”

Finn looked from the dark woods around them back to the distant, faint glow of the town. A town that was no longer home. It was a cage, and the bars were closing in. He shivered again, a violent, rattling tremor.

“Okay,” he whispered. “Okay. But if he tries to eat me, I’m blaming you.”

Getting to the cabin was another ordeal. They had a general idea of its location, deeper into the woods, near Miller’s Creek. They stumbled through the darkness, tripping over roots and pushing through thorny bushes that tore at their clothes and skin. Their fear of the men behind them was now matched by a primal fear of the woods at night. Every hoot of an owl sounded like a warning.

After what felt like hours, they saw it. A pinprick of warm, yellow light through the trees. A single window. As they got closer, they could make out the dark silhouette of a small, rustic cabin, a thin curl of smoke rising from its stone chimney. They crept to the edge of the small clearing where it stood, hiding behind a thick oak tree. It looked peaceful, impossibly so.

“Now what?” Finn whispered, his teeth chattering.

“Now we ask for help,” Leo said, though his own heart was pounding with a new kind of fear. What if the stories were true?

He took a deep breath and stepped out from behind the tree, walking slowly toward the cabin with his hands held out where they could be seen. Finn hesitated for a second, then scurried to follow him.

They were halfway across the clearing when the cabin door creaked open. A tall, gaunt figure stood silhouetted in the doorway. In his hands, he held a long, dark object. A shotgun.

“That’s far enough,” a low, gravelly voice said. It was not angry, just firm. The voice of a man who was used to being alone and intended to keep it that way.

Leo and Finn froze. The barrel of the shotgun seemed to be pointed right between Leo’s eyes.

“Please,” Leo stammered, his voice trembling. “We… we’re not here to cause trouble. We need help. We’re in trouble.”

The man in the doorway didn’t move. “What kind of trouble brings two boys, covered in filth, to my door in the middle of the night?”

“We saw something,” Leo blurted out, the words tumbling over each other in a desperate rush. “At the old cannery. They were printing money. Fake money. And Mr. Abernathy was there. From the town council. He was in charge. He saw me, and they chased us, they chased us through the storm drains and we barely got away and we can’t go home.”

He finished, gasping for breath, his whole story laid bare in the cold night air. Finn stood beside him, shaking like a leaf, his eyes fixed on the gun.

There was a long silence. The man, Hemlock, just stood there, the gun held steady. Leo was sure he was about to tell them to get lost, or worse. The man’s silhouette was unreadable.

Then, slowly, he lowered the shotgun.

“Abernathy,” he said, the name sounding like a sour taste in his mouth. “That figures. The man’s smile never did reach his eyes.”

He stepped back from the door. “Get inside. Before you catch your death.”

The relief was so sudden and overwhelming that Leo’s knees almost buckled. He stumbled forward, Finn right behind him, and they practically fell into the small, one room cabin. The warmth from a cast iron stove washed over them, and it felt like the most wonderful thing in the world.

The cabin was spartan but meticulously tidy. A narrow bed was in one corner, a small table and two chairs in the center. Books were stacked neatly on shelves built into the walls. It smelled of woodsmoke, old paper, and drying herbs.

Hemlock closed the door behind them, the soft click of the latch sounding like a final seal on their old lives. He was older than Leo had thought, his face a roadmap of wrinkles, his hair a shock of wiry grey. But his eyes, a pale, clear blue, were sharp and missed nothing. They took in their filthy clothes, their scraped hands, their terrified expressions.

“There’s a pump out back, and a basin,” he said, his voice still gruff but without the hard edge it had outside. “Clean yourselves up as best you can. There are blankets by the stove. Your clothes are a lost cause.”

He watched them as they clumsily washed their hands and faces, the cold well water stinging their cuts. He handed them two thick, grey wool blankets. They shed their disgusting, wet clothes in a pile by the door and wrapped the warm, scratchy blankets around their shivering bodies.

They sat at the small table while Hemlock put a kettle on the stove. He didn’t coddle them. He didn’t offer false assurances. He just watched them with those piercing blue eyes.

“Now,” he said, sitting down opposite them. “Tell me everything. From the beginning. And don’t leave a single detail out.”

So Leo told him. He told him about the dare, the fence, the drainpipe. He described the sounds and smells of the cannery, the grunting workers, the thump of the press. He described the man with the limp, and the moment he saw Mr. Abernathy’s face. He told him about the dropped grate, the chase, the escape. Finn chimed in with panicked, fragmented details, his voice still shaky.

Hemlock listened without interruption, his weathered face a stony mask. When they were finished, he was silent for a long time, staring into the flames visible through the small grate of the stove.

Finally, he spoke, his voice low and serious. “You boys need to understand what you’ve done. You haven’t just stumbled onto a few crooks printing funny money. A man like Abernathy doesn’t do things small. This operation, it’s not just a crime. It’s an engine. It’s probably been propping up this town for years. The new park, the library renovations… you have to wonder where all that money really came from.”

He looked at them, and for the first time, Leo saw a flicker of something like pity in his eyes.

“You didn’t just see a secret,” Hemlock said, his voice dropping to a near whisper. “You saw the truth. And in a town built on a lie, the truth is the most dangerous thing in the world. They won’t stop looking for you. Not ever.”

Leo and Finn huddled deeper into their blankets. The warmth of the cabin couldn’t touch the icy chill that was spreading through them. They had escaped the cannery, but they had run straight into a much larger, more complex trap. They were not just witnesses to a crime anymore. They were a threat to the very heart of their town, and their only ally was a strange, grim man in a lonely cabin, who now looked at them as if they were already ghosts.

The word hung in the air, heavy and final. Ghosts. The kettle began to whistle, a thin, shrill scream that cut through the silence. Hemlock got up without a word and poured the steaming water into three chipped mugs, each containing a dark lump of tea leaves. He placed them on the table. The steam rose in fragrant clouds, smelling of mint and something earthy, like the forest floor after a rain. Leo wrapped his cold fingers around the warm ceramic, the heat a small, temporary comfort. Finn just stared at his, his knuckles white where he clutched the blanket.

“Drink,” Hemlock ordered. It was not a suggestion. Leo took a sip. It was bitter and strong, but it warmed him from the inside out. He nudged Finn, who flinched, then robotically picked up his own mug and took a small, shuddering sip.

“What are we going to do?” Leo asked, his voice barely a croak.

Hemlock looked from Leo to Finn, his gaze lingering on the younger boy’s pale, trembling face. “For now, you are going to do exactly as I say. You will eat what I give you. You will sleep where I tell you. You will not make a sound. You will not go near the window. You will not, under any circumstances, leave this cabin. Is that understood?”

Both boys nodded, their heads bobbing in unison.

“This cabin is isolated,” Hemlock continued, his voice a low rumble. “But not invisible. They’ll be searching the woods. They won’t expect you to have found shelter, not at first. They’ll expect to find you cold and lost, or… worse. But Abernathy is not a man who leaves things to chance. His men, the ones you saw, they are not simple factory workers. They are his enforcers. They know these woods too, though not as well as I do.”

He took a long drink from his own mug, his eyes never leaving them. “There is an old root cellar beneath this floor. The entrance is under the rug. If anyone comes near, that is where you go. You will not make a sound, not even if you think a spider is crawling on your face. You will breathe shallow. You will become part of the dirt and the dark until I tell you it is safe. Do you understand?”

Again, they nodded. The image of being buried alive in the dark, spiders crawling on them, sent a fresh wave of nausea through Leo. He glanced at Finn, who looked like he was about to be sick.

Hemlock seemed to read their thoughts. He rose and went to the large cast iron pot that simmered on the stove. The smell of a rich stew, venison and root vegetables, filled the small space. It was a smell from their old lives, a smell of home and safety, and the contrast with their current reality was so sharp it was painful. He ladled the thick stew into two wooden bowls and set them on the table with two spoons.

“Eat,” he said again.

This time, the command was easier to obey. Hunger, sharp and gnawing, asserted itself over their fear. Leo picked up the spoon and dug in. The meat was tender, the potatoes and carrots soft, the broth hot and salty. It was the best thing he had ever tasted. He ate ravenously, not stopping until the bowl was clean. Finn ate too, more slowly at first, then with a desperate hunger that matched his own. The food seemed to push back the cold, to build a small wall against the terror. When they were done, a heavy drowsiness settled over them, the result of exhaustion, fear, and a full stomach.

“Your clothes,” Hemlock said, gesturing to the disgusting, muddy pile by the door. “I’ll burn them after dark. They can’t be found. There are some old things in that chest. They’ll be too big, but they’re clean and dry.” He pointed to a wooden trunk at the foot of a narrow cot. “You two will sleep there. Take turns. One of you stays awake, to watch. And to listen.”

The idea of sleep seemed impossible, but the idea of staying awake, listening to the night sounds of the forest and imagining searchers creeping through the trees, was even worse.

“I’ll take first watch,” Leo volunteered, trying to sound braver than he felt. He looked at Finn, whose eyes were already drooping, his body slumping against the table.

Hemlock gave a curt nod. “Wake him in four hours. The moon will be at its peak over the ridge.”

He showed them the clothes in the chest. They were old work shirts and trousers, made of heavy canvas and flannel, worn soft with age. They smelled of woodsmoke and cedar. The boys changed, rolling up the sleeves and trouser legs. Dressed in the oversized clothes, they looked even younger and more pathetic, like children playing dress up. Hemlock pointed them to the cot, then took up his own position in the rocking chair by the stove, a long, lean hunting rifle resting across his lap. He did not look like he intended to sleep at all.

Leo helped Finn onto the cot and pulled a second blanket over him. Finn was asleep almost instantly, his breathing shallow and rapid, his face still tight with fear even in unconsciousness. Leo sat on the floor at the foot of the cot, his back against the rough hewn logs of the cabin wall. The only light came from the orange glow of the stove. The only sounds were Finn’s breathing, the gentle creak of Hemlock’s rocking chair, and the pop and hiss of the fire.

Outside, the wind whispered through the pines. Every gust sounded like footsteps. Every snap of a twig made Leo’s heart hammer against his ribs. He thought about his mom and dad. What were they doing right now? They would have called the police. They would be frantic. He imagined his mom’s face, crumpled with worry. He imagined his dad, trying to be strong, organizing a search party with the neighbors. A search party that would be looking for a lost boy, not a fugitive. Would Mr. Abernathy be there, offering his help, his face a mask of civic concern? The thought made Leo’s stomach turn.

Hemlock was right. The truth was a poison. It would make them look crazy. Two boys, hysterical and lost in the woods, telling a wild story about the town’s greatest benefactor. Who would believe them? The chief of police, who probably had dinner at Abernathy’s house? The mayor, who cut the ribbon at the new Abernathy Park? They had not just seen a crime. They had seen the town’s beating, rotten heart.

Hours crawled by. The moon climbed the sky, a sliver of bone-white light visible through the small, grimy window. The fire in the stove died down to glowing embers. Leo’s eyes grew heavy. He fought it, pinching his arm, digging his nails into his palms. He thought about the man with the limp, his face a cruel sneer. He thought about the heavy thud of the grate falling into place, sealing them in. The memories were a jolt of adrenaline, clearing the fog of sleep from his brain. He was on watch. He had to stay awake.

Finally, when the moonlight fell in a perfect, pale square on the wooden floor, he gently shook Finn’s shoulder. “Finn. It’s your turn.”

Finn woke with a gasp, his eyes wide with terror. “What is it? Are they here?”

“No, no. It’s okay,” Leo whispered, his voice hoarse. “It’s your turn to watch. I have to sleep.”

Finn sat up, pulling the blanket tighter around his shoulders. He looked around the dark cabin, at the motionless figure of Hemlock in the chair, and his body began to tremble. “I can’t, Leo. Don’t make me. I’ll just… I’ll just fall asleep. Or I’ll hear something and I’ll scream.”

“You have to,” Leo insisted, his own exhaustion making him sharp. “Hemlock said. We have to do what he says.”

He saw the tears welling in Finn’s eyes. He felt a pang of guilt, then a surge of something else. A grim responsibility. He was the older one. He was the one who had gotten them into this. He sat back down. “Okay,” he said softly. “It’s okay. I’ll stay up. You go back to sleep.”

Finn looked at him, his expression a mixture of relief and shame. “I’m sorry, Leo.”

“It’s fine,” Leo lied. “I’m not tired anyway.”

He watched Finn lie back down and curl into a tight ball. Leo leaned his head back against the wall, closed his eyes for just a second, and was immediately asleep.

He was woken by a hand on his shoulder. Not a gentle shake, but a hard, firm grip. He snapped awake, his heart leaping into his throat. It was Hemlock, silhouetted against the grey light of dawn. The old man’s face was grim.

“Get up,” he said, his voice a low command. “Both of you.”

Leo scrambled up and shook Finn awake. This time Finn didn’t cry out. He just sat up, his eyes already wide with the familiar dread.

“What’s wrong?” Leo asked.

“I went out before sunrise,” Hemlock said. “Scouting the perimeter. They’re getting systematic. A search line, moving west from the cannery. They’re not just wandering and shouting. They’re professionals. And they found something.”

He opened his hand. In his palm lay a single, muddy sneaker. It was Finn’s. The one that had been sucked off his foot by the mud near the drainpipe.

Finn made a small, choked sound.

“They know you came this way,” Hemlock said, his pale blue eyes like chips of ice. “They know you’re in this part of the woods. It’s only a matter of time before they stumble on one of my trails.” He tossed the shoe into the stove, where it landed on the hot coals. A foul smell of burning rubber and wet canvas filled the air. “This cabin is no longer safe. We leave in ten minutes.”

Panic seized Leo. “Leave? Go where? We can’t go out there. They’re everywhere.”

“We are not going toward them,” Hemlock said, his patience wearing thin. “We are going deeper in. North, over the Black Ridge. It’s hard country. They won’t follow us far in there, not unless they’re certain. By the time they figure it out, we’ll be long gone.”

“Gone where?” Finn whispered.

Hemlock was already moving, stuffing a canvas rucksack with hard biscuits, a slab of dried meat, a coil of rope, and a small tin box. “There’s a logging road about three days’ walk north of here. An old one, hardly used. A man I know runs a supply truck up it once a week. If we can get to that road by Wednesday, he can get you out of the county, maybe out of the state.”

Three days. Three days of walking through the wilderness with Abernathy’s men hunting them. It seemed like an eternity. It seemed impossible.

“Now listen closely,” Hemlock said, turning to face them. He slung the rucksack over one shoulder and picked up his rifle. “The first rule of moving through the woods is to be water. You flow. You find the path of least resistance. You make no sound. You do not step on a dry twig when you can step on damp moss. You do not push a branch aside when you can duck under it. You do not speak. You do not cough. You do not sneeze. You will copy my every footstep. If I freeze, you freeze. If I drop, you drop. Your life depends on it. There are no second chances out there.”

He looked at them, his expression severe. “This is not a game. This is not a dare. This is survival. Are you ready?”

Leo looked at Finn, who was pale but seemed to have found a tiny kernel of resolve in the face of a direct plan. He looked at Hemlock, at the rifle in his hands and the grim certainty in his eyes. Their old life was a ghost. This was their new life. Leo nodded. “We’re ready.”

“Good.” Hemlock strode to the door. “Let’s go.”

He opened the door a crack, peering out into the misty dawn. The air that drifted in was cold and damp, smelling of pine needles and wet earth. He slipped outside, as silent as a shadow. He beckoned for them to follow. Leo took a deep breath, grabbed Finn’s arm, and pulled him out of the cabin and into the vast, dangerous wilderness.

The forest was a cathedral of grey light and deep green shadows. A thick mist clung to the ground, muffling sound and swallowing up the world just a few yards ahead of them. Hemlock moved with a quiet, fluid grace that belied his age. He walked with a slight stoop, his eyes constantly scanning, his feet finding purchase on stones and roots as if by instinct. Leo and Finn followed, trying to imitate him, trying to place their feet in his exact footprints.

It was harder than it looked. Their oversized boots felt clumsy. Twigs seemed to jump under their feet, snapping with reports like rifle shots. Every rustle of their borrowed canvas trousers sounded like a roar in the silent woods. After the first ten minutes, Hemlock stopped dead. He turned around so fast that Leo almost ran into him.

He didn’t speak. He just pointed a long, bony finger at Leo’s feet, then at his own. He took a single, exaggeratedly slow step, demonstrating how he rolled his foot from heel to toe, testing the ground before committing his weight. Then he pointed to his ears and shook his head. The message was clear. You are too loud.

Leo’s face burned with shame. He nodded, and they set off again. This time he focused, his every sense straining to obey. He learned to feel the ground through the soles of his boots. He learned to watch the branches ahead, to anticipate the duck and weave. Finn, smaller and lighter, was actually better at it. He moved behind Leo like a little grey ghost, his face a mask of concentration.

They walked for what felt like hours. The terrain grew steeper, the forest thicker. They climbed, their legs burning, their lungs aching in the thin, cold air. Hemlock never slowed. He was a machine, a creature of the wilderness, as much a part of it as the ancient pines and the moss-covered rocks.

Sometime around midday, he held up a hand. They froze instantly. He crouched, gesturing for them to get down. Leo and Finn dropped to the damp earth behind a thicket of ferns. Hemlock crept forward a few paces, peering down the slope. Leo crawled up beside him, his heart pounding.

Below them, perhaps half a mile away, was a clearing. And in the clearing were three men. They were dressed in dark, practical clothing, and they were not carrying hunting rifles. They carried short, ugly-looking shotguns. One of them held a leash attached to a large, restless dog, a German shepherd that sniffed the air, its tail low. It was not a search party of concerned citizens. These were the men from the cannery. These were the hunters.

They were talking, their voices too distant to make out the words, but the tone was clear. They were frustrated. They were methodical. They were spreading out, preparing to sweep the next section of the hillside. The section just below where Leo, Finn, and Hemlock were hiding.

Hemlock backed away from the edge, silent as smoke. He motioned for the boys to follow him, not back the way they came, but sideways, moving parallel to the ridge. He led them into a jumble of massive boulders, a place where the forest floor was a maze of stone and deep shadows. He squeezed them into a narrow crevice between two giant rocks, a space so tight they were pressed together, their faces inches apart. The air was cold and smelled of stone and damp lichen.

“Stay here,” Hemlock breathed, his voice a ghost of a whisper. “Do not move. Do not make a sound.”

And then he was gone.

Leo’s heart was a frantic drum against his ribs. He could hear Finn’s ragged, shallow breaths beside him. They were trapped. The hunters were below them, the dog with them. If the dog got their scent… He pressed his face against the cold, rough stone, squeezing his eyes shut, trying to will himself invisible. He could hear the dog now. A sharp bark, then another. It was closer. He heard the muffled shouts of the men. They were coming up the slope. They were coming toward the boulders. This was it. They were going to be found.

The dog’s bark was a hammer blow in the quiet woods. It was not a bark of discovery, not yet. It was a bark of interest, a sharp, questioning sound that sliced through the damp air. Leo felt Finn flinch beside him, a tiny, convulsive movement. He wanted to reach out, to put a hand on his brother’s shoulder, but Hemlock’s command was absolute. Do not move.

The shouts of the men followed, closer now, the words still indistinct but the tone sharp with command. The dog barked again, a series of excited yaps. It had their scent. It had to. There was no other reason for it to be so agitated in this specific place. Leo pressed his cheek harder against the rock. The cold of it was a solid, real thing in a world that was dissolving into pure fear. He could smell the wet lichen, the rich, dark soil, the decay of a hundred autumns packed into the earth.

He strained his ears, trying to separate the sounds. There was the rustle of leaves, the snap of a twig. A man’s voice, clearer this time. “What is it, boy? What you got?”

The scrape of a boot on stone. So close. So terribly close. Leo held his breath, his lungs burning. He could feel his own pulse in his throat, a wild, panicked bird trapped in a cage of bone. Finn’s breathing had become a series of tiny, hitching gasps. It was the sound of a child trying not to cry. Leo squeezed his eyes shut. Don’t cry, Finn. Please, please don’t make a sound.

The dog whined, a high, frustrated noise, right at the base of their boulder. It was sniffing, loud, wet, snuffling sounds that seemed to be coming from just inches below Leo’s feet. He could almost feel the vibration of it through the rock. He imagined its wet black nose, twitching, its intelligent eyes trying to peer into the shadows.

A shadow fell over the narrow opening of the crevice.

Leo’s eyes flew open. A boot. A heavy, mud-caked work boot was planted on the stone not two feet from his face. He could see the dark, thick fabric of the man’s trousers, the way they creased at the ankle. He could smell him. A stale, sour smell of sweat and cigarette smoke and wet wool. The man was standing right there. He had only to bend down, to peer into the darkness.

The dog whined again, pawing at the base of the rock.

“There’s nothing here,” the man said. His voice was a low rumble, impatient. “Just a rabbit hole or something. C’mon.”

Another voice, further away. “He’s got something, Jed. That dog don’t lie.”

“It’s a damn maze of rocks,” Jed grumbled. He shifted his weight. The boot lifted and slammed down again. “Could be anything. A coon. Let’s keep moving. We need to clear this ridge before dark.”

Leo’s entire existence had shrunk to that boot, that voice, the smell of that man. He watched, mesmerized by terror, as the man leaned forward slightly. The fabric of his pants strained. He was going to look. He was going to bend down and his eyes would adjust to the dark and he would see them. He would see two pairs of terrified eyes staring back.

Then it happened.

From somewhere off to their left, high up on the ridge, came a sudden, sharp clatter. It was the sound of a dozen small rocks skittering and tumbling down a stone face, a distinct and unmistakable noise in the otherwise quiet woods. It was too loud for a squirrel, too deliberate for a random slide. It sounded like something heavy had stumbled.

The dog’s head snapped up. It let out a sharp, loud bark, completely different from its frustrated whining. This was a bark of pursuit.

“What was that?” the other voice called out.

“Over there,” Jed said, his body turning away from the crevice. The shadow lifted. The boot was gone. “Sounded big. A deer maybe. C’mon, boy!”

There was the jingle of a chain, the sound of the dog scrambling through the undergrowth, its barks receding as it charged toward the source of the noise. Leo heard the men crashing after it, their voices eager now. “Head it off! Don’t let it get down the other side!”

The sounds faded. The crashing, the shouting, the barking. They grew fainter and fainter until they were swallowed by the vast, indifferent silence of the forest.

Leo and Finn stayed frozen in the crevice. Leo did not dare to move, did not dare to breathe. Was it a trick? Were they waiting, circling back? He listened, his body a single, taut nerve. He heard nothing but the drip of water from a fern, the sigh of the wind in the high branches of the pines. His heart was still hammering, but the frantic, panicked rhythm was beginning to slow.

Minutes passed. Five. Ten. It felt like an eternity. Finn started to shake, a deep, uncontrollable shivering that had nothing to do with the cold. Leo finally risked moving. He slid a hand over and gripped his brother’s arm. The muscles were rigid.

“It’s okay,” Leo whispered, the words catching in his dry throat. “They’re gone.”

As if summoned by his voice, a shadow appeared at the entrance. Leo flinched, his throat closing with a fresh wave of fear, but it was Hemlock. He materialized from the woods without a sound, his face as grim and unreadable as ever. He put a finger to his lips, then beckoned them out.

Leo and Finn scrambled from the crevice, their limbs stiff and clumsy. Leo’s legs trembled, and he had to put a hand against the cold rock to steady himself. Finn just stood there, his face pale, his eyes wide and vacant with shock.

“They will be back,” Hemlock said, his voice low and urgent. “When they find nothing where the noise was, they will know it was a trick. They will sweep this area again, properly. We have to move. Now.”

He didn’t wait for an answer. He turned and plunged back into the forest, moving faster than before. Leo grabbed Finn’s hand. It was ice cold. “C’mon, Finn. We have to go. We have to keep up.”

Finn stumbled after him, his feet dragging. They half ran, half fell down the other side of the ridge, away from the hunters. The terrain was treacherous, a steep slope of loose soil and slippery rocks, hidden under a thick blanket of dead leaves. Leo fell twice, his hands and knees scraped raw. Each time, he scrambled up, pulling a whimpering Finn along with him. Hemlock never looked back. He was a relentless force, pulling them forward through sheer momentum.

The sun was sinking lower, casting long, distorted shadows through the trees. The air grew colder. The forest, which had felt alive and wild, now began to feel menacing. Every dark space between the trees looked like a place to hide, or a place where something could be hiding.

“Hemlock,” Leo panted, his voice ragged. “Where are we going?”

Hemlock glanced over his shoulder, his eyes dark. “A place they will not look.”

“Why are they looking so hard?” Leo asked, the question that had been burning in his mind. “What did we see?”

Hemlock stopped for a second, turning to face them. His expression was serious, the lines around his eyes etched deep. “You saw the crates. You saw what was in them.”

“Fish,” Leo said. “It was just fish.”

“No,” Hemlock said, and his voice was flat, final. “It was not just fish. The cannery is a door. It lets things into this town that do not belong here. And it lets things out. Things they do not want anyone to know about. They are not hunting you because you were trespassing. They are hunting you because you are loose ends. Now come. We are losing the light.”

He said nothing more, just turned and resumed his punishing pace. A door. The word echoed in Leo’s mind. It made no sense, but it filled him with a dread far deeper than the fear of shotguns and dogs. This was bigger than he could understand.

The sky, visible in patches through the canopy, was turning a bruised purple. A cold wind picked up, rustling the leaves with a sound like whispering voices. Then Leo felt it. A single, cold drop of water on his cheek. Then another.

Within minutes, a steady, miserable drizzle was falling. It slicked the leaves and the rocks, making every footstep a potential disaster. The temperature plummeted. The thin jackets the boys wore were quickly soaked through, the cold seeping into their bones. Finn’s shivering returned, worse this time. His teeth were chattering, a constant, audible clicking.

“I’m c-cold, Leo,” he stammered, his small face pinched and blue.

“I know. Just keep moving. Moving keeps you warm,” Leo said, parroting a line he’d heard his father say once. It sounded hollow and stupid now.

They pushed on, the rain growing heavier, turning the forest floor into a slick, muddy soup. The world was reduced to the grey, dripping trunks of trees, the sucking sound of their boots in the mud, and the constant, drumming rain. Leo lost all track of time, all sense of direction. He was just following the dark shape of Hemlock, putting one foot in front of the other, his body an aching machine running on the last fumes of adrenaline.

Finally, Hemlock led them to the edge of a ravine. Below, a creek that might have been gentle in the summer was now a raging, brown torrent, swollen with the downpour. It churned and foamed, carrying branches and debris with it, roaring in the gloom.

“We have to cross,” Hemlock stated, not asked.

“How?” Leo cried, his voice nearly lost in the noise of the water. “We’ll be swept away!”

Hemlock was already scanning the opposite bank. He pointed. “There. That log. It is wedged fast.”

Leo followed his gaze. A massive, mossy log spanned the creek, one end jammed into the bank they were on, the other caught in a tangle of roots on the far side. White water surged over the top of it, making it slick and treacherous. It looked impossible.

“I can’t,” Finn whimpered, pulling back. “I can’t, Leo.”

Hemlock turned to him, and for a moment, Leo saw something other than hardness in his eyes. It was not softness, but a kind of grim understanding. “The water will wash away our tracks,” he said, his voice raised over the roar. “The dog will lose us here. It is our only chance. I will go first. I will help you from the other side. Leo, you will bring your brother.”

He did not wait for an argument. He stepped onto the log, his movements fluid and certain. He balanced on the slick, rounded surface as easily as if he were walking on a flat road. The water crashed against his boots, but he was a rock. In seconds, he was on the other side, turning to face them.

“Leo. Now,” he commanded.

Leo’s heart was in his throat. He looked at Finn, at his brother’s terrified face, and knew he had no choice. “Okay, Finn. Listen to me. We’re going to do this together. You hold onto my jacket. Don’t look down. Just look at me, okay? Just keep looking at me.”

Finn nodded, his eyes huge. He clung to the back of Leo’s jacket with a desperate grip.

Leo took a deep breath and stepped onto the log. The bark was slimy with moss and rain. The power of the water surging over his boots was terrifying, a living thing trying to pull him off his feet. He took a shuffling step, then another, his arms out for balance.

“That’s it, Leo. Come on,” Finn whispered behind him.

He focused on Hemlock, a steady shadow on the far bank. He took another step. The log vibrated with the force of the current. He was halfway across. He could feel Finn’s weight pulling on his jacket, feel the small, trembling body behind him.

Then Finn slipped.

His foot went off the side of the log. He cried out, a sharp gasp of terror, and his weight yanked Leo backward. Leo’s own feet slid on the slick moss. He windmilled his arms, fighting for balance, the roar of the water filling his ears. Finn was dangling, his full weight now pulling on Leo’s jacket, his feet kicking in the churning water.

“Leo!” he screamed.

Pure instinct took over. Leo threw himself forward, dropping to his stomach on the log, the rough bark scraping his chest. The icy water poured over him. He grabbed a handful of Finn’s collar, his other hand clinging desperately to the slimy wood.

“I’ve got you!” he yelled, his voice raw. “I’ve got you, Finn!”

Finn was crying now, terrified sobs torn away by the wind and the water. The current was pulling at him, trying to tear him from Leo’s grasp. Leo’s fingers were numb with cold, his arm screaming with the strain. He wasn’t going to be able to hold on.

Suddenly, a hand clamped down on his shoulder. It was Hemlock. He had come back onto the log. He reached past Leo, his long arm snaking down, and grabbed Finn under the arms. With a grunt of effort that seemed impossible for one man, he hauled Finn up out of the water and deposited him, sputtering and coughing, onto the log.

“Get across,” Hemlock ordered, his voice tight with strain.

He practically herded them the rest of the way. Leo crawled on his hands and knees, dragging a sobbing Finn behind him. They collapsed onto the muddy bank, soaked, freezing, and utterly spent. Hemlock was beside them a moment later.

Leo lay on his back, the cold mud seeping through his clothes, the rain pelting his face. He was exhausted beyond feeling. He had nothing left. Finn was curled in a ball beside him, his shivering so violent it seemed he might shake apart.

“Get up,” Hemlock said.

Leo didn’t move. “I can’t.”

“Yes, you can.” The voice was hard, but there was a new urgency to it. “Your brother will die of cold if we stay here. We are close.”

The words cut through Leo’s exhaustion. He looked at Finn, at the blue lips and the pale, vacant face. Fear, sharp and clear, gave him a new surge of strength. He pushed himself up onto his elbows, then his knees.

“Help me,” he said to Hemlock.

Together, they got Finn to his feet. He was a dead weight between them. They supported him, half carrying, half dragging him away from the roaring creek and up another, shorter incline. The trees here were different. Ancient, massive hemlocks and cedars, their branches so thick that the rain barely penetrated. The ground was a soft carpet of needles, and the air was still and quiet.

Hemlock led them to what looked like a solid wall of rock, a cliff face covered in moss and ferns. It seemed like a dead end. But then Hemlock pushed aside a thick curtain of hanging ferns, revealing a dark, narrow opening. It was not a cave. It was a doorway, its edges too square, too deliberate, to be natural.

He guided them inside. The air was immediately still and dry, and smelled of woodsmoke and pine. It was pitch black. Leo heard Hemlock shuffling, a scrape of flint and steel, and then a spark flared. A moment later, the golden light of a lantern bloomed, pushing back the darkness.

They were in a small, low-ceilinged room. It was built into the rock, the back wall the raw stone of the mountain itself, the other walls made of thick, hand-hewn timbers. A stone hearth was built into the rock wall, a bed of cold ashes within it. In one corner was a low cot covered in animal furs. In another, a stack of firewood. It was the simplest, most primitive dwelling Leo had ever seen, and it was the most beautiful.

“Get his wet clothes off,” Hemlock commanded, already kneeling at the hearth, skillfully arranging kindling.

Leo fumbled with Finn’s buttons, his own fingers numb and useless. Hemlock came over and brushed him aside, his movements quick and efficient. He stripped Finn of his soaked clothes and wrapped him in two thick, heavy bearskins from the cot. He carried the shivering boy and set him close to the hearth. A moment later, he had a small fire crackling, its warmth beginning to spread through the tiny room.

Hemlock then handed Leo a rough, homespun tunic and trousers. “Yours too.”

Leo changed, his wet clothes falling to the floor in a sodden heap. The dry fabric was coarse but wonderfully warm. He sat on the floor by the fire, next to Finn, and held his hands out to the growing flames, feeling the life creep back into them. Finn’s shivering was starting to subside, his breathing evening out.

Hemlock moved about the small space, stoking the fire, hanging their wet clothes on a line strung near the ceiling. He moved with a quiet purpose, a man in his own territory. He put a small, blackened pot over the flames.

For a long time, no one spoke. The only sounds were the crackle of the fire and the faint, distant drumming of the rain outside. The warmth was a heavy blanket, making Leo’s eyelids feel like lead. He had never felt so tired. But he was safe. For now.

He looked at Hemlock, who was staring into the flames, his craggy face illuminated by the flickering light. He was a man of the forest, a man of rocks and shadows. He had saved them.

“Thank you,” Leo said, his voice quiet.

Hemlock did not look at him. He just nodded, once. He took the pot from the fire and poured a steaming, fragrant broth into two wooden cups. He handed one to Leo. It smelled of herbs and meat.

“Drink,” he said. “It will help.”

Leo took the cup. The warmth spread through his hands, up his arms. He looked at his brother, who was now sleeping, his face relaxed, nestled deep in the warm furs. He looked at the fire, at the rough but solid walls of their refuge. They had escaped. They had survived. But as he sipped the hot, savory broth, Hemlock’s words came back to him. The cannery is a door. He knew, with a certainty that settled deep in his gut, that the hunters and the dog were only the beginning. This was not over. It had just started.

The warmth of the broth was a lie. It was a temporary comfort, a fleeting truce in a war he was only just beginning to understand. Leo drank it slowly, making it last. He watched the steam rise from his wooden cup and curl into the smoky air of the hut. Outside, the storm had not relented. The rain beat a frantic, endless rhythm against the mountain, a sound that should have been soothing but now felt like a countdown.

“They are not hunters,” Hemlock said, his voice a low rumble that barely disturbed the quiet. He had not moved, still staring into the fire as if reading a story in the embers.

Leo’s head snapped up. He had thought the man was lost in his own world. “What?”

“The men who were chasing you. They are not hunters.”

“They had a dog. They were tracking us.”

“That was not a dog,” Hemlock said simply. He finally turned his head, and his eyes, deep-set and shadowed, found Leo’s. They were the color of wet stone, and held a weariness that seemed older than the mountain itself. “And they are not hunters. They are Wardens.”

The word meant nothing to Leo, but the way Hemlock said it sent a fresh chill down his spine, a cold that the fire could not touch. “Wardens? Wardens of what?”

Hemlock looked away again, back to the flames. “The cannery. The door.” He picked up a long, thin stick and prodded the logs, sending a shower of bright orange sparks up toward the stone ceiling. “They guard it. They gather for it.”

Leo felt his mind struggling to connect the pieces. The cannery, that stinking, derelict building on the coast. His father had worked there, years ago, before it closed down. It was just a place of rust and rot, of broken windows and seabirds. “I don’t understand. It’s just an old factory.”

“It was,” Hemlock corrected. “Now it is something else. A place of transition. A place where things are… changed.” He paused, and the silence stretched, filled only by the fire’s crackle. “You and the boy, you came from the mainland. From the city.” It was not a question.

Leo nodded, his throat tight. “Our parents… they sent us here. To stay with a friend. They said it would be safer.” The irony of it was a bitter taste in his mouth.

“There is no safe place on this island,” Hemlock stated, his voice flat and final. “Not anymore. Not for those who carry the scent.”

“The scent?” Leo asked, confused. “What scent?”

“The scent of outside. The scent of before.” Hemlock gestured vaguely with the stick. “They can smell it on you. The ones who have passed through the door, they lose it. The Wardens, they can tell who belongs and who does not. That creature they used to track you… it was a vessel. It follows the scent.”

Leo thought of the dog’s eyes, the unnatural stillness of its gaze before it lunged. A vessel. The word was cold, clinical. “What was it?”

“It was a man, once,” Hemlock said, and the simple horror of the statement landed like a physical blow. “A man who went to the cannery. They all go to the cannery. Some are chosen. Most are… repurposed. Used as tools. Like that one.”

Leo felt sick. The savory broth in his stomach churned. He set the cup down on the stone floor, his appetite gone. He looked over at Finn, whose small chest rose and fell in the steady rhythm of sleep. His brother was untouched by this knowledge, safe in his dreams. Leo felt a fierce, desperate wave of protectiveness wash over him. He had to keep him that way. He had to.

“Why?” Leo whispered. “Why are they doing this? Who is doing this?”

Hemlock was silent for a long time. He seemed to be weighing his words, or perhaps deciding if Leo was worth the effort of an explanation. “Years ago,” he began, his voice softer now, almost a memory. “A man came to the island. A rich man from the city. He bought the cannery. Said he was going to reopen it. Modernize it. He offered jobs. Good jobs. People were hopeful.” He let out a short, harsh sound that might have been a laugh. “They were fools. I was a fool. I almost worked there myself.”

He turned to look at Leo again, and for the first time, Leo saw a flicker of something other than grim resolve in his eyes. It was pain. Deep and old. “He wasn’t interested in canning fish. He was a scientist. Or a madman. Or both. He was looking for something. A way to open a door that was meant to stay shut. He used the town. The people. They were his first experiments.”

“And the Wardens?”

“The successful ones,” Hemlock said. “The ones who went through the door and came back… different. Stronger. Faster. But empty. They serve him now. They bring him new subjects. From the mainland. From the boats that wreck on the shoals. Anyone who wanders too close.”

“They took everyone who stayed,” Hemlock added, his voice dropping so low it was almost lost in the sound of the rain. “My wife. My daughter.”

The confession hung in the small, warm room, heavier than the smoke, more chilling than the storm. Leo finally understood. This was not a random act of kindness. Hemlock was not just a hermit who happened upon two boys in the woods. He was a soldier in a war that had already cost him everything.

“I’m sorry,” Leo said, the words feeling small and useless.

Hemlock just shook his head, a curt, dismissive gesture. He was done with sorrow. It was a luxury he could no longer afford. He stood up. “The broth is gone. You need to rest. Both of you.”

He pointed to the cot where Finn slept. “There is room. Sleep.”

Leo looked at the furs, then back at Hemlock. “Where will you sleep?”

Hemlock gestured to the hard stone floor by the hearth. “I will watch the fire.”

Leo knew it was more than that. He would watch the door. He would listen to the storm. He would be their sentinel. Leo did not argue. He crawled over to the cot, the rough floor scraping his knees. He lay down beside his brother, careful not to wake him. Finn mumbled in his sleep and shifted closer, his small hand finding Leo’s arm and gripping it tightly, even in his unconsciousness.

Leo pulled a heavy fur over both of them. It smelled of pine and woodsmoke and wild things. He lay on his back, staring up at the rough timbered ceiling, barely visible in the flickering firelight. His body screamed for sleep, but his mind was a whirlwind. Wardens. Vessels. A door that changed people. A madman in a cannery. It was the stuff of nightmares, of children’s scary stories told around a campfire. But it was real. The chase, the cold, the terror… it was all real.

He turned his head to look at Hemlock. The man sat with his back against the stone wall, his legs crossed. He was sharpening a long, wicked-looking knife on a whetstone. The blade was not steel. It was a dull, black material that seemed to drink the firelight. The rhythmic scrape of stone on metal was a steady, unnerving sound. Scrape. Scrape. Scrape. A sound of preparation. A promise of violence.

Leo closed his eyes. He thought of his mother’s face, her hand on his cheek as she told him to be strong, to look after Finn. He had promised. He drifted into a restless, shallow sleep, the scrape of the knife and the beat of the rain following him into his dreams. He dreamed of a rusty door groaning open onto a sky filled with black stars, and of a dog with a man’s eyes, running endlessly through a forest of dead trees.

He woke with a start, his heart pounding. The hut was filled with a soft gray light. The rain had stopped. The silence was absolute, and somehow more frightening than the storm. The fire was a bed of glowing red embers, giving off a gentle warmth. Finn was still asleep beside him, his face peaceful.

Hemlock was gone.

Panic seized Leo. He sat bolt upright, his eyes darting around the small dwelling. The door, a heavy plank of wood, was slightly ajar, letting in a sliver of the cold morning air. Had he left them? Had he decided they were too much trouble?

Just as the thought took root, the door swung open and Hemlock entered. He carried a brace of fat rabbits in one hand and a small bundle of green herbs in the other. He moved with the same quiet purpose as the night before. He did not seem surprised to see Leo awake.

“The storm has passed,” he said, laying the rabbits on a flat stone near the hearth. “They will be able to track again.”

Leo’s blood ran cold. “The Wardens?”

Hemlock nodded. He added a few small logs to the embers, which quickly caught, and soon a small fire was dancing again. “Their vessel will be able to pick up your scent now that the ground is not washed clean. We cannot stay here. This place is a respite, not a fortress. They know of it. It will be one of the first places they look.”

Finn stirred on the cot, his eyelids fluttering. “Leo?” he mumbled, his voice thick with sleep. He sat up, rubbing his eyes, looking around the strange room with a child’s unfiltered curiosity. “Where are we?”

“We’re safe, Finn,” Leo said, his voice softer than he intended. He put a hand on his brother’s shoulder. “This is… a friend’s house.”

Finn looked at Hemlock, who was now skillfully skinning one of the rabbits with his strange black knife. The boy did not look afraid, just curious. “Are we going home now?”

The question hung in the air. Leo looked at Hemlock, whose face was unreadable. “Not yet, Finn,” Leo said gently. “We’re going to go for a long walk first. An adventure.”

It was a weak lie, but it was the only one he had.

Hemlock did not acknowledge the exchange. He worked quickly, butchering the meat and putting it into the pot with some water and the herbs he had gathered. Soon, the smell of cooking meat filled the hut. It should have been comforting, but to Leo it just felt like fuel. Fuel for a journey he did not want to take.

They ate in silence, the three of them. Hemlock gave them each a piece of roasted meat on a strip of bark. It was simple and delicious. Finn ate eagerly, his ordeal from the day before seemingly forgotten, replaced by the immediacy of a warm meal and a new place. Leo forced himself to eat, knowing he would need the strength.

When they were finished, Hemlock stood. “Gather your things.”

Their things consisted of the clothes on their backs. Their wet clothes from the day before were now stiff and dry, hanging from the line near the ceiling. Hemlock took them down and handed them to Leo. “Put these on over what you are wearing. Another layer will help against the cold. The mountain air is thin.”

They dressed. The homespun tunic Hemlock had given him was warm, but Leo was glad for the familiar feel of his own shirt and trousers, even if they were wrinkled and smelled of damp wool. As Leo was tying his boots, Hemlock came to him and pressed something cold and heavy into his hand.

It was a knife. Smaller than Hemlock’s, with a simple wooden handle and a sharp steel blade. It was a practical, sturdy tool.

“You know how to use one?” Hemlock asked.

Leo nodded. His father had taught him how to carve, how to whittle sticks on their rare camping trips. This was different. He knew Hemlock was not giving him a tool for carving.

“Good,” Hemlock said. “Your brother is your charge. You keep him safe. You keep him quiet. I will watch the path ahead, and the one behind. Do exactly as I say. Do not ask questions. Do you understand?”

“I understand,” Leo said, his fingers tightening around the handle of the knife. He slid it into his belt. The weight of it was a small, hard point of reality against his hip.

Hemlock went to the door and peered out, scanning the landscape for a long moment. The world outside was painted in the sharp, clean colors of a morning after a storm. The sky was a pale, brilliant blue. The wet trees glistened. It looked peaceful. Beautiful. Leo knew it was a lie.

“It is time,” Hemlock said, turning back to them. He looked at Finn. “The game we play now is called silence. The one who makes a sound, loses. Can you play that game, boy?”

Finn, ever eager for a game, nodded seriously. “I can be quiet.”

“I know you can,” Hemlock said, and for a fleeting second, the hard lines of his face softened. He was looking at Finn, but Leo knew he was seeing another child. A ghost in the firelight.

Hemlock pushed the door open wide. The cold, crisp air flooded the hut. He gestured for them to go. Leo took Finn’s hand. The boy’s hand was small and warm in his own. Leo gave it a squeeze, a silent promise.

He led Finn out of the hut, stepping from the warm, smoky dark into the bright, cold morning. The light was almost blinding. They stood on a narrow ledge. Below them, the forest canopy stretched out like a vast, green sea. In the distance, plumes of mist rose from the valleys.

Hemlock followed them out, pulling the heavy wooden door shut behind him. He took a moment to brush dirt and leaves over the threshold, obscuring their exit. A predator’s instinct. Then he pointed, not down into the forest, but up. Up the steep, rocky face of the mountain.

“We go high,” he said. “The Wardens do not like the high rocks. Their vessels are not made for climbing.”

He started moving, not on any discernible path, but finding handholds and footholds in the rock face with an ease that spoke of long practice. He moved like a goat, steady and certain. He paused and looked back at them. “Stay close. Step where I step.”

Leo looked at the sheer rock. He looked at Finn. Fear, cold and sharp, stabbed at him. This was impossible.

But then he looked at Hemlock, who was waiting patiently, his craggy face set like the stone he climbed. And he looked at Finn’s small, trusting face looking up at him. There was no choice. There was only the promise he had made.

“Okay, Finn,” Leo said, his voice a low whisper, mindful of the game. “Hold on to me tight. Don’t look down.”

He put Finn on his back, piggyback style. The boy wrapped his arms around Leo’s neck and his legs around his waist. He was not heavy, but his weight felt immense, a precious, fragile burden.

Leo took a deep breath, the thin air burning his lungs. He placed his hands on the rock, feeling for a hold. The stone was cold and wet. He found a grip, and then a place for his foot. He began to climb, following the grim figure above him, leaving the temporary safety of the hut far below.

They had been climbing for what felt like hours. The sun was higher now, but it offered little warmth. A sharp wind whipped around them, pulling at their clothes, trying to pry them from the mountain’s face. Leo’s muscles burned. His fingers were raw. Finn was a dead weight on his back, his silence a testament to his fear.

Hemlock, impossibly, seemed tireless. He moved ahead, a gray shape against the gray rock, always finding the path. He stopped on a narrow, windswept ledge and waited for them.

Leo scrambled the last few feet, his lungs heaving, and collapsed onto the ledge next to him, gently lowering Finn to the ground. The boy’s face was pale, his lips blue with cold, but he gave Leo a weak, brave smile.

“I’m winning the game,” he whispered.

Leo’s heart ached. “You are,” he whispered back.

Hemlock was not looking at them. He was staring down at the forest below, his eyes narrowed. “They have found the hut,” he said.

Leo’s head snapped up. He crawled to the edge of the ledge and peered over. From this height, the forest was a textured carpet of green and brown. At first, he saw nothing. Then Hemlock pointed.

“There.”

Leo followed his finger. He saw it now. A flicker of movement in the trees near the base of the rock face where the hut was hidden. And then another. Two figures, dressed in dark, practical clothing, moving with a fluid, unnatural grace. The Wardens.

As he watched, a third shape emerged from the trees. It was low to the ground, moving with a terrifying purpose. It was not a dog. It was too large, its movements too jerky, too controlled. The vessel. It stopped at the base of the cliff and lifted its head. Even from this distance, Leo felt as if its dead eyes were looking right at him.

It let out a sound. Not a bark or a howl, but a high, piercing shriek that echoed off the rocks, a sound of metal scraping and a human scream all at once. It was a sound of pure wrongness.

Finn flinched and buried his face in Leo’s side.

“It has our scent,” Hemlock said, his voice grim. “It knows we came this way.”

Leo watched in horror as one of the Wardens looked up, directly at their ledge. The figure raised a hand and pointed. There was no doubt. They had been seen.

“They will follow,” Hemlock said. He was already turning away, looking further up the mountain. “We must keep moving. Now.”

He did not wait for an answer. He began to climb again, moving faster this time. Leo grabbed Finn’s hand. The game was over. Now, they were just running. He scrambled after Hemlock, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs, the horrifying shriek of the vessel echoing in his ears, a promise of what was coming for them. The door was behind them, but its servants were right below.

The air grew thinner, colder. Each breath was a shard of ice in Leo’s lungs. He did not look down again. He could not. The image of the pointing Warden and the upturned, empty face of the vessel was burned into his mind. He focused on the rock in front of him, on the placement of his raw fingers, on the small, terrified trembles that ran through Finn’s body, still clinging to his back. The boy was silent now. The game had faded completely, leaving behind only the stark, freezing reality of the chase. Ahead, Hemlock was a blur of efficient motion. He did not seem to tire. He was a part of the mountain, a piece of animated granite and resolve. He moved with a certainty that bordered on inhuman, his gray clothes blending with the stone until he was just a suggestion of a man, a ghost leading them up into the clouds.

The shriek came again. It was closer this time. It sliced through the whistle of the wind, a sound that vibrated in Leo’s bones. He risked a glance over his shoulder. The two Wardens were climbing. They moved with a chilling, synchronized grace, their dark forms scaling the rock face with an ease that defied the laws of physics. They were hunters, born to this. But it was the third shape that stole the air from Leo’s chest. The vessel was climbing too. It did not climb like a man or an animal. It scuttled, its limbs bending at impossible angles, finding purchase where there was none. It moved in a series of violent, jerky bursts, like a broken clockwork toy powered by malice. Metal scraped against stone, a rhythmic, grinding counterpoint to its piercing screams. It was gaining on them.

“Faster,” Hemlock’s voice was a low command, carried back by the wind.

Leo didn’t have anything left. His arms screamed, his legs were numb with effort. Finn’s weight felt immense, a small, precious anchor pulling him back toward the monsters below. “I can’t,” he gasped, his voice thin and lost in the vastness.

Hemlock paused on a slightly wider outcrop, his face a mask of stone. He looked back, not at Leo, but at Finn. His eyes, usually so hard and distant, held a flicker of something else. Pity, perhaps. Or memory. “Yes, you can,” he said, his voice flat but absolute. “You will.” He turned and continued, not waiting for a reply. There was no alternative. There was only up, or the thing scuttling toward them. Leo dug his fingers into a new hold, the sharp rock tearing at his skin. He gritted his teeth and pulled himself upward, dragging Finn with him. He had to. He would.

They climbed into the low, gray belly of a cloud. The world dissolved into a swirling mist. For a moment, Leo felt a surge of hope. They were hidden. But the hope died as the vessel’s shriek echoed through the fog, seeming to come from all directions at once. It did not need to see them. It could smell them. It could feel them. It was a predator of a different kind, hunting a sense Leo could not comprehend. The fog was a shroud, not a shield. It made the climbing worse, the rock slick with moisture, the next handhold a mystery until his fingers found it. He was moving on instinct alone, following the faint sounds of Hemlock’s movement ahead.

Suddenly, Hemlock stopped. Leo nearly collided with his legs. They were pressed into a narrow fissure, a vertical crack in the mountain’s face barely wide enough for a man to stand. “In here,” Hemlock grunted, and before Leo could ask, he seemed to melt into the rock. He was gone.

“Hemlock?” Leo whispered, his voice trembling.

“Here. Get the boy inside.” The voice was muffled, coming from the rock itself. Leo peered into the fissure. It was a dark, tight opening, a black slash against the gray. It looked like a tomb. But behind them, the scraping sound was getting closer, louder. He could hear the vessel’s unnatural claws finding purchase just below their last position. He turned, carefully maneuvering Finn off his back. The boy was shivering uncontrollably, his eyes wide and dark in his pale face. “It’s okay,” Leo lied, his own fear a cold knot in his stomach. “It’s another part of the game. A secret tunnel.”

Finn just shook his head, burying his face in Leo’s jacket. Leo pushed him gently toward the opening. “Go on, Finn. Squeeze through. Hemlock is right there.” Finn hesitated, then, with a small whimper, he slipped into the crack and vanished into the darkness. Leo followed, sucking in his chest, his shoulders scraping against the cold, wet stone. The opening was horribly tight. For a terrifying second, he thought he was stuck, a plug in a hole with a monster snapping at his heels. Then a strong hand grabbed his collar and pulled. He stumbled forward, falling out of the fissure and onto a level, dusty floor.

He was in a cave. It was not large, a small, round chamber hidden within the mountain’s heart. The only light was the faint gray glow from the crack they had just entered. The air was still and frigid, but it was a merciful silence. The wind was gone. The shrieking was gone. For the first time in hours, Leo could hear his own frantic heartbeat. Hemlock was already moving to the entrance, pulling a loose slab of rock, wedging it into the fissure. It did not seal the opening completely, but it covered most of it, plunging the small cave into near total darkness.

Leo’s legs gave out. He slid down the rock wall, pulling Finn into his lap. The boy was limp, exhausted. Leo held him tight, trying to share what little warmth he had left. He could feel the fine tremor in his own limbs, the deep, aching burn of his overtaxed muscles. He closed his eyes, just for a second, but the image of the scuttling vessel made him snap them open again.

“Will it find us?” he asked the darkness, his voice hoarse.

Hemlock’s shape moved near the blocked entrance. “Yes,” he said simply. “It will smell us at the opening. It will try to get in.”

“Then why are we stopping?”

“Because you are fading. And the boy is worse.” Hemlock came and knelt before them. Leo could not see his face, only his silhouette against the faint light of the crack. “The air is thin. The cold is deep. But mostly, it is the pursuit. The thing from the door, it resonates with him. It feeds on his fear, and it drains the warmth from him by its very nature.”

Leo looked down at Finn. The boy’s breathing was shallow. He felt colder than the air around them. “What is that thing?” Leo asked, his voice low and intense. “You called it a vessel. What does that mean?”

Hemlock was silent for a long moment. The only sound was the faint, distant moan of the wind outside the cave. “It was a person, once,” he said finally. His voice was grim, weighted with a knowledge Leo did not want to have. “The Wardens find them. The lost. The broken. Those with no one to miss them. They are taken to the door. They are hollowed out. Their spirit, their will, their memory, it is all scraped away. Then the door fills them. It pours a sliver of its own awareness into the husk. It animates the flesh and bone, reinforces it with shards of metal and whispers of its own wrong geometry. It is not alive. It is a puppet. A tool. A vessel for the door’s will to hunt in this world.”

A wave of nausea washed over Leo. He thought of the jerky, unnatural movements, the shriek that was part human and part machine. A person. That thing had been a person. He held Finn tighter. “And it hunts him,” Leo whispered. “Why?”

“The boy is an anomaly,” Hemlock said. “He is a key, but one that was dropped on the wrong side of the lock. He has a spark of the door’s power inside him, but it is not the same. It is… different. Uncontrolled. The door wants it back. Or it wants it extinguished. It cannot tolerate a loose thread. The vessel doesn’t hunt by sight or sound. It hunts by that resonance. It is drawn to the boy like a compass needle to the north. A compass that wants to shatter the pole it points to.”

Leo felt a fresh wave of despair. There was no hiding. There was no escape. They were a beacon, and the monster was a heat seeking missile of supernatural design. “Then what are we doing, Hemlock? Where are we running? We can’t outrun it forever.”

“We are not trying to outrun it forever,” Hemlock replied. “We are running to a place it cannot go. Or a place where its power is undone. There are places on this mountain, old places, where the world is thin. Where the rules are different. That is where we are going. Up. To the Teeth.”

“The Teeth?”

“The peaks. The very summit. Long ago, before the door, there were other powers here. Older things. Their residue remains. The vessel is a new poison. It cannot abide the old medicines.”

A loud scraping sound came from the entrance. Leo flinched. Finn whimpered in his sleep. A metallic claw was scratching at the rock slab Hemlock had wedged in the fissure. It was testing the barrier. A high pitched, inquisitive shriek, muffled by the stone, vibrated through the chamber. It had found them.

“We don’t have long,” Hemlock said, his voice devoid of panic. It was a simple statement of fact. He reached into a leather pouch at his belt and pulled out a small, hard biscuit and a strip of dried meat. He handed them to Leo. “Eat. Give some to the boy if he wakes. You need strength for what is next.”

Leo took the food, his fingers clumsy and numb. The biscuit was as hard as a rock, but he gnawed on it, the dry crumbs a comfort in his mouth. It was real. It was sustenance. “Why are you doing this, Hemlock?” he asked between bites. “Why are you helping us?”

The scraping at the entrance grew more frantic. It sounded like a giant insect trying to burrow through the mountain. Hemlock’s silhouette was still. “I was a Warden once,” he said, and the words fell into the silence of the cave like stones into a deep well.

Leo stopped chewing. He stared at the dark shape of the man who was their only hope. “What?”

“Not for long. I was young. I was drawn by the promise of order, of purpose. I saw what they did. What they became. I saw them make a vessel for the first time.” His voice was low, haunted. “I ran. Just like you. I ran up this same mountain. I was hunted. But someone helped me. An old woman who lived in the high rocks. She knew the paths. She showed me the way. She saved me from the door and its servants.” He paused. “She died two winters ago. Helping you is how I honor her. A debt paid is a soul settled. Now, eat. We move when I say.”

Leo finished the biscuit and the tough, salty meat. A small measure of strength returned to his limbs. He understood now. Hemlock wasn’t a guide. He was a penitent. This was his pilgrimage. A loud crack echoed through the cave as the vessel slammed its full weight against the stone slab. The rock shuddered in its frame. Dust rained down from the ceiling.

“It is time,” Hemlock said. He did not go to the entrance. He went to the back of the cave, to a wall that looked like solid rock. He ran his hands over it, pressing, feeling. “The mountain is not solid,” he murmured, more to himself than to Leo. “It is a honeycomb. Full of passages. You just have to know how to ask it to open.” He pressed one spot, and with a low groan of grinding stone, a section of the wall receded, revealing a passage so dark it seemed to drink the faint light from the cave. It was a maw of absolute black.

Another tremendous bang at the entrance. The slab cracked. A sliver of gray light appeared, and with it, the tip of a metallic claw, probing the air.

“Now,” Hemlock commanded. “Quickly.”

Leo scrambled to his feet, lifting a still drowsy Finn into his arms. The boy was lighter now, his body pliant with exhaustion. Leo carried him to the new opening and peered into the oppressive darkness. He could feel a cold draft coming from its depths. He hesitated.

“It is the only way,” Hemlock said, right behind him. “Go. I will be right behind you.”

Leo took a breath and stepped through, into the heart of the mountain. The passage was narrow and sloped steeply downward. The rock was slick with moisture under his boots. As soon as Hemlock was through, the stone door groaned shut behind them, plunging them into a darkness so complete it felt like a physical weight. The sound of the vessel was gone, replaced by a profound, ringing silence and the drip, drip, drip of water somewhere in the distance.

“I can’t see,” Leo said, his voice tight with panic.

“You do not need to,” Hemlock’s voice was calm in the blackness. “Keep one hand on the left wall. The path is straight for now. Walk. Do not run.”

Leo did as he was told, shuffling forward into the unknown. His hand slid along the cold, wet rock, his only connection to reality. He held Finn tightly with his other arm, the boy’s soft breathing a small, warm spot in the immense, suffocating cold. They walked in silence for what felt like an eternity, the only sound their own footsteps and the rhythmic drip of water. The air grew colder, and the downward slope began to level out.

After an immeasurable time, Hemlock spoke again. “Stop.”

Leo froze. Ahead, a faint gray light began to bleed into the darkness. They were approaching another opening. Hemlock moved past him, a shadow in the growing light. He peered out cautiously. Leo crept forward and looked over his shoulder.

They had emerged onto another ledge, much higher up the mountain. The cloud they had climbed into was below them now, a roiling sea of white. Above, the sky was a deep, bruised purple. It was snowing. Fat, wet flakes drifted down, coating the rocks in a layer of white. The world was silent, muffled by the falling snow.

“The storm will hide us,” Leo whispered, a flicker of hope returning.

“It will also try to kill us,” Hemlock countered, his gaze sweeping the terrain. “The snow will turn to ice on the rocks. We must cross this next section before it becomes impassable.” He pointed. Ahead, the ledge narrowed into a treacherous looking spine of rock, a natural bridge no wider than a man’s foot, arching over a dizzying drop. On the other side, the mountain rose in a series of jagged, shattered peaks. The Teeth.

“There,” Hemlock said, his voice low. “That is our path.”

As if summoned by his words, a sound cut through the soft hiss of the falling snow. A shriek. It came from below. Leo looked down, his stomach plummeting. Out of the sea of clouds, the vessel emerged. It was clinging to the rock face directly below the start of the stone spine, its head tilted back, its dead eyes fixed on them. The falling snow melted and steamed where it touched its metallic parts. It looked up at them and shrieked again, a sound of fury and triumph. It had found them again. And it was close.

At the same time, two other figures appeared, further down and to the right. The Wardens. They were moving steadily, undeterred by the snow, their dark clothes stark against the whitening landscape. They were flanking them, cutting off any chance of retreat. The net was closing.

“We go now,” Hemlock said, his voice urgent. He stepped onto the narrow spine of rock, balancing with an unnerving ease. “Do not look down. One foot in front of the other. Come.”

Leo looked at the narrow bridge, then at Finn in his arms, then at the monster climbing toward them. The game was over. The running was almost over. This was the end of the path. He took a deep, shuddering breath, the freezing air burning his lungs. He held Finn a little tighter, kissed the top of his head, and stepped onto the spine, into the heart of the storm, the shriek of the hollow man echoing all around them.
