The beam of the flashlight cut a weak yellow circle on the damp concrete floor. "Anything?" Mark whispered, his voice echoing slightly in the vast, empty space of the old sewer tunnel.

Leo shook his head, swinging his own light from side to side. "Just wet walls and more tunnel. Are you sure you saw it come this way?"

"I'm sure," Mark insisted. "It was big. And it wasn't any dog I've ever seen." He lowered his voice. "It went right through the grate and dropped down here."

They fell into a cautious silence, broken only by the drip, drip, drip of water from the arched ceiling. The air was cold and smelled of earth and decay. Leo shivered, pulling his jacket tighter. This was a bad idea. He knew it had been a bad idea from the moment Mark had started climbing down the rusty ladder into the storm drain.

"Let's head back," Leo said. "We can tell my dad. He'll call someone."

"And have them laugh at us? No way." Mark took a step forward. "We'll just go to the next junction. Then we'll turn around. Promise."

Leo sighed but followed. They moved carefully, their boots making soft squishing sounds on the slick floor. The tunnel ahead opened into a larger chamber, a place where several tunnels converged. In the center was a raised platform of drier concrete. Mark swept his light across it. "See? Nothing."

Suddenly a low growl rumbled from the darkness of a tunnel to their left. It was a deep, menacing sound that made the hairs on Leo's arms stand up. "Mark," he breathed.

Mark froze, his own light snapping toward the source of the noise. Two points of green light gleamed back at them, low to the ground. They weren't moving. They were just watching.

"Let's go," Leo whispered urgently. "Now."

He tugged on Mark's sleeve and they began to back away, never taking their eyes off the twin green sparks. They retreated the way they came, step by careful step. The growling stopped. For a moment, Leo felt a surge of relief. Maybe it wouldn't follow.

Then he heard it. A soft scraping sound. The click of claws on concrete. It was coming.

"Run!" Mark yelled.

They scrambled, their flashlights bouncing wildly, turning the tunnel into a chaotic strobe of light and shadow. Leo risked a glance behind him. A large, dark shape was loping after them, gaining fast. He didn't need to see it clearly to know it was not a dog. It was too big, its movements too fluid.

The ladder was just ahead. "You first!" Leo gasped, pushing Mark toward it.

Mark didn't argue. He grabbed the iron rungs and started climbing, his feet slipping on the wet metal. Leo aimed his flashlight back down the tunnel, trying to blind their pursuer. The creature paused, shaking its massive head, then let out a terrifying snarl. Leo's heart hammered against his ribs. He started up the ladder, his hands numb with cold and fear. He was halfway up when a powerful weight slammed into the ladder below him. The whole structure shuddered.

He felt a searing pain in his calf. He cried out as sharp teeth sank into the denim of his jeans and into his flesh. He kicked wildly, his foot connecting with something solid. The teeth let go. He scrambled the rest of the way out of the manhole and collapsed onto the wet pavement, gasping for air. Mark was there, hauling him away from the opening just as a dark head appeared. Mark slammed the heavy iron cover back into place. A muffled howl of rage came from below.

"Are you okay?" Mark asked, his voice shaking.

"It got me," Leo groaned, clutching his leg. He felt a warm wetness spreading through his jeans. Mark knelt, his flashlight beam illuminating the torn fabric and the dark blood welling up from two deep puncture wounds.

"We need to get you home," Mark said, his face pale in the dim light. "We need to get that cleaned." A new voice cut through the night. "You boys aren't going anywhere."

They both looked up, startled. Two men stepped out of the shadows of a nearby alley. They were large, and their faces were hard. One of them held something that glinted in the streetlight.

"What do you want?" Mark asked, trying to sound brave. He stood up, putting himself between Leo and the men.

"Just a little chat," the first man said. He took a step closer. "About what you were doing in our tunnel."

"It's a city sewer," Leo said, his teeth chattering.

"Not tonight it isn't," the second man grunted. "Get them."

Mark tried to dodge, but the first man was too fast. He grabbed Mark by the collar of his jacket and lifted him effortlessly. The second man hauled Leo to his feet. Leo cried out as pain shot up his leg.

"He's hurt," Mark yelled, struggling.

"He'll live," the first man said. "For now."

They were dragged into the alley and pushed roughly against a brick wall. A car door opened and a third man got out. He was smaller than the other two, dressed in a suit that looked out of place. He looked at them with cold, uninterested eyes.

"They saw the asset?" the man in the suit asked.

"They were in the tunnel with it," the first man confirmed. "The kid's leg is proof."

The man in the suit studied Leo's bleeding leg for a moment, then looked at their faces. "Pity," he said softly. "They're too old to forget what they saw." He turned to his men. "Put them in the trunk. We'll drop them off with the others. We move out in an hour."

"What about the asset?"

"Leave it," the man in the suit commanded. "It's done its job. It will find its way out by dawn." He got back into the car without another glance.

Rough hands grabbed Leo. He felt himself being lifted, then the world went dark as the trunk lid slammed shut. He landed on something soft. It was Mark.

"Leo? You there?" Mark's voice was a muffled whisper right next to his ear.

"I'm here," Leo managed to say. The trunk smelled of gasoline and old carpet. His leg throbbed with a fiery pain. The car's engine started, and they began to move.

"What was that thing?" Mark asked after a moment.

"I don't know," Leo said. "And I don't want to find out." He tried to shift into a more comfortable position, his hand brushing against something hard and metallic on the floor of the trunk. His fingers closed around it. It felt like a tire iron. A small spark of hope ignited in the terrifying darkness.

"Me neither," Mark whispered. "But I think we're going to."

The car ride was a series of jolts and lurches in a black, suffocating box. Every bump in the road sent a fresh wave of agony through Leo’s leg. He gritted his teeth, the taste of rust and fear in his mouth. The tire iron was a cold, heavy promise in his hand. It was the only real thing in the darkness besides Mark’s breathing and the throbbing in his own body.
“Are you okay?” Mark’s whisper was a dry rustle of leaves.
“My leg hurts,” Leo said. It was an understatement of epic proportions. The skin felt tight and hot, stretched over a core of burning coals.
“I know. Just hang on.” Mark’s voice was trying for calm, but Leo could hear the tremor underneath. “When they open this thing, we have to be ready.”
“Ready for what?” Leo asked, his voice barely audible. “They’re huge, Mark.”
“I know,” Mark said again. “But we have this.” He nudged Leo’s hand, the one holding the tire iron. “When the lid opens, the light will blind them for a second. That’s our chance. You swing for the legs. I’ll go for the head. Just one good hit. Then we run.”
It sounded like a plan from a movie. A stupid movie where the heroes always won. In the stinking dark of the trunk, it felt like a prayer.
“Okay,” Leo whispered. He didn't know if he could even stand, let alone swing a heavy piece of metal. But he had to. The alternative was a quiet place with "the others." He didn't think the others were playing cards.
The car slowed. It made a wide, sweeping turn, the tires crunching on what sounded like gravel. Then it stopped. The engine cut out, and the sudden silence was more terrifying than the noise. Leo’s heart hammered against his ribs. He tightened his grip on the tire iron, his knuckles white in the imagined dark.
He heard two car doors slam shut. Muffled voices. Footsteps on the gravel, moving away from the car. Then a new sound. A heavy, groaning scrape, like a massive metal door sliding on rusty tracks. They were inside a building. A garage or a warehouse. The sound echoed, telling him the space was large.
Footsteps crunched back toward the car. Keys jingled. A metallic click right behind their heads. This was it.
“Get ready,” Mark hissed.
Leo tensed his entire body, preparing for the blinding light, for the explosion of movement and violence. He tried to coil his legs under him, but a bolt of pure fire shot up from his calf and he gasped, nearly dropping the tire iron.
The trunk lock clicked open, but the lid didn't lift.
Instead, they heard the car doors open again. The seats creaked. The engine roared back to life. The car lurched forward a few feet and then stopped. The engine went silent again. Car doors slammed. Footsteps walked away. And then, silence. A deep, profound silence that soaked into the metal box around them.
They waited. One minute. Five. Ten. Nothing.
“What’s happening?” Leo’s whisper was ragged.
“I don’t know,” Mark said. His voice was tight with confusion. “They just left us.”
Hope, fragile and treacherous, fluttered in Leo’s chest. Maybe this was it. Maybe they just wanted to scare them and leave them in a locked car. They could try to break out. They could wait for someone to find them.
The sound of the heavy sliding door scraped through the silence again. Footsteps approached the car. Not the heavy tread of the two big men, but a lighter, quicker step.
The trunk lid flew open.
Light flooded in, a single, naked bulb hanging from a high ceiling, and it was just as blinding as Mark had predicted. Leo flinched, throwing an arm over his eyes. Before he could react, a pair of hands grabbed his jacket and yanked him out. He landed hard on a concrete floor, his injured leg twisting beneath him. A scream tore from his throat, raw and animal.
He looked up through a blur of tears. It was the two big men. They loomed over him like buildings. The first one, the one who had grabbed Mark earlier, reached back into the trunk and hauled Mark out. Mark landed more gracefully, already trying to get his feet under him. He saw the tire iron lying on the floor of the trunk, forgotten in the chaos. The man saw him look. He glanced into the trunk, grunted, and slammed the lid shut. The chance was gone.
“This way,” the second man growled, kicking Leo’s good leg to get him moving.
Pain made Leo clumsy. He scrambled to his feet, leaning heavily on Mark as they were shoved across the cold concrete floor. It was a vast, empty garage. The air smelled of oil and damp earth. In the far wall was a single, heavy wooden door. It looked ancient.
The first man unlocked it with a large iron key and pulled it open. He pushed Mark through the doorway. The second man grabbed Leo by the collar and practically threw him in after. The door slammed shut behind them, the sound echoing like a gunshot. A heavy bolt slid into place on the other side.
Darkness. Not the complete blackness of the trunk, but a deep, murky gloom. A sliver of light from the garage crept in under the door, and a tiny, barred window high up in the door itself let in a pathetic square of illumination from the bare bulb outside.
It was a room, not a cage. A basement room with stone walls that wept with moisture. The air was cold and smelled of mold and despair.
And they were not alone.
“Look what the cat dragged in,” a voice said from the corner. It was a girl’s voice. Hoarse and flat.
As their eyes adjusted, they saw them. Two figures huddled against the far wall. A girl and a boy, probably around their age. The girl had dark, tangled hair and eyes that seemed too old for her face. The boy sat with his knees pulled to his chest, staring at the floor. He didn't look up.
“Who are you?” Mark asked, his voice shaking slightly.
“Does it matter?” the girl replied. “We’re the others.”
The words hit Leo like a punch to the gut. *We'll drop them off with the others.* So this was it. This was the holding pen.
“I’m Sarah,” the girl said, her tone softening a little. She seemed to decide it didn’t matter if they knew her name. “That’s Ben.” She nudged the boy with her foot. He flinched but didn't respond. “He doesn’t talk much anymore.”
Leo slid down the rough stone wall, his leg screaming in protest. He had to look. He pulled up his torn jeans. The two puncture wounds were no longer just bleeding. The flesh around them was swollen and puffy, an angry purple color that spread in a web across his skin. The wounds themselves were dark, almost black, and oozing a thin, yellowish fluid. It looked wrong. Unnatural.
“What happened to your leg?” Sarah asked, her old eyes fixing on the injury.
“Something bit me,” Leo said, his throat dry. “In the tunnel.”
Sarah’s expression changed. A flicker of something, maybe fear, maybe recognition, crossed her face. “You saw it too, then.”
“Saw what?” Mark asked, kneeling beside Leo to inspect the wound. His face went pale. “Leo, this looks bad. Really bad.”
“It’s the asset,” Sarah said quietly, as if the word itself was dangerous. “That’s what they call it. We saw it too. Me and Ben. We were cutting through the old train yards two nights ago. It was… fast. All teeth and claws.” She shuddered. “They found us right after.”
“What is it?” Mark pressed.
“I don’t know,” she said, shaking her head. “Some kind of animal, I guess. But not like any animal I’ve ever seen. It moves weird. It’s all angles and shadows.”
Ben finally looked up. His eyes were wide and vacant. “It doesn’t have eyes,” he whispered, his voice a cracked ruin. “It doesn’t need them.” He went back to staring at the floor.
An icy dread, colder and deeper than the chill of the room, settled over Leo. He remembered the thing in the tunnel. The scuttling sound. The unnatural speed. The feeling of being hunted in the dark. Ben was right. He hadn’t seen any eyes. Just a shape that was wrong.
“What happens now?” Mark asked Sarah, his voice low.
Sarah gave a bitter, humorless laugh. “They bring food once a day. Water. Then we wait.”
“Wait for what?”
She looked at Mark, her gaze unflinching. “They took a boy away yesterday morning. Another one who saw it. He didn’t come back. They said they were taking him home.” She paused. “They were lying.”
The unsaid words hung in the cold air. They were waiting to be killed. The man in the suit’s voice echoed in Leo’s memory. *They’re too old to forget what they saw.*
“No,” Mark said, standing up. He started pacing the small room, a caged tiger. “No, we’re not just going to wait here. They said they were moving out in an hour. That was… what, half an hour ago? We don’t have time.”
“And do what?” Sarah asked, her voice laced with the bitterness of lost hope. “Did you see the size of those guys? Or the door?”
Mark stopped his pacing. He looked at the heavy wooden door, at the thick iron bolt he’d heard slide home. It was hopeless. He ran his hands through his hair, his bravado crumbling.
Leo felt a surge of something hot and defiant push back against the cold fear. It was his fault they were here. His idea to go into the tunnel. He had to do something.
“The hinges,” Leo said, his voice raspy.
Everyone looked at him.
“What?” Mark asked.
“The hinges on the door,” Leo repeated, pointing. “The lock is new, but the door is old. The hinges are ancient. Look at them. They’re rusted.”
Mark and Sarah moved to the door. The hinges were thick, hand forged straps of iron, but where they met the wooden frame, they were pitted and eaten away by decades of damp. Long, rusted pins held them in place.
“How does that help us?” Sarah asked. “We have nothing to pry them with.”
Mark’s face fell. He remembered the tire iron, left uselessly in the trunk. It was a stupid, tiny mistake that had cost them everything. He slammed his fist against the unyielding stone wall.
“Wait,” Leo said. He reached down and patted his pockets. Nothing. He checked his jacket. His fingers brushed against something hard in the inside pocket. He’d forgotten it was there. He pulled it out. It was a multi tool. The one his dad had given him for his birthday last year. It had a small pair of pliers, a knife, a file, a few screwdriver heads. It was a toy compared to a tire iron.
He showed it to Mark. Mark took it, turning it over in his hand. It was small, but it was steel.
“The pins,” Mark said, his eyes lighting up. He opened the pliers. “If we can get a grip on the heads of the pins, maybe we can work them loose.”
“It’ll make noise,” Sarah warned. “They’re right outside.”
“We have to try,” Mark insisted. He handed the multi tool back to Leo. “Your hands are smaller. You try to get a grip on the bottom one. I’ll keep watch.”
Leo crawled over to the door, his leg sending out blinding pulses of pain with every movement. He ignored it. He had a new focus. He wedged himself into the corner, opened the small pliers, and tried to clamp them onto the head of the lower hinge pin. The metal was smooth and rounded with age. The pliers slipped off. He tried again. Slipped.
“It’s no use,” he grunted, frustrated. “I can’t get a grip.”
Mark looked at the door again, his mind racing. “The file,” he said suddenly. “Use the file. Try to file a flat edge onto the side of the pin head. Just enough for the pliers to bite.”
It was a brilliant idea. Leo folded the pliers back in and opened the small metal file. He reached down and began to saw away at the rusted pin. The sound was horribly loud in the silent room. A high pitched shriek of metal on metal.
*Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.*
Ben looked up from the floor, his eyes wide with terror. “They’ll hear,” he whispered.
“I know,” Mark said, his ear pressed to the door. “Just be quiet.”
From outside, they heard a chair scrape on the concrete. A low murmur of voices.
Leo froze, his heart in his throat.
“Keep going,” Mark urged in a whisper. “They’re just talking. Go.”
Leo gritted his teeth and went back to work, trying to make the strokes quicker and quieter. It felt like an eternity, but after a few minutes, he had managed to wear a small, rough patch onto the side of the pin head.
“Okay, try now,” Mark said.
Leo switched back to the pliers. He carefully positioned them on the filed patch. He squeezed with all his might. The metal bit in. It held.
“I got it,” he breathed.
“Okay, now pull. Wiggle it,” Mark instructed.
Leo pulled. The pin didn’t move. It was seized tight by a century of rust and grime. He put all his weight into it, his knuckles turning white, his body trembling with the effort and the pain from his leg. A low groan came from the hinge. Not loud, but a definite sound of movement.
“It’s moving,” Leo gasped. “A little.”
He wiggled it back and forth, pulling with every ounce of strength he had. Slowly, agonizingly, the pin began to slide upwards. An inch. Then two. He could hear the rust flaking and grinding inside the hinge.
Suddenly, they heard heavy footsteps approaching the door.
“They’re coming!” Sarah hissed, her eyes wide.
Leo gave one last desperate yank. The pin came free in his hand. It was a long, ugly spike of rusted metal.
The sound of the key turning in the lock began.
“The top one!” Mark whispered frantically.
There was no time. The bolt slid back.
“Get ready!” Mark yelled. He grabbed Leo and pulled him away from the door. “Sarah, Ben, get back!”
The door swung inwards. One of the big men stood there, silhouetted against the light from the garage. His face was a mask of bored indifference.
“You two,” he grunted, pointing at Mark and Leo. “Let’s go. The boss is getting impatient.”
Mark didn’t hesitate. He wasn’t going to be taken away like the other boy. He lunged forward, not at the man, but at the door. He threw his entire body weight against it.
The door, now held only by the top hinge, swung wildly on its damaged axis. Instead of just pushing the man, the bottom corner of the heavy wooden slab twisted outwards and slammed into his knee with a sickening crunch.
The man roared in pain and surprise, stumbling backward. He hadn’t been expecting a fight. He had been expecting sheep.
In that split second of chaos, Leo saw his chance. Adrenaline surged through him, a powerful drug that momentarily eclipsed the pain in his leg. He scrambled forward, holding the rusted hinge pin he’d just pulled. It wasn’t a tire iron, but it was heavy and pointed. He swung it like a club, aiming for the man’s other leg.
The pin connected with the man’s shin with a dull thud. It wasn't a disabling blow, but the shock and the pain made the giant stagger, his balance completely gone. He went down hard on the concrete floor, bellowing in rage.
The second man, who had been standing a few feet away, was momentarily stunned. He stared at his partner on the ground, then at the four kids framed in the doorway of the cell.
“Run!” Mark screamed, grabbing Sarah’s arm and pulling her out into the garage.
For a second, Ben was frozen in place, a deer in the headlights. Leo grabbed his shirt and hauled him forward. “Move!”
They burst out of the cell, a chaotic tangle of limbs. The garage was bigger than it looked from the inside. Their stolen car was parked near the center. The massive, sliding metal door they’d heard earlier was on the far side of the room. It was their only way out. Between them and the door stood the second guard, his shock already turning to fury.
To their left, a set of narrow metal stairs led up to a darkened upper level.
The guard started toward them, his hands clenched into fists the size of bricks. The one on the floor was already trying to push himself up, his face purple with rage. They were trapped between two monsters and a locked door.
“The stairs!” Mark yelled, pointing. It wasn't the way out, but it was the only way they could go.
He shoved Sarah toward the staircase and turned to face the advancing guard, buying them a precious second. Leo, hobbling badly but still moving, pushed Ben after her. The garage was a battlefield of concrete and shadows, and they were unarmed, injured, and running out of time. The echo of their frantic footsteps was the only sound besides the enraged roars of their captors.

Mark didn’t wait to see if his gambit had paid off. He spun on his heel, his sneakers squeaking on the oil stained concrete, and sprinted for the stairs. The advancing guard, the one whose fists were still clenched, was momentarily distracted by the sight of Mark, a scrawny kid in a dirty shirt, turning to face him. It was a bluff, a matador’s flick of the cape, and it worked. The man’s momentum carried him a half step past the base of the staircase, his eyes locked on Mark. That half step was all they needed.

Sarah was already halfway up the clanging metal steps, her hands gripping the cold, thin railings. Ben was right behind her, his movements clumsy with fear, his eyes wide and unfocused. Leo, dragging his throbbing leg, was at the bottom, his face a pale sheet of sweat. Mark reached him and practically threw him onto the first step. “Go, Leo, go!”

The metal rang out with each frantic footfall, a discordant alarm bell in the vast, echoing space. The guard on the floor, his face a grotesque mask of fury, was pushing himself up. He put weight on his ruined knee and collapsed again with a guttural scream that was pure, animal rage. The other guard, recovering from his brief confusion, roared and lunged for the stairs. His hand snatched at the air just inches behind Mark’s ankle as Mark scrambled up after Leo.

The stairs were steep and narrow, built for function, not comfort. They felt flimsy, vibrating under the weight of their desperate ascent. From below, the bellowing of the two men blended into a single, terrifying sound. Mark risked a look back. The standing guard was on the stairs now, taking them two at a time. He was huge, a mountain of muscle and anger, and he was closing the distance with terrifying speed. The injured one was crawling, dragging his useless leg, his eyes burning with a murderous light as he clawed his way toward the base of the staircase.

They reached the top, a small metal landing that opened onto a dark, cavernous upper floor. It was a mezzanine, a storage loft that ran the length of the garage. The air was thick with the smell of old wood, dust, and something chemical, like turpentine. The only light came from the dim fixtures of the garage below, casting long, distorted shadows that made the clutter of the loft look like a forest of monsters. Old furniture lay shrouded in white cloths, like sleeping giants. Stacks of cardboard boxes teetered precariously. Metal shelving units were crammed with paint cans, tools, and unidentifiable junk.

“Split up! Hide!” Mark hissed, shoving Sarah toward a towering stack of dusty boxes.

Fist-Guard’s heavy footsteps pounded on the metal stairs, each one a hammer blow to their frayed nerves. Ben, true to form, had frozen again at the top of the stairs, his back pressed against the wall, his breath coming in short, sharp gasps. Leo grabbed his arm. “Ben, come on!” he pleaded, his voice cracking. He pulled Ben away from the landing and behind a decrepit looking wardrobe whose door hung open on one hinge.

The guard reached the top of the stairs. He paused for a second, his massive frame blocking the light from below. He was breathing heavily, not from exertion, but from rage. His head swiveled, his eyes scanning the darkness. “You little rats,” he growled, the sound low and menacing. “You are going to regret this. You are going to regret being born.”

He took a step onto the wooden floor of the loft, which groaned in protest under his weight. He was being cautious now. The element of surprise was gone. He moved slowly, deliberately, a predator entering a new hunting ground.

From their hiding place behind the boxes, Sarah was trembling so hard Mark could feel the vibrations through the floor. He put a hand on her shoulder, a gesture meant to be reassuring, but his own hand was shaking. He peered through a gap between two boxes. The guard was moving toward the center of the room, his silhouette a hulking black shape against the light from below.

Leo, crouched with Ben behind the wardrobe, felt a fresh wave of agony shoot up his leg. He bit his lip to keep from crying out, the coppery taste of blood filling his mouth. The rusted hinge pin was still clutched in his hand. It felt pitifully small. He looked around their hiding spot. The wardrobe was empty except for a thick layer of dust and a few cobwebs. His eyes scanned the floor, searching for anything, anything at all. His gaze fell on a length of heavy, rust-pocked chain coiled near the wall, half hidden under a grimy tarp.

Meanwhile, the guard on the floor below had finally managed to drag himself to the base of the stairs. He couldn’t climb, not with his knee shattered, but he could shout. “They’re up there, Gus! In the junk! Don’t let them get away!”

The guard in the loft, Gus, grunted in acknowledgement. “I hear ‘em,” he muttered to himself. He kicked an empty paint can, sending it clattering across the floor. The noise made Ben jump, and he let out a tiny, choked sob.

It was all Gus needed.

His head snapped in the direction of the sound. “There you are.” He started moving toward the wardrobe, his steps slow and measured.

Mark’s mind raced. They were about to be found. They would be cornered, and it would be over. He had to do something. His eyes darted around their hiding spot. The boxes were stacked high, almost to the ceiling. They were old, the cardboard soft and damp in places. But they were heavy. Filled with what, he couldn’t guess. Old files, maybe. Machine parts. Books. It didn’t matter. All that mattered was their weight.

“Sarah,” he whispered, his voice barely audible. “Help me push.”

She looked at him, her eyes wide with incomprehension, then followed his gaze to the top of the stack of boxes. Understanding dawned. She nodded, her fear momentarily replaced by a flicker of desperate resolve.

Leo saw what they were planning. He also saw Gus getting closer and closer to his and Ben’s position. He had to buy them time. He looked at Ben, who was practically catatonic, his eyes screwed shut. “Ben,” Leo whispered urgently. “I need you to be quiet. Just for a second.” He didn’t wait for an answer. He reached for the heavy chain. It was stiff with rust and disuse, but he managed to get a grip on one end. It was heavier than he expected. He dragged it silently out from under the tarp.

Gus was ten feet from the wardrobe. He reached out a hand to shove it aside.

“Hey!”

The shout came from Mark’s direction. Gus paused, turning his head. Mark was standing up, partially visible behind the tower of boxes. He was holding a small, empty can.

“Over here, you idiot,” Mark yelled, his voice cracking with a mixture of fear and bravado. He hurled the can. It flew past Gus’s head and clattered against the far wall.

It was a stupid, childish gesture, but it was also an insult. Gus’s face contorted. The slow, methodical predator was gone, replaced by the raging bull. “You,” he snarled, and changed direction, stalking toward Mark and Sarah’s hiding place.

“Now!” Mark grunted. He and Sarah put their shoulders against the teetering column of cardboard and pushed with all their might. For a second, nothing happened. The stack was too heavy, too solid. Gus was closing in. “Push!” Mark yelled, his muscles straining.

From behind the wardrobe, Leo saw his chance. Gus was distracted, his back partially turned. Leo swung the heavy chain, not as a whip, but as a pendulum. He let it go. It arced through the air, a dark, rusty blur, and wrapped itself around Gus’s ankles.

Gus wasn't expecting it. The sudden, binding weight around his legs threw him completely off balance. He stumbled, letting out a grunt of surprise. He looked down to see the chain tangled around his boots. It was this moment of distraction, this slight shift in his balance, that gave Mark and Sarah the leverage they needed.

The tower of boxes groaned. It tilted, slowly at first, then with gathering speed. Gus looked up, his eyes widening in genuine alarm. He tried to step back, but his feet were hobbled by the chain. He was trapped.

With a final, desperate shove from Mark and Sarah, the entire column went over. It fell like a felled tree, a cascade of cardboard and unknown, heavy contents. The crash was monumental, a deafening explosion of sound that shook the entire loft. A thick cloud of ancient dust erupted, filling the air and choking them. Boxes burst open, spilling their contents. Yellowed papers, greasy machine parts, and heavy ledgers rained down onto the floor.

The tower collapsed right where Gus had been standing. He roared as it hit him, a sound of pain and fury that was abruptly cut off. He wasn't completely buried, but a half dozen of the heaviest boxes had slammed into his chest and legs, pinning him to the floor. One of his arms was trapped beneath a particularly large crate. He was down. He was alive, but he was down.

For a moment, there was silence, broken only by the sound of their own ragged breathing and the settling of dust. The air was thick and gray.

“Did we… did we get him?” Sarah coughed, her voice raspy.

Mark peered through the haze. Gus was struggling, trying to push the weight off himself, but he was pinned. His free hand clawed at a box, but he couldn’t get any leverage.

Downstairs, the injured guard fell silent. Then a new voice cut through the air. It wasn’t a roar or a shout. It was calm, cold, and edged with irritation.

“What in God’s name is going on up there?”

It was the boss.

The voice seemed to sober them instantly. The temporary victory felt hollow. They had taken down the foot soldiers, but now the general was entering the field.

“We have to go,” Leo said, pulling himself up. He gestured with the hinge pin toward the far end of the loft. “There has to be another way out. A window. Something.”

They scrambled out from their hiding places, moving through the debris field they had created. The dust was starting to settle. Gus was groaning on the floor, swearing viciously.

“The window!” Sarah pointed.

At the far end of the loft, high up on the wall, was a large, grimy square of darkness. A window. It was boarded over with thick planks of wood, nailed crudely into the frame.

“Get me over there,” Mark said, already moving. They clambered over piles of junk, their feet slipping on scattered papers. They reached the wall beneath the window. It was at least ten feet off the ground.

“How do we get up?” Ben asked, his voice trembling again. The sound of the boss’s voice had terrified him.

“Here!” Leo said, pointing at one of the metal shelving units that hadn't been knocked over. It was tall and relatively sturdy. “We can climb this.”

They dragged the heavy unit across the floor, the metal legs scraping horribly against the wood. The noise was answered by a shout from below. “I’m coming up. If you’ve hurt him, I will peel the skin from your bones.” The boss’s voice was still unnervingly calm, a surgeon describing a procedure.

They shoved the shelving unit against the wall. “Go,” Mark said to Sarah. “See if you can get those boards off.”

Sarah didn’t hesitate. She climbed the shelves like a ladder, her small frame finding easy handholds. She reached the top and balanced precariously, pulling at the first wooden plank. It was nailed in tight. “I can’t,” she gasped, her fingers raw. “It won’t move.”

Leo was already climbing up after her, the hinge pin in his teeth. He handed it to her. “Use this. Pry it.”

Mark stayed on the ground, his eyes fixed on the top of the stairs. Ben stood beside him, wringing his hands. “What if he has a gun?” Ben whispered.

The thought sent a spear of ice through Mark’s chest. They hadn’t considered that. The guards had relied on their size. The boss might be different.

At the top of the shelves, Sarah jammed the pointed end of the hinge pin into the gap between the board and the window frame. She put her weight on it. The old wood groaned. A nail screeched as it was slowly pulled free.

The first footstep sounded on the metal stairs. Not the heavy, pounding tread of Gus, but a lighter, more deliberate step. The boss was coming.

“Faster!” Mark urged.

Leo had climbed up to join Sarah. He added his weight to the hinge pin. With a loud crack, the first board splintered and pulled away from the frame. Light, or what passed for it in the dark alley outside, filtered in. It was a grey, watery twilight.

Another step on the stairs. He was halfway up.

“Ben, climb!” Mark ordered. “Get up there now!”

Ben scrambled onto the shelves, his fear giving him a clumsy agility. Mark was right behind him. Below, the figure of the boss reached the top of the stairs. He was not a giant like his men. He was of average height, wearing a dark suit that looked strangely out of place in the grimy garage. His hair was neat. In one hand, he held a sleek, black pistol.

He surveyed the scene with a look of utter disgust. His eyes took in Gus, pinned and groaning under the boxes. Then they moved, locking onto the four children clinging to the shelving unit by the far wall.

“Get down from there,” he said. His voice was flat. It was not a request. He raised the pistol, aiming it not at them, but at the shelving unit.

Sarah and Leo, working together, tore the second board free. It clattered to the floor. They could see the window now. It was a single, large pane of glass, filthy and webbed with cracks.

“Break it!” Mark yelled from below them.

The boss took another step forward. “I will not say it again.”

Leo didn’t have to be told twice. He raised the heavy hinge pin and slammed it into the center of the glass. The window didn’t shatter into a thousand pieces like in the movies. A spiderweb of cracks erupted from the point of impact, and a large, jagged hole appeared. Cold, damp air washed over them.

“Go!” Leo shouted, pushing Sarah toward the opening.

The boss fired. The sound was deafening in the enclosed space. It wasn't the boom they expected, but a sharp, vicious crack. The bullet didn't hit them. It hit the shelving unit, just below Mark’s feet. Metal screamed. A rivet popped, and the entire structure swayed violently.

Sarah screamed as she was thrown against the window frame. She scrambled through the hole, cutting her arm on a shard of glass, and disappeared from view.

“Ben, go!” Mark shoved him upward. Ben squeezed through the broken window, his eyes screwed shut.

Now it was just Mark and Leo. Leo was trying to get his leg through the opening, but the awkward angle and the searing pain were making it almost impossible. The boss was advancing, his gun still raised. Gus, from under his pile of boxes, let out another groan.

“Leave him,” the boss said, his voice a low snarl. He was talking to the injured guard downstairs. A moment later, the sound of a side door sliding open echoed from below. The other guard was coming. A pincer.

Mark knew they had seconds. He grabbed Leo by the back of his shirt and his belt. With a surge of adrenaline fueled strength, he lifted and shoved. Leo cried out in pain as his injured leg scraped against the broken glass, but he was through.

Mark was alone on the wobbling shelf. The boss was twenty feet away, his expression unreadable, his gun steady. Mark didn’t think. He just moved. He launched himself at the window, diving headfirst through the jagged opening. Shards of glass sliced at his jacket and his hands, but he was through. He landed in a heap on top of Leo in the narrow, trash-strewn alleyway outside.

The drop had been about twelve feet. They had landed on a pile of overflowing trash bags, which had cushioned their fall but now threatened to suffocate them. They untangled themselves, gasping for air, the stench of rotting garbage filling their lungs. Sarah was leaning against the brick wall, clutching her bleeding arm. Ben was on his hands and knees, hyperventilating.

Above them, the face of the boss appeared in the broken window. He looked down at them, his expression one of cold fury. He didn’t shout. He just watched them.

“Run,” Mark croaked, scrambling to his feet. He grabbed Leo, helping him up. “We have to run.”

They stumbled down the alley, away from the garage, away from the cold eyes watching them from the window. They were out. They were free from the cell, free from the building. But as they limped into the deepening twilight of the unfamiliar city, the sound of the garage’s large metal door beginning to slide open echoed behind them. They were not free from the hunt. It had only just begun.

The sound was a mechanical growl, a predator awakening. It echoed down the narrow canyon of the alley, seeming to come from all directions at once. They ran. They had to run. Mark half dragged, half carried Leo, whose every limping step was a choked gasp of pain. Sarah ran just ahead of them, her uninjured hand clamped over the gash on her arm, her face a pale mask in the gloom. Ben stumbled behind, his breath still coming in ragged, panicked sobs.

The alley ended abruptly, spitting them out onto a street. It was not a main thoroughfare. It was a service road, lined with the backdoors of restaurants and darkened shops. A single, flickering streetlight cast long, distorted shadows that seemed to claw at them. Cars rushed by on a wider, brighter street a block away, their headlights sweeping past like searchlights. The city was a cacophony of distant sirens, honking horns, and the low, omnipresent hum of a million lives they were not a part of. They were ghosts here. Hunted ghosts.

“Which way?” Sarah’s voice was thin, strained.

Mark’s head swiveled, his eyes darting, trying to process the chaos. Left was toward the brighter street, toward more people, more witnesses, but also more exposure. Right led deeper into a warren of industrial buildings and dark corners. A car engine roared, much closer than the others. A pair of headlights turned sharply off the main road, flooding their side street with sudden, blinding white light. It was a dark sedan, the kind that looked official and menacing. It accelerated toward them.

“This way!” Mark yelled, shoving them to the right.

He put his shoulder under Leo’s arm and lifted, taking most of his weight. They hobbled desperately along the sidewalk. Leo’s teeth were gritted, a low moan escaping his lips. The sedan was gaining. Its tires squealed on the pavement. Ben let out a yelp of pure terror and sprinted ahead, a wild, panicked burst of speed that left the others behind. He didn't look back.

“Ben!” Sarah screamed after him, but he was gone, swallowed by the shadows between two warehouses.

The car was almost on them. Mark saw an opening, a narrow gap between a dumpster and the loading dock of a wholesale bakery. The air smelled of sugar and yeast. He shoved Leo toward it. “In here. Now.”

They scrambled into the tight space, pressing themselves against the cold brick. The dumpster was a wall of corrugated metal, reeking of sour dough and old flour. Mark held his breath, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. The sedan screeched to a halt exactly where they had been standing. The passenger door opened. A man got out. It wasn't the boss. It was the other guard, the one from downstairs. He was broad and thick, a silhouette against his own headlights. He scanned the street, his head turning slowly. He looked toward the spot where Ben had vanished. Then he looked toward the dumpster.

Mark pushed Leo and Sarah deeper into the recess, flattening himself against the wall. He could see the guard’s breath fogging in the cold air. The guard took a step toward them. Then he stopped. His radio crackled, a burst of static and a low, calm voice. The boss’s voice. The guard spoke into his shoulder. “They split. One ran east. The others… I lost them.”

There was a pause. The radio crackled again. Mark couldn’t make out the words, but the tone was clipped, authoritative.

“Right,” the guard said. He got back in the car. The sedan executed a tight three point turn and sped off in the direction Ben had run.

For a long moment, nobody moved. The only sounds were the distant city and their own harsh breathing. The red taillights of the sedan disappeared around a corner.

“They’re gone,” Sarah whispered, her voice trembling.

Mark let out a breath he didn’t realize he’d been holding. His muscles ached with tension. He slid down the brick wall, his legs suddenly weak. Leo was already on the ground, his back against the dumpster, his injured leg stretched out in front of him. In the dim light, his pant leg was a dark, wet mass.

“Ben,” Sarah said, her voice hollow. “He just ran.”

“He panicked,” Mark said, rubbing his face with his hands. His palms were scraped raw from the window. “They went after him.” A cold knot of guilt tightened in his stomach. They had used Ben as a decoy, unintentionally or not.

“Will they…?” Sarah couldn’t finish the sentence.

“They want what we have,” Mark said, his voice flat. “They probably won’t kill him. Not until they know where we are.” He reached into his jacket, his fingers fumbling, and touched the hard, rectangular outline of the microcassette tape in his inner pocket. It was still there. This whole nightmare was about this tiny piece of plastic and magnetic tape.

He looked at Leo. “How bad is it?”

Leo shook his head, his face slick with sweat. “Bad, Mark. It’s bad. I think… I think there’s still glass in it.”

Mark crawled over to him. Sarah was already there, tearing a long strip from the bottom of her own shirt with her teeth. Her arm was still bleeding sluggishly, a dark stain spreading across her sleeve.

“Let me see,” Mark said gently.

He carefully tore the shredded denim of Leo’s jeans wider. The wound was ugly, a deep, ragged gash across his shin. It was bleeding freely, but the most alarming part was the unnatural angle of the leg just below the knee. It wasn’t just a cut.

“It’s broken, Leo,” Mark said, his voice grim. “The fall. When we landed.”

Leo closed his eyes, his head falling back against the metal. “I knew it. I felt it snap.”

Sarah handed Mark the strip of cloth. “We have to stop the bleeding.”

“And we need to splint it,” Mark added, his mind racing. He looked around their small, filthy sanctuary. Nothing. Brick, metal, concrete. Then his eyes fell on the loading dock. There were discarded wooden pallets stacked against the wall. Some of them were broken.

“Stay here,” he said. He pulled himself up and crept to the edge of the dumpster, peering out onto the empty street. It was quiet for now. He hurried to the loading dock and wrenched two long, flat pieces of wood from a broken pallet. They were splintery and damp, but they were straight and strong. He returned to the others.

“This is going to hurt,” he warned Leo.

Leo just nodded, his jaw tight. “Just do it.”

With Sarah holding the leg as steady as she could, Mark placed the pieces of wood on either side of Leo’s shin. He took off his own belt and used it to bind the top of the makeshift splint. It wasn’t enough.

“Sarah, your belt,” he said.

She unbuckled it without a word and handed it to him. He used it to secure the bottom. It was a crude, clumsy fix, but it would hold the bone in place. Leo hadn't made a sound during the process, but Mark could see the tears tracking through the grime on his face.

Now they had to deal with the bleeding. Sarah’s strip of cloth was quickly soaked through.

“We need pressure. And something clean,” Sarah said, her voice gaining a sliver of medical authority. “We need a pharmacy. Something.”

“We can’t risk it,” Mark said. “We have no money. We look like… this.” He gestured at their torn, dirty clothes, their scraped faces. They looked like vagrants, victims of a mugging. They would attract attention, questions they couldn’t answer.

He looked at Sarah’s own wound. “What about your arm?”

“It’s just a cut. It’s stopped bleeding mostly,” she said, dismissing it. “Leo is the priority. He can’t walk on that. We can’t run.”

She was right. The calculus of their survival had changed. They were no longer a group of four fugitives. They were two people trying to help a man who couldn't move, with one of their party missing and being actively hunted.

“What about Ben?” Sarah asked again, her voice softer this time.

“We can’t go back for him,” Mark said, hating the words as he said them. “We don’t even know where to look. And they’ll be waiting.”

They fell into a heavy silence, a small island of misery in the heart of a city that didn't know or care they existed. The adrenaline had completely worn off, replaced by a deep, throbbing exhaustion. Mark’s hands stung. His back ached from the fall. Every muscle protested.

“So what do we do?” Sarah finally asked. “We can’t stay here all night. We’ll freeze. Or they’ll come back and check again.”

Mark looked down the dark street. They were trapped. They couldn’t move fast, and they couldn’t stay put. He felt a wave of despair wash over him, so powerful it almost buckled him. He was the one who had gotten them into this. His investigation, his source, his stupid, relentless need to know the truth. And now Leo’s leg was broken, Sarah was hurt, and Ben was a lamb for the slaughter.

He pushed the thought away. Guilt was a luxury he couldn't afford. He needed a plan. Not a good plan, just a plan. Any plan.

“We need to get off the street,” he said, thinking aloud. “We need to get underground.”

“Underground?” Sarah asked.

“The subway,” Mark said. “It’s warm. It’s crowded. Anonymity. And it’s a way to move across the city without being seen by a car. If we can get on a train, we can be miles from here in minutes.”

“How do we get Leo there?” Sarah gestured to the splinted leg. “And how do we get on a train with no money?”

“I’ll carry him,” Mark said, though he wasn’t sure he had the strength. “We’ll be two guys helping a drunk friend. People see that all the time. They look away. As for the money… we’ll jump the turnstile. We’ll deal with that when we get there.”

It was a flimsy, desperate plan, riddled with holes. But it was better than sitting here waiting to be found.

“Okay,” Sarah said, her voice resolute. “Okay. The subway. Where’s the nearest station?”

Mark had no idea. He’d only been in this city twice, and never in this part of it. He looked up at the buildings, trying to get his bearings from street signs he couldn't quite read in the dark. He saw the faint, pulsating glow of neon signs a few blocks away, in the direction of the main street. That was their best bet.

“That way,” he said, pointing. “It’s a few blocks. We’ll go slow. Stay in the shadows.”

Getting Leo to his feet was an ordeal. He cried out, a sharp, ragged sound that he bit off immediately, as Mark and Sarah hoisted him up. He leaned heavily on Mark, his arm slung over Mark’s shoulders, his good foot shuffling, the splinted leg held stiffly off the ground. It was agonizingly slow. Every step was a fresh wave of pain for Leo and a new test of Mark’s dwindling strength.

They moved like a single, wounded creature, hugging the walls of the buildings. The street was still deserted. They crossed the first intersection, their heads on a constant swivel. The neon glow grew brighter. They could hear more traffic, the faint strains of music from a distant bar. They were getting closer to civilization. Mark wasn't sure if that was a good thing or a bad thing.

They reached the corner of the main street. It was a river of light and motion. People bustled along the sidewalks, bundled in coats, their faces illuminated by shop windows and flashing signs. Cars and buses streamed past. It was overwhelming. They were exposed here. Utterly exposed.

Mark pulled them back into the shadows of an alley entrance. “We need to find a subway sign.”

Sarah, her eyes sharper, scanned the street. “There,” she said, pointing. A block down, on the other side of the street, was the familiar green and white globe of a subway entrance. A staircase leading down into the earth. It looked like the entrance to a tomb, and the entrance to salvation.

“Okay,” Mark breathed. “We have to cross the street. We’ll do it at the crosswalk. We’ll just be part of the crowd.”

He took a deep breath. Leo leaned on him, his weight a deadening anchor. “I don’t know, Mark. I don’t know if I can make it.”

“You can,” Mark said, his voice harder than he intended. “You have to.”

They waited for the light to change. The small white figure of the crosswalk sign lit up. “Now,” Mark said.

They stepped out into the street, a slow, limping trio surrounded by a fast moving river of strangers. People flowed around them, a few casting curious glances, but most were lost in their own worlds, staring at their phones, talking to their friends. To them, Mark and Leo were just an inconvenience, a slow spot in the current. It was the perfect camouflage.

They reached the other side. Only a hundred more feet to the station entrance. They were so close. Mark could practically feel the warm, stale air of the subway on his face.

They shuffled past a brightly lit electronics store. A wall of televisions was visible through the window, all tuned to the local news. Mark glanced at them, and his blood ran cold.

On every screen was a picture of Ben. It was a driver’s license photo, grainy and washed out. He was smiling awkwardly. Beneath it, the headline read: “Manhunt Underway for Questioning in Downtown Burglary.”

Sarah saw it at the same time. She stopped, her hand flying to her mouth. “Oh god.”

They hadn’t just made Ben a decoy. They had made him a criminal. The boss wasn't just using brute force. He was using the system. He had called the police, fed them a story, and turned the entire city into his personal hunting ground. Now, any cop, any security guard, anyone who saw the news would be looking for Ben. And by extension, for them.

“Keep moving,” Mark hissed, pulling Sarah along. “Don’t look. Keep moving.”

They reached the subway entrance. The stairs seemed to descend into blackness. They stumbled down, one step at a time, Leo’s splinted leg bumping awkwardly against the railing. The air grew warmer, thick with the smells of ozone, sweat, and roasted peanuts. The noise of an approaching train rumbled up from below.

They emerged onto a mezzanine level. The turnstiles stood before them like a row of metal guards. Beyond them, another set of stairs led down to the platforms. A lone transit officer stood by the token booth, looking bored.

“Okay,” Mark whispered, his heart pounding. “We wait for him to be distracted. When I say go, we go. Sarah, you first. Then me and Leo.”

They huddled near a pillar, trying to look casual, invisible. It was impossible. Leo was pale and sweating, Mark was covered in dust and grime, and Sarah was clutching a bloody, makeshift bandage. They were a beacon of desperation. The transit officer glanced over at them, his eyes lingering for a second before moving on. He didn't seem concerned.

A train roared into the station below, the sound echoing up to them. A surge of people came up the stairs from the platform, heading for the exit. This was their chance.

“Now,” Mark said.

Sarah darted forward. She didn’t vault the turnstile. She just pushed through the rotating bars with a frantic burst of strength. The machine beeped in protest, but she was through. Mark followed, half lifting, half dragging Leo. He pushed Leo through first. The metal bars banged hard against Leo's good leg. He cried out. Mark shoved his way through right after him, the momentum carrying them both forward.

“Hey!” the officer shouted from the booth. “Hey, you two!”

He was starting to come out from behind the counter. They didn't wait. They scrambled down the next flight of stairs, a clumsy, falling descent. They reached the platform just as the doors on a waiting train were beginning to slide shut.

“Hold the door!” Mark yelled.

A man in a suit near the door stuck his hand out, stopping it. Mark shoved Leo inside, tumbling in after him with Sarah right behind. The doors closed with a pneumatic hiss, shutting out the officer's shouts. The train lurched forward, plunging them into the darkness of the tunnel.

They had made it. They collapsed onto the hard plastic seats, a tangled heap of limbs and ragged breaths. The other passengers in the car stared, a mixture of fear and disgust on their faces, before pointedly looking away. Mark didn't care. They were moving. They were safe, if only for a few minutes.

He looked at Leo, who was slumped against the window, his eyes closed, his face a deathly white. He looked at Sarah, who was staring at her own reflection in the dark glass, at the stranger she had become. He looked down at his own trembling, filthy hands.

The train rattled and swayed, a cocoon of light and noise hurtling through the dark. For the first time since they’d burst through that window, the hunt wasn’t right at their heels. They had a moment. A moment to breathe. A moment to think.

“Where do we go?” Sarah asked softly, her voice barely audible over the screech of the wheels.

Mark looked at the subway map on the wall above the windows. It was a spiderweb of colored lines, a diagram of a city he didn't know. To him, it wasn’t a map of places. It was a map of possibilities, of escape routes.

“As far as this train will take us,” he said. “End of the line. Someplace quiet. Someplace they won’t think to look.”

The train pulled into the next station. The doors slid open. Most of the passengers in their car got off. A few new ones got on. As the doors began to close, Mark’s eyes scanned the platform, a reflexive, paranoid habit. And then he saw him.

It was the guard from the sedan. He was standing near a pillar, not looking at their train, but scanning the length of the platform in the other direction. He was methodical. He was checking every station, looking for them. He hadn't seen them. Not yet.

The doors slid shut. The train began to move again. Mark ducked his head down, pulling Leo with him. “Get down,” he whispered harshly to Sarah.

The guard’s head turned as the train pulled away. For a fraction of a second, his eyes met Mark’s through the window. There was no surprise in his expression. Only a cold, flat certainty. He raised the radio to his mouth as the train receded into the tunnel.

He knew. They were on the train. He knew which line they were on, and which direction they were heading. The subway wasn't a sanctuary. It was a trap. A long, linear trap with a limited number of exits, and the hunters knew exactly where they were going.

The certainty in the guard’s eyes was a physical blow. It was worse than a gunshot, worse than a fist. It was the calm, patient look of a predator that knows its prey is already cornered. The radio at his lips was not a call for a search. It was a call for a kill box. Mark’s mind raced, faster than the train itself. Every station ahead of them would be an ambush. The doors would slide open not to a bustling platform of commuters, but to a line of men with guns. Men like the ones who had torn their lives apart. Men who would not hesitate to open fire in a crowded subway car.

The other passengers were a liability. They were shields, yes, but they were also witnesses. They were collateral damage waiting to happen. The hunters wouldn't care.

“He saw us,” Mark breathed, his voice a low, guttural rasp. He didn’t have to look at Sarah. He could feel her go rigid beside him.

“What? Who?” she asked, her whisper sharp with a new, immediate terror.

“The guard. From the car. He saw us.”

He felt Leo stir weakly beside him. The boy’s eyes fluttered open, unfocused and cloudy with pain. “Mark?” he mumbled, his voice thin as a thread. “Cold.”

“I know, Leo. Just hold on.” Mark pulled the thin, dirty fabric of his own jacket tighter around the boy. It was a useless gesture. The cold Leo felt wasn’t from the air in the car. It was a deep, internal winter, the body shutting itself down.

Sarah’s eyes darted to the subway map, then back to Mark. The flicker of hope she’d allowed herself was extinguished, replaced by the grim reality of their situation. “So what do we do? We can’t get off. We can’t stay on.”

“No,” Mark said, his gaze fixed on the swaying metal pole in front of him. “We can’t play by their rules.” He looked at the map again, his brain not processing the names of the stations. He was looking at the spaces between them. The black lines that represented the tunnels. The gaps.

The train screamed into the next station, its brakes groaning. The lights of the platform flooded the car, stark and unforgiving. Mark kept his head down, shielding Leo with his body, forcing Sarah to crouch below the window line. He risked a glance. He didn't see the guard from before, but he saw two other men. They wore plain clothes, unremarkable jackets and slacks, but they stood with a stillness that ordinary commuters lacked. They weren’t waiting for a train. They were watching the doors. Their eyes swept the interior of each car as it slowed, methodical and emotionless.

The doors slid open with a chime. Nobody in their car got on or off. The two men on the platform exchanged a look. One of them spoke into his collar. They were reporting. Car by car. The doors slid shut. The train lurched forward, back into the comforting, deceptive darkness.

They had passed one checkpoint. How many more would there be?

“They’ll stop the train soon,” Sarah said, her voice shaking. “They’ll stop it in a tunnel and swarm us.”

“No,” Mark countered, his own idea solidifying from a desperate spark into a concrete, terrifying plan. “They won’t. They want to control the situation. An open station is controllable. A dark tunnel with a third rail and panicked passengers isn’t. They’ll wait for us at the next stop. They’ll have more men. They’ll be ready.”

“Then what do we do, Mark?” Her voice was on the edge of breaking. “We have maybe two minutes.”

Mark looked from the door to the small, red handle encased in a plastic box above it. The words were stenciled in white: EMERGENCY BRAKE. Then he looked at the door itself, at the smaller, recessed lever for the emergency door release.

“We don’t get off at the station,” he said, his voice flat. “We get off before it.”

Sarah followed his gaze. Her eyes widened. “In the tunnel? Mark, that’s insane. It’s pitch black. The tracks are electrified. We could be killed.”

“We will be killed if we get off at the next station,” he snapped, the adrenaline making his words sharp. “It’s a chance. It’s the only one we have. They’ll be waiting for the train to arrive. They won’t be expecting us to be walking toward them in the dark.”

He didn't give her time to argue. He didn’t have time. He shifted Leo’s dead weight, trying to prop him up against the seat. “Sarah, I need your help. When I pull that brake, this whole car is going to lurch. People will fall. There will be chaos. That’s our cover. We have to get to that door. We have to get it open, and we have to get him out.” He nodded at Leo.

Sarah looked at Leo’s pale, slack face. The boy was barely conscious, a faint wheeze escaping his lips with every breath. Dragging him through a normal doorway was hard enough. Hauling him out of a train into a dark, treacherous tunnel seemed impossible. But she looked at Mark’s face, at the raw, desperate determination in his eyes, and she nodded. The stranger in the reflection was gone. In her place was a survivor.

“Okay,” she said, her voice small but firm. “Okay. What do I do?”

“Stay with Leo. Keep him ready. As soon as we stop, we move. No hesitation. I’ll handle the brake and the door.”

The train was picking up speed now, rattling along the express track. The darkness outside was absolute. Mark took a deep breath, the foul, recycled air of the subway car filling his lungs. He stood up, steadying himself against the pole. The few other passengers in the car, a woman with a grocery bag and two young men listening to music, glanced at him, then looked away. Just another city crazy.

Mark reached up and smashed his fist against the plastic cover of the emergency brake. It shattered, raining small shards onto the floor. The woman gasped. The two young men pulled their headphones off.

“Hey, man, what are you doing?” one of them asked, starting to get to his feet.

Mark ignored him. He wrapped his hand around the red handle. He looked at Sarah. She had her arms under Leo’s shoulders, trying to lift him. Her face was a mask of terrified concentration. He gave her a sharp nod. Now.

He yanked the handle down with all his weight.

The effect was instantaneous and violent. A deafening screech of metal on metal filled the air, a sound that vibrated through the floor and up into his teeth. The train didn’t slow so much as it convulsed, bucking like a dying animal. The lights flickered, died, then came back on at half power, casting a sickly yellow glow. Mark was thrown against the wall. The two young men tumbled into a heap. The woman screamed as her groceries flew across the aisle, oranges and cans rolling under the seats. An automated voice, tinny and distorted, began to drone from the speakers. “An emergency brake has been activated. Please remain calm. Assistance is on the way.”

Assistance. That was the last thing they needed.

“Now, Sarah!” Mark roared over the din.

He scrambled to the subway door, fumbling for the emergency release lever. It was set deep in a recess, stiff from disuse. He dug his filthy fingers in, pulling with all his might. The metal wouldn’t budge. It was rusted or jammed. Panic, cold and sharp, pierced through his adrenaline. This was it. This was where they failed.

“Help me!” he yelled.

Sarah, dragging Leo, was already there. She wedged her fingers next to his, her knuckles scraping against the metal housing. They pulled together. Their combined grunts of effort were lost in the groaning of the train as it finally shuddered to a complete stop. For a second, there was silence, broken only by the whimpers of the other passengers and the distant, rhythmic dripping of water.

Then, with a painful crack, the lever gave way. A hiss of pneumatic pressure escaped, and the doors slid open a few inches, no longer locked. Mark and Sarah threw their shoulders against them, forcing them apart just enough to squeeze through.

The world outside the door was a black void. Mark peered out. There was a narrow service catwalk, barely a foot wide, running alongside the train. Below it was a dark pit, crisscrossed with rails and cables. He couldn't see the third rail, but he knew it was down there, a silent, lethal serpent.

“Go, Sarah, get him out,” he commanded.

Getting Leo through the gap was a nightmare. He was all dead weight and awkward angles. Sarah grunted with the effort, her arms shaking as she supported his upper body while Mark tried to maneuver his legs. Leo moaned, a low, pained sound that tore at Mark’s heart.

“I’ve almost got him,” Sarah strained.

From the front of the train, Mark heard shouting. The conductor, probably. Or maybe a transit cop who was riding along. They had seconds.

“Just get him onto the catwalk,” Mark urged.

With a final, desperate heave, they tumbled Leo out of the train. He collapsed onto the narrow concrete ledge, dangerously close to the edge. Sarah scrambled out after him, her hands immediately going to shield him, to keep him from rolling into the abyss.

Mark was about to follow when one of the young men from the car grabbed his shoulder. “You can’t just leave! You’re in deep trouble, man!”

Mark didn’t have time to argue. He spun, driving his elbow back into the man’s gut. The man doubled over with a wheezing gasp. Mark didn’t look back. He slipped through the gap in the doors and onto the catwalk. He slammed the heavy doors shut behind him. They didn't lock, but they closed with a heavy thud, sealing them in the darkness.

For a moment, all he could hear was his own ragged breathing and the frantic pounding of his heart. The train’s taillights were two red eyes, shrinking as it began to slowly, automatically, pull away. The automated voice had been right. Assistance had been on its way. The system was resetting. In a minute, the train would be gone, and they would be alone.

The darkness that swallowed them was total. It was a thick, oppressive blackness that felt heavy, like a physical weight. The air was cool and damp, thick with the smell of mold, stagnant water, and the electric tang of ozone. Water dripped from the curved ceiling somewhere nearby, each drop echoing in the vast, silent space. The only light was the faint, green glow of an emergency marker far down the tunnel.

“Mark?” Sarah’s voice was a trembling whisper right beside him. He could feel her shaking.

“I’m here.” He reached out, his hand finding her arm. Her muscles were tense as steel cables. “We need to move. Now. Before they come looking.”

“Where? I can’t see anything.”

“Stay against the wall. Feel your way. Do not, under any circumstances, step off this ledge.”

He knelt beside Leo. The boy was shivering violently, his breathing shallow. Mark felt a surge of despair. What had he done? He had dragged them from a moving trap into a static one. A cold, dark, filthy one. But it was a trap of his own choosing. That had to count for something.

“Leo, can you walk?” Mark asked, knowing the answer.

A weak, incoherent mumble was the only reply.

“We have to carry him,” Sarah said, her voice gaining a sliver of resolve.

“No. We drag him. It’s faster. You take his arms, I’ll take his legs.”

It was slow, grueling work. The catwalk was uneven and slick with a fine layer of grime. Every few feet, a metal bracket jutted out from the wall, threatening to trip them. Mark held Leo’s ankles, walking backward, his free hand skimming the damp, curved wall of the tunnel. He kept his eyes peeled, letting them adjust to the profound dark. He could just make out the double set of tracks and the lethal third rail, a darker line running parallel to them, slightly elevated on ceramic insulators.

They moved toward the faint green light. It seemed impossibly far away. The only sounds were their shuffling feet, Leo’s boots scraping against the concrete, and their own labored breaths, which plumed in the cold air. From somewhere in the darkness ahead, a rat skittered across the tracks, its claws making a dry, scratching sound that made Sarah gasp.

“Keep moving,” Mark said, his voice a low command. “Don’t think about it.”

They had been moving for what felt like an eternity, maybe five minutes, maybe ten. Time had lost all meaning. Then they heard it. A new sound. A low hum, growing steadily louder. A light appeared at the far end of the tunnel, the end they had come from. It grew, splitting into two distinct headlights. A train. Or something else.

“They’re coming,” Sarah breathed, her voice choked with panic. “Mark, they’re on a work train or something.”

Mark squinted into the approaching light. It was moving slowly, methodically. Sweeping the tunnel. “Get down,” he hissed. He pulled Leo flat onto the catwalk, pressing him against the wall. He and Sarah flattened themselves beside him. The ledge was barely wide enough. His shoulder hung out over the drop.

The vehicle approached. It wasn't a full train. It was a small, yellow maintenance cart, the kind used by track workers. There were four men on it. Two in the front, two in the back. Even from a distance, Mark could see the long, dark shapes of rifles in their hands. The cart’s powerful headlights scanned from side to side, illuminating every inch of the grimy tunnel walls.

There was no cover. No place to hide. They were caught. Exposed.

The beam of the headlight swept toward them. It crawled up the opposite wall, across the tracks. Mark closed his eyes, bracing for the light, for the inevitable shout, for the burst of gunfire that would follow. This was it. After all the running, all the fighting. A dead end in a sewer.

The light hit the wall just above their heads. And then, from down the tunnel, behind them, a noise. A loud clatter, followed by a shower of sparks from the tracks. Mark chanced a look. A hundred yards away, a piece of metal debris, maybe something they had kicked loose, had fallen onto the third rail, causing a brilliant, sputtering short circuit.

The attention of the men on the cart was drawn instantly. The headlights swiveled toward the shower of sparks. “What was that?” one of them yelled, his voice echoing in the tunnel.

“Probably just a piece of trash. Keep your eyes open,” another voice, harder, commanded.

The cart slowed, its occupants distracted by the new event. It was the slimmest of chances. A few seconds of inattention.

“Now,” Mark whispered to himself. The faint green light was closer. Not a marker. It was an exit sign. Below it was the dark rectangle of a door. An emergency exit. A way out. It was twenty yards away. An impossible distance.

He couldn't drag Leo. Not fast enough.

“Sarah, stay here. Don’t move, don’t make a sound,” he whispered.

Before she could answer, he was up. He wasn’t running. He was crawling. A low, fast, desperate scramble on his hands and knees, staying below the main thrust of the headlights. The concrete scraped his palms raw. His knees screamed in protest. The cart was still moving slowly toward them. The men were still looking at the fizzling sparks. Ten yards. Five. He reached the door.

It was heavy steel, cold and slick with condensation. There was no handle. Just a wheel, like on a submarine hatch. He put both hands on it and turned. It was frozen with rust. He put his shoulder into it, grunting, his muscles straining. The cart was getting closer. He could hear the hum of its electric motor clearly now. The men were talking again, their voices calm, professional.

The wheel wouldn't move. He was going to fail. They were all going to die here.

He took a step back, aimed a kick at the wheel’s hub, and put every last ounce of his fear and rage into it. The impact jarred his entire leg, but the wheel groaned, scraping, and moved a single inch. He hit it again. It moved another inch. He grabbed it, ignoring the pain in his hands, and threw his entire body weight into turning it. With a final, agonized shriek of tortured metal, the locking pins retracted.

He pulled the heavy door open. It opened inward, revealing a small, square concrete room and a flight of steep metal stairs leading up into blackness.

He scrambled back to Sarah and Leo, his heart hammering against his ribs. The cart was closer now, maybe fifty yards away. The men were looking forward again. Their lights would find them at any second.

“We have to go. Now,” he panted, grabbing Leo under the arms. “The door. I got it open.”

Hope surged through Sarah. Together, they half lifted, half dragged Leo's limp body across the final stretch of catwalk. It felt like moving through water. Every step was an agony of slowness. Leo’s head lolled, his feet dragging.

They reached the doorway. The headlight beams were painting the walls just yards from where they had been hiding.

“Get him up,” Mark gasped.

They hauled Leo through the opening, his body thudding onto the landing. Sarah followed, stumbling on the first step. Mark slipped in last, pulling the heavy steel door shut behind him. He fumbled for the wheel on the inside. He spun it, the locking pins sliding home with a series of heavy, final-sounding clunks.

They were plunged back into absolute, total blackness. Outside, they heard the maintenance cart roll past their position. They heard the men’s voices, oblivious, continuing their sweep down the tunnel. They hadn't been seen.

They had made it.

Mark collapsed against the cold steel of the door, his body screaming with exhaustion and pain. Sarah was a few feet away, her ragged sobs the only sound in the small, dark space. They were safe. For a moment. They were off the tracks, out of the tunnel. But they were in a new, unknown place. A concrete box deep beneath the city. The stairs led up into a darkness as complete and as threatening as the one they had just left. He didn't know where they went. To another tunnel? A maintenance corridor? A sewer? Straight into another trap?

He pushed himself up. The respite was over. “We have to go up,” he said, his voice raw. “We can’t stay here.”

Sarah’s sobbing subsided into shuddering breaths. “I can’t, Mark. I can’t carry him anymore.”

Mark felt for Leo in the dark. The boy’s skin was clammy. His breathing was dangerously faint. He was fading. They were running out of time in a way that had nothing to do with the men hunting them.

“I’ll carry him,” Mark said. It was an insane promise. He was running on fumes. But it was the only thing to say.

He crouched down, positioning himself. He hoisted Leo’s limp form over his shoulder in a fireman’s carry. The weight was immense, a crushing burden. His knees buckled, and he had to brace himself against the wall to stay upright. Every muscle screamed.

“Sarah,” he grunted. “I need you in front of me. On the stairs. Tell me what you feel. Tell me what you see.”

He felt her move past him, her hand brushing his arm. He heard her tentative footsteps on the first metal step. It rang out in the confined space.

“It’s just stairs,” she said, her voice a disembodied echo from the darkness above. “They go straight up. They’re… wet.”

One step at a time. That’s all he could manage. He put one foot on the first step, testing his balance with Leo’s dead weight threatening to pull him over. He began to climb, his left hand sliding up the cold, slimy concrete wall for support. Each step was a monumental effort, a new peak of pain and exhaustion. Up into the dark. Away from the hunters. Deeper into the belly of the beast.

The metallic ring of Sarah’s footstep was followed by the wet scrape of Mark’s boot. Then another ring, another scrape. The rhythm became a form of torture, a two beat measure of their slow, agonizing ascent. Ring. Scrape. Ring. Scrape. The darkness was a physical presence, pressing in, stealing air. The only relief was the slimy wall under his left hand and the sound of Sarah’s breathing somewhere above him. He focused on that sound, the small, shaky inhalations. It was his only guide, the one thing telling him he was not alone in this vertical grave.

Leo’s weight was a malevolent thing. It was no longer just a boy; it was a sack of lead, a dead thing intent on pulling him back down into the depths. The fireman’s carry, which had seemed like the only option, was a nightmare on the narrow stairs. Leo’s dangling arm kept catching on the rough concrete wall, his head lolling against Mark’s back with a sickening, loose movement. Each step sent a fresh wave of fire through Mark’s thighs and shoulders. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drum against the slow, funereal beat of their climb.

“How many?” he grunted, the words torn from his throat.

“I don’t know,” Sarah’s voice floated down, thin and reedy. “I can’t see the top. It just… keeps going.”

He squeezed his eyes shut for a second, a bead of sweat tracing a path down his temple. He could feel the fine tremors starting in his legs. He wasn’t going to make it. The thought was a cold, smooth stone in his gut. He was going to drop Leo. He was going to fall. They would die here on these stupid, wet stairs.

“Mark?” Sarah’s voice was sharper now, laced with alarm. He had stopped. He hadn’t even realized it. His foot was poised for the next step, but his muscles refused to obey.

“Just a second,” he wheezed. He leaned his forehead against the damp, cold wall. The slime was disgusting, but the cold was a small shock that cleared his head a little. He adjusted Leo’s weight, a futile gesture that only shifted the pain to a new set of screaming muscles.

“We can’t stop,” she said, her voice trembling. “They could come. They could have heard the door.”

“They didn’t,” he said, more to convince himself than her. “They went past. Just… give me a second.”

He took a breath. It felt like sucking air through a wet cloth. He thought of Leo. The faint, shallow breaths he had felt against his neck. The boy was a flickering candle flame, and this climb, this place, was the wind trying to snuff him out. Mark had to be the hand that cupped around it, that protected it. The thought, as desperate as it was, gave him a sliver of something. Not strength. Just will.

He lifted his foot. He placed it on the next step. Scrape.

“I’m moving,” he said.

He heard her let out a breath she had been holding. “Okay. Okay.”

Ring. Scrape. They fell back into the rhythm. He lost track of time. It could have been five minutes or an hour. His world shrank to the circle of his own pain, the sound of his ragged breathing, and the reassuring clang of Sarah’s footsteps leading him onward, upward. He stopped thinking. He just moved. One step. Then the next. His body was a machine, a broken one, but it was still running.

“Wait,” Sarah’s voice was different. A new note in it. Not fear. Not exhaustion. Surprise. “There’s a landing. A door.”

Hope was a dangerous, painful thing. It lanced through his fatigue. He forced himself up another three steps, his boots clanging heavily. His shins bumped into the solid edge of a concrete platform. He had made it. He had reached the top.

“Easy,” he grunted, bending his knees carefully. He tried to lower Leo gently, but there was no strength left for gentleness. The boy’s body slid from his shoulder and landed with a soft, awful thud on the concrete. Mark collapsed beside him, his legs giving out completely. He lay on his back, gasping, staring up into a darkness that was no different from the darkness on the stairs. His body felt like it was dissolving, every cell buzzing with a painful, electric exhaustion.

He heard Sarah moving. A soft scraping sound. The click of a metal handle. “It’s locked,” she said, her voice flat. “Another wheel lock. Like the one downstairs.”

Of course. Mark rolled onto his side, a monumental effort. He felt for the door. His fingers found cold steel, and then the familiar shape of a large, spoked wheel. He gripped it. His hands were slick with sweat and grime. He tried to turn it. It wouldn’t budge. It was rusted tight or seized from disuse.

“I can’t,” he gasped, his voice barely a whisper. “It’s stuck.”

“Try again,” Sarah urged, her voice close now. He felt her hand on his shoulder.

He took a deep breath, held it, and put every last ounce of his remaining energy into the effort. He pulled, his muscles screaming in protest. He felt a grinding, groaning resistance. For a second, nothing happened. Then, with a horrible screech of protesting metal, the wheel moved. Just an inch. He grunted, pulling again. It moved another inch. It was like trying to turn the world on its axis. He kept pulling, his vision going gray at the edges, the sound of his own blood roaring in his ears.

Then Sarah was beside him. “Together,” she said.

Her small hands found a spoke next to his. On the count of three, they both heaved. The screech was louder this time, a piercing shriek of metal on metal that echoed in the tiny space. But the wheel turned. A foot. Then another. They felt the heavy thud of the locking pins retracting. Mark fumbled for a handle, found a recessed lever, and pushed.

The door swung inward with a low groan, spilling not darkness, but a faint, sterile light into their landing. Mark and Sarah flinched, throwing hands up to shield their eyes. It wasn’t bright, but after so long in absolute black, it was blinding. The light was a sickly yellow green, the hum of old fluorescent tubes. It illuminated a long, straight corridor of bare concrete.

Mark scrambled backwards, away from the opening, dragging Leo with him. Sarah pushed the door shut, though there was no way to lock it from this side. They were in. They were in another place.

The air was different here. Not the damp, organic smell of the subway tunnels. This was stale, dry, with a faint, chemical scent. The smell of a place that had been sealed for a very long time. The corridor stretched before them, maybe a hundred yards long. Doors lined one wall, flat steel doors with small, rectangular windows of wire mesh glass. Above them, the long fluorescent fixtures flickered, casting shifting, unreliable shadows. Some of the tubes were dead, creating pools of darkness along the hallway.

“Where are we?” Sarah whispered, her eyes wide as she took in the scene.

“I don’t know,” Mark said, his voice raspy. He was on his knees beside Leo. He put his fingers to the boy’s neck. The pulse was still there, a weak, thready flutter. Too fast. Too faint. He gently pulled back the edge of Leo’s torn shirt. The makeshift bandage they had applied hours ago was completely soaked through with dark blood. The wound was worse than he had feared.

“We need to help him,” Sarah said, her voice cracking. “We need to find something. Anything.”

Mark nodded, pushing himself to his feet. His legs felt like jelly, but he forced them to hold him. “Stay with him. I’ll check the doors.”

He moved down the corridor, trying each door as he came to it. The first was locked. The second, too. He peered through the small, grimy window of the third door. It looked like an office. A metal desk, an overturned chair, papers scattered across the floor as if someone had left in a great hurry. He tried the handle. Locked.

He felt a surge of despair. They had come all this way just to be trapped in a useless hallway. He moved to the fourth door, his hope dwindling. He put his hand on the lever. He pushed down. It moved. With a soft click, the door swung open.

The room inside was small. It wasn't an office. There were two metal cots with thin, stained mattresses. On the far wall was a metal cabinet, its door slightly ajar. A symbol was painted on the cabinet door, a red cross, faded and peeling.

“Sarah,” he called, his voice echoing in the silent corridor. “Over here. I found something.”

She was there in a second, her face pale and drawn in the flickering light. She looked past him into the room, her eyes locking on the cabinet. A shared, unspoken hope passed between them.

Together, they carried Leo into the small room and laid him carefully on one of the cots. The mattress springs groaned in protest. In the better light, Leo looked even worse. His face was a waxy, ashen color, his lips tinged with blue. His breathing was a shallow, rattling sound.

Sarah went straight to the cabinet and pulled the door open. It was a first aid station. An old one. Dust coated everything. There were glass bottles of brown liquid with faded, unreadable labels. Rolls of bandages, yellowed with age. A tarnished set of forceps. It wasn't modern medicine, but it was more than they’d had.

“Okay,” Sarah said, her voice taking on a new, focused tone. She was pushing her fear down, channeling it into action. “Let’s see what we have.”

She began carefully taking things out, examining them under the weak light. Mark watched her for a moment, then turned his attention back to Leo. He had to get the boy’s shirt off, get a clear look at the wound. He gently unbuttoned it. The fabric was stuck to the skin around the injury. He had to peel it back.

The wound was ugly. A deep, jagged puncture on his right side, just above his hip. The flesh around it was inflamed and swollen. Something was still in there. A dark, metallic glint deep in the torn tissue.

“It’s a piece of metal,” Mark said, his stomach churning. “It’s still in him.”

Sarah came over, holding a brown bottle. She unscrewed the cap and sniffed it. “Antiseptic, I think. It smells like iodine.” She looked at the wound, her face hardening. “It has to come out. If we leave it in, he’ll…” she trailed off. They both knew.

“Can you do it?” Mark asked, his voice low.

She looked from the wound to the rusty forceps in her hand, then to Leo’s pale face. She took a shuddering breath. “I don’t have a choice, do I?”

Mark looked around the small, grim room. “We need to clean it. We need more light.” One of the fluorescent tubes in their room was flickering erratically, threatening to die at any moment.

“I’ll hold him,” Mark said. “Just… be quick.”

Sarah nodded, her jaw tight. She went back to the cabinet, finding a small, shallow metal tray. She poured some of the antiseptic into it, the dark liquid fuming slightly. She dropped the forceps in to sterilize them as best she could. She found a roll of gauze that looked relatively clean under its brittle paper wrapping.

Mark positioned himself on the cot, gently holding Leo’s shoulders. The boy was completely limp, unresponsive. Maybe it was a blessing.

Sarah came back with the tray. She placed it on the cot beside Leo. She picked up the forceps. Her hand was shaking.

“You can do this,” Mark said softly.

She took a deep breath, held it, and leaned over Leo. She carefully, painstakingly, probed the wound with the tips of the forceps. Mark watched, his own body tensed, feeling a sympathetic echo of the violation. A fresh bead of dark blood welled up. Sarah’s breath hitched, but she didn’t pull back. She repositioned the forceps, trying to get a grip on the shard of metal.

After a few agonizing seconds, she grunted with effort. “I think I have it.”

She pulled. Slowly, steadily. Leo’s body gave a sudden, violent jerk. His eyes flew open. They were unfocused, wild with pain and delirium. A low, animal moan escaped his lips.

“Hold him!” Sarah cried.

Mark tightened his grip, murmuring nonsense, anything to soothe the boy, though he knew the words were useless. “It’s okay, Leo. It’s almost over. It’s okay.”

With a final, sickening pull, Sarah extracted the object. It was a twisted, jagged piece of steel, about two inches long, covered in blood. She dropped it onto the metal tray with a clatter.

Leo went limp again, his eyes rolling back in his head. His breathing was even more shallow than before.

“Quick,” Mark urged.

Sarah didn’t hesitate. She grabbed a wad of the old gauze, soaked it in the remaining antiseptic, and pressed it directly into the wound to clean it. Then she took the cleanest roll of bandages and began to wrap it tightly around Leo’s torso, creating a pressure dressing to stop the bleeding. Her movements were sure and efficient now, the initial hesitation gone, replaced by a desperate, focused competence.

When she was finished, she sat back, her shoulders slumping. The new bandage was stark white against Leo’s grimy skin. A small, clean island in a sea of filth. For now, the bleeding seemed to have stopped.

They watched him for a long moment. His breathing was still faint, but the rattling sound was gone.

“We bought him some time,” Sarah said, her voice barely audible. “That’s all.”

Mark knew she was right. They had plugged a leak in a sinking ship, but the ship was still sinking. He needed a real hospital. He needed doctors. And they were trapped in some forgotten hole in the ground.

“I’m going to look around,” Mark said, getting to his feet. “Find out where we are. Find a way out.”

“Be careful,” she said, not taking her eyes off Leo.

He left the small infirmary, stepping back into the long, flickering corridor. He felt a profound sense of dislocation. The silent, sterile concrete was a world away from the roaring chaos of the tunnels. It felt like they had passed through a portal into another time.

He moved down the hall, his footsteps the only sound. He passed more locked doors. At the end of the corridor, it turned a corner. He followed it. This new hallway was shorter, ending in a pair of heavy double doors. A sign was stenciled on the wall next to them. The paint was faded, but he could just make it out.

SECTION 7B: CENTRAL PLANNING.

He pushed on the doors. They swung open into a much larger room. It was a command center. A huge map of the city was mounted on the far wall, covering it from floor to ceiling. It was an old map, some of the highways and buildings wrong, but it was undeniably their city. Dotted across the map were red lights, dozens of them. A few were dark. A large table stood in the center of the room, covered in dusty, archaic radio equipment. In the corner, a rack of binders was falling apart, their pages yellow and brittle.

Mark walked toward the huge map, his eyes tracing the familiar grid of streets. This was a bunker. A civil defense shelter. Something built decades ago, during a time of different fears, and then forgotten, paved over, and left to rot. That’s why the hunters in the tunnel hadn’t known about the door. It wasn't on their maps. It belonged to an older, buried world.

His eyes scanned the map, looking for their location. A single, blinking green light pulsed steadily in a section deep below the downtown core. YOU ARE HERE, read a small plaque beneath it.

His gaze followed the lines and schematics radiating out from their position. He saw the subway line they had come from, marked as a newer addition that cut straight through a section of the shelter’s periphery. He saw access shafts, power conduits, and ventilation systems. He saw multiple exits marked on the map. Exit A, Exit B, Exit C. They all led up into what were now major government buildings or public plazas. They would be sealed, alarmed, or guarded. Useless.

But then he saw something else. A thin, faint line, drawn in a different color. It snaked away from their section of the bunker, away from the main exits. It was labeled “Emergency Drainage Conduit.” It connected to a much larger, thicker line labeled “North Branch Interceptor Sewer,” which in turn wound its way for miles before terminating at a point marked “Stormwater Outfall 4.” The outfall looked to be on the industrial outskirts of the city, near the old shipping yards.

It was a path. A filthy, dangerous, and uncertain one, but it was a way out. A way that bypassed the streets, the patrols, the hunters.

He traced the route with his finger. To get to the drainage conduit, they would have to go deeper into the bunker. Through Section 8 and 9. According to the map’s legend, Section 9 was marked with a crosshatched pattern. He checked the key. FLOODED. And the conduit itself had a warning note next to it: “Atmospheric Hazard Possible. Methane.”

It wasn’t a good plan. It was a terrible plan. But it was the only one they had.

He took one last look at the blinking green light, committing the path to memory. He turned and walked back out of the command center, his steps more purposeful now. He had a destination.

He found Sarah just as he had left her, sitting on the edge of the cot, her hand resting on Leo’s forehead.

“His fever is getting worse,” she said without looking up.

Mark knelt beside her. “I found a map,” he said. “I know where we are. And I think I found a way out.”

She finally looked at him, her eyes searching his face. He saw the exhaustion in them, the fear, but also a flicker of the same resilience he had seen when she held the forceps.

He explained it to her quickly. The bunker. The sewers. The outfall. The risks. The flooded section. The methane. He left nothing out.

When he was done, she was quiet for a long time. The only sound was the hum of the light and Leo’s labored breathing.

“It’s miles,” she finally said.

“I know.”

“Through a flooded shelter and a sewer.”

“Yes.”

“With him.” She gestured to Leo’s still form.

“Yes,” Mark said. There was nothing else to say.

She looked from Leo, to Mark, and then toward the open door of their small room, out into the long, uncertain corridor. She nodded slowly.

“Okay,” she said, her voice a low whisper. “Okay. We have to try.”

Mark felt a small, grim sense of relief. The decision was made. The respite, as brief as it had been, was over. He looked at Leo. The boy’s life was a debt he had to repay.

“We’ll need to find something to carry him on,” Mark said, his mind already working on the logistics. “A stretcher. Something with wheels.”

“And water,” Sarah added. “And more light than just your flashlight.”

The tasks grounded them. They had a plan. They had purpose. It was a fragile shield against the overwhelming despair, but it was a shield nonetheless.

“Alright,” Mark said, pushing himself to his feet once more. The pain in his muscles was a dull, constant ache now, a new baseline. “Let’s go find what we need. Then we go deeper.”

They moved with the quiet efficiency of ghosts haunting their own tomb. The bunker was vast, a concrete labyrinth that had been designed to preserve life and had instead become a monument to its abrupt end. Mark led the way, flashlight beam cutting a nervous swath through the oppressive dark. Sarah followed close behind, her own light playing over the walls, searching.

“Medical stations,” Mark said, his voice a low rumble that the corridor seemed to swallow. “Or a maintenance depot. That’s our best bet.”

The air was different here, in the sections they had not yet explored. It was colder, and the faint, sterile scent of the upper levels had given way to a cloying odor of dust and slow, inexorable decay. They passed rows of identical doors, each numbered, each sealed tight. Dormitories, most likely. Lives frozen in time behind sheets of steel. Mark felt a morbid curiosity, a desire to twist a handle and see what lay within, but he suppressed it. They had no time for archeology.

They found what looked like a common area first. Tables and chairs were overturned, as if in a sudden panic. A deck of cards lay scattered across the floor, their faces pale and accusing in the flashlight beams. On a nearby wall, someone had scrawled a message in what looked like charcoal from a fire pit: WHERE IS THE SUN.

Sarah paused, her light lingering on the words. “How long were they down here?” she whispered.

“Too long,” Mark answered, his gaze sweeping the room for anything useful. He saw nothing but despair. He nudged her arm gently. “Come on.”

Two corridors later, they found it. A set of double doors with a red cross painted on them, faded but still clear. The infirmary. One of the doors was slightly ajar. Mark put his shoulder to it and pushed. It scraped open with a sound like grinding teeth, revealing a scene of organized chaos.

Medical cots lined the walls, their thin mattresses stained and torn. IV stands stood like skeletal trees. Cabinets had been flung open, their contents ransacked, glass vials crunching underfoot. But in the center of the room, tucked between two overturned supply carts, was exactly what they needed. A gurney. It was old, with a stained pad and a slightly bent frame, but its wheels looked solid.

“There,” Mark said, a small victory in a war of attrition.

While he checked the gurney’s wheels, making sure they turned freely, Sarah moved through the infirmary with a specific purpose. She scavenged with a practiced eye, her movements deft and sure. She found a half empty box of saline bags, a roll of sterile gauze, and a bottle of antiseptic. She gathered them into a canvas bag she’d found near the door. Then her light fell on something else.

“Mark,” she said. He looked up. She was holding two heavy, rubberized flashlights, the kind used by emergency crews. And next to them, a small cardboard box. “Batteries,” she said, shaking the box. It rattled with promise. “And look.” She reached back into the cabinet and pulled out a headlamp, its strap still coiled neatly. “It feels new.”

He took the headlamp from her, his fingers brushing hers. It was a small thing, a simple tool, but it felt like a priceless artifact. It was freedom for his hands. He strapped it to his forehead and clicked the button. A bright, focused beam of white light shot out, far more powerful than their old flashlights. It cut through the gloom with an authority that was almost startling.

“That’s better,” he said. He could see the exhaustion etched around her eyes, the grime on her cheek, but also the sharp intelligence that never seemed to dull.

They found a small, forgotten storage closet at the back of the infirmary. Inside, miraculously untouched, was a case of bottled water. Twelve sealed one liter bottles. It was a treasure beyond measure. They loaded half of them onto the lower shelf of the gurney, along with the medical supplies.

As they were about to leave, Mark’s new headlamp beam caught the glint of metal in a tipped over locker. It was a pry bar. A long, heavy piece of steel, painted red, with a wicked curve at its end. He hefted it. It felt good in his hands. Solid. A tool for opening things that did not want to be opened. He secured it to the side of the gurney with a strap from the stained mattress.

“Okay,” he said, looking at their haul. A gurney, light, water, medical supplies, a tool. “I think we’re ready.”

They wheeled the gurney back through the silent corridors. The squeak of its wheels was an obscenely loud intrusion, echoing off the concrete and steel. Each protest of the axles felt like a broadcast of their position, a shout into the void. Mark found himself constantly looking over his shoulder, his ears straining against the rhythmic noise.

Leo was just as they had left him, but the flush on his cheeks was deeper, a feverish red against the pale gray of his skin. He mumbled something incoherent, his head tossing on the thin pillow. Sarah placed a cool hand on his forehead, her expression a mixture of tenderness and grim resolve.

“His breathing is shallower,” she said.

“The sooner we move, the better,” Mark replied, his voice tight.

Lifting Leo was a delicate operation. He was limp, a dead weight, and he groaned when they moved him. They settled him onto the gurney, covering him with the thin, rough blanket. Sarah arranged the blanket around him, tucking it in as a mother would, a gesture of fierce protection against the encroaching cold of the bunker.

Mark took the front of the gurney, his hands gripping the push bar. Sarah took the back. The headlamp cast a clear, steady path before them. They left their small room, their temporary sanctuary, and pushed the gurney out into the main corridor, turning not back toward the surface, but deeper into the earth. Toward Section 8.

The journey was slow and taxing. The floors were uneven in places, littered with debris that had to be cleared or navigated around. The gurney’s wheels caught on cracks in the concrete, jarring Leo and sending shudders through Mark’s arms. They fell into a silent rhythm, a team born of necessity. Mark would steer and push, his headlamp illuminating the path. Sarah would guide the back, her flashlight beam sweeping from side to side, a constant, silent patrol of their flanks.

The signs on the wall counted down. Section 5. Section 6. The air grew heavier, thick with the smell of damp and decay, a scent like wet soil and old iron. The silence was deeper here, too. The hum of the emergency lights had long since faded, leaving only the sound of their own efforts: the squeal of the wheels, the scuff of their shoes, the ragged pattern of their breathing.

They reached the bulkhead for Section 7. It was a heavy, circular hatch, like on a submarine. It stood open. Beyond it, the character of the bunker changed again. This was not a residential or medical wing. The corridor was wider, and pipes as thick as a man’s chest ran along the ceiling, weeping a slow, black condensate that smelled of oil and rust.

“Engineering, maybe,” Mark muttered, his voice muffled in the dead air.

They pushed on, the gurney’s wheels splashing through shallow puddles of the foul smelling liquid. The path grew more treacherous. A section of the ceiling had collapsed, forcing them to detour through a series of narrow maintenance tunnels. They had to tilt the gurney almost vertically to get it through the tight spaces, Sarah holding Leo in place while Mark maneuvered the unwieldy frame. The boy whimpered, a thin, pitiful sound that cut Mark to the core.

Finally, they saw the sign, stenciled in faded yellow paint on a massive steel door. SECTION 8. Below it, another sign read: LONG TERM HABITATION. The door was immense, a slab of reinforced concrete and steel that must have weighed tons. It was closed. A manual wheel, thick with rust, was set into its center.

“It’s going to be heavy,” Sarah said, stating the obvious.

“Together,” Mark said.

They positioned themselves on either side of the wheel. It was cold and rough beneath their hands. “On three,” he grunted. “One. Two. Three.”

They threw their combined weight into it. For a moment, nothing happened. The wheel was frozen solid. Mark felt a spike of despair. If they couldn’t get through, their plan was finished. He repositioned his feet, digging his heels into the grimy floor.

“Again,” he gasped. “Harder.”

They pushed, muscles screaming in protest. A groan echoed from within the door’s mechanism, a deep, resonant sound of tortured metal. Then, with an agonizing shriek, the wheel moved. An inch. Then another. It was like turning the world on its axis. Sweat poured down Mark’s face, stinging his eyes. He could hear Sarah’s strained breathing beside him. They worked in a desperate unison, putting every ounce of their dwindling strength into the task.

Slowly, ponderously, the massive door began to slide sideways into the wall, revealing the darkness of Section 8. An inch of blackness became a foot, then a yard. The air that washed over them was different. It was stale, but it held a ghost of something else. Something organic. The smell of old paper, of fabric, of a thousand contained lives.

When the opening was just wide enough for the gurney, they stopped, gasping for breath, their arms trembling. Mark shone his headlamp into the space. It was a wide central nexus, radiating corridors in every direction. It was like a town square, utterly dark and silent. Personal effects were everywhere, but not scattered in panic. They were placed with a strange, almost ceremonial order. Shoes were lined up neatly against a wall. A child’s doll sat propped on a bench, its button eyes staring into the void.

“What happened here?” Sarah whispered, her voice full of awe and dread.

“It’s like they just… vanished,” Mark said.

They pushed the gurney into the nexus. The wheels were quieter on this floor, the silence more profound. It felt deeply wrong to be there, like treading on sacred ground. They had to cross the nexus to reach the corridor on the far side, the one marked with a sign for Section 9.

As they moved past the center of the large room, Mark’s light caught something that made him stop dead. In the middle of the floor, a large circle had been painted. And within the circle, arranged with meticulous care, were dozens of objects. A pair of glasses. A locket. A single, worn leather glove. A dog eared book. It was a memorial. A shrine to the people they had lost, or the world they had lost.

“They knew,” Sarah said, her voice barely audible. “They knew they weren’t getting out.”

The realization settled on Mark with a chilling weight. The people here hadn’t fled in a panic. They had waited. They had organized. And they had died, surrounded by the ghosts of their former lives. This wasn't just a shelter. It was a tomb they had furnished themselves.

He forced himself to look away from the shrine. “The corridor is over there.”

They had almost reached the other side when a sound made them both freeze.

A soft, rhythmic scraping.

It came from one of the radiating corridors to their left. Scrape. Pause. Scrape. Pause. It was slow, deliberate. It was not the sound of the bunker settling. It was the sound of something moving.

Mark killed his headlamp instantly, plunging them into absolute blackness. He held his breath, his heart hammering against his ribs. Sarah stood frozen, her hand gripping the gurney. The only light was the faint, dying glow from her own cheap flashlight, which she quickly extinguished.

The scraping continued, getting closer. Scrape. Pause. Scrape. It was a heavy sound, the sound of something being dragged across the concrete floor. Mark’s hand found the cold steel of the pry bar strapped to the gurney. He silently unhooked it, his knuckles white. The weight of it was a small comfort.

Who else could be down here? What else? After so long. The questions burned in his mind, each one more terrifying than the last. The people of Section 8 were long dead. The hunters stayed on the surface. Whatever was making that noise was something else entirely. Something that belonged to the deep places.

The sound was in the main nexus with them now, moving slowly through the darkness. Mark strained his ears, trying to pinpoint its location. It seemed to be circling the central shrine, moving with a dreadful patience. He could hear a faint, wet sound accompanying the scraping now, a soft, dragging slickness.

Sarah’s hand found his arm in the dark, her grip like a vice. He could feel her trembling. He put a finger to his lips, a useless gesture in the pitch black, but she understood. They couldn't move. They couldn't make a sound. They couldn't even risk breathing too loudly.

Leo chose that moment to cry out, a sharp, fevered gasp that cut through the silence like a shard of glass.

The scraping stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the noise. It was a heavy, listening silence. Mark felt a cold dread wash over him, colder than any water. It knew they were there.

He had to see. He had to know what was hunting them in the dark. Taking a steadying breath, he raised his headlamp, aimed it in the direction of the last sound, and clicked it on.

The beam of pure white light slashed across the nexus and landed on the thing by the shrine. It was not human. It was tall and gaunt, its limbs too long, its skin the color of bone mold. It had no eyes, only a smooth, pale face that tilted toward the light with a birdlike quickness. It was dragging a heavy, rusted piece of metal, a shard of the collapsed ceiling from the other section perhaps, its edge sharpened to a crude blade. Its other hand was gripping the small, button eyed doll from the bench. It held the doll delicately, almost tenderly, before its blank face.

As the light hit it, it dropped the doll. It opened its mouth, a perfect black circle in its white face, and let out a high, clicking shriek that echoed off the walls of the chamber. It was the sound of a thousand insects, of breaking twigs, of pure, alien malice.

Then it started to move toward them. Not running, but lurching forward in a fast, unnatural gait, its long limbs bending at odd angles. It was closing the distance with terrifying speed.

“Go!” Mark screamed, his voice cracking. “Go now!”

He didn't wait for Sarah. He shoved the gurney with all his might, sending it careening toward the corridor for Section 9. Sarah scrambled after it. The creature shrieked again, the sound vibrating in Mark’s bones. He turned to face it, the pry bar held in front of him like a spear. He was no soldier. He was a broken man running on fumes and guilt. But Leo was behind him. Sarah was behind him.

The creature lunged. Mark swung the pry bar in a desperate arc. Steel met something that was not quite bone, not quite flesh, with a sickening, wet crunch. The creature stumbled, its featureless face turning toward him. It didn’t seem to register pain, only surprise. It raised its sharpened shard of metal.

Mark didn't wait for the counterattack. He turned and ran, sprinting for the corridor where Sarah was wrestling with the gurney, trying to get it through the narrower opening. He reached them, grabbed the front of the gurney, and helped her shove it through.

“The door!” Sarah yelled. “Is there a door?”

Mark’s light found it. Another bulkhead, this one smaller, but just as heavy. And emblazoned on it, a stark warning in red: SECTION 9. FLOODED. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

The creature was coming. They could hear its lurching steps, the scrape of its weapon on the floor. It was just seconds behind them.

The wheel on this door was just as rusted as the last one. They had no time for that.

“The pry bar!” Mark yelled, fumbling for it. He jammed the flattened end into the seam of the door, near the locking mechanism. “Help me!”

They both threw their weight against the bar. The metal groaned. The creature’s shriek echoed from the corridor behind them, impossibly close. Mark roared, a raw, primal sound of fury and fear, and put every last bit of his existence into the effort.

With a deafening crack, the locking pins sheared. The heavy door swung inward with a groan, pushed by the pressure of what lay beyond.

A wall of black, stagnant water surged out, knocking them off their feet. It was freezing cold, and it smelled of a grave. The gurney was swept up, Leo crying out as the icy water hit him. Mark grabbed for it, his fingers closing on a metal rail. Sarah surfaced, gasping, her hair plastered to her face.

The creature was at the doorway now, silhouetted against the faint light of the nexus. It paused at the edge of the water, its blank face tilting, considering. It seemed hesitant.

“Close the door!” Sarah screamed, spitting out a mouthful of filthy water.

Mark scrambled for purchase on the slick floor. The water was already up to his waist. He and Sarah fought the surge, pushing the monstrously heavy door back against the flow. The creature just watched them, its head cocked. It made no move to enter the water.

With a final, desperate heave, they slammed the door shut. The water churned around them.

“The wheel!” Mark gasped.

They found it and began to turn. It was easier this time, aided by the water pressure. With a final, heavy clang, the bulkhead sealed. They were in Section 9. They were in the flood.

Plunged into a darkness broken only by their wavering beams, they stood waist deep in freezing, foul water. The sound of their own ragged breaths and the soft lapping of the water against the gurney was all that remained. Behind them, on the other side of a meter of steel and concrete, the creature was silent. For now. They had escaped it. But they had traded one nightmare for another. Ahead of them lay a flooded tomb, and beyond that, a sewer filled with poison gas.

Mark shone his headlamp forward. The beam cut through the blackness, revealing a long, submerged corridor stretching into infinity. Debris floated on the surface like funeral barges. Wires dangled from the ceiling, their tips kissing the water.

He looked at Sarah. Her face was a pale oval in the darkness, her eyes wide. Leo was shivering uncontrollably on the gurney, soaked and terrified.

“Okay,” Mark said, his voice a hoarse whisper that barely carried over the water. “Okay. We go forward.”

They went forward. The word was a promise, a command, a desperate prayer against the suffocating dark. Moving was a unique kind of agony. The water, thick and heavy as liquid concrete, resisted their every motion. Mark took the front of the gurney, his back to the unseen path ahead, while Sarah pushed from behind. Her headlamp bobbed over his shoulder, a second jittery star in their personal constellation of misery. The gurney was a clumsy, half submerged beast. Its wheels, designed for smooth linoleum floors, scraped and caught on the uneven concrete below. Every few feet, one would wedge itself into a crack or against some submerged piece of debris, and they would have to heave and wrench it free, their movements sending sluggish, greasy waves radiating out into the blackness.

The cold was the worst part. It was not a simple chill. It was a deep, invasive cold that had settled into their bones. Mark’s legs were numb, clumsy stilts that he could barely feel. He could only command them to move through sheer force of will. He looked back at Sarah. Her teeth were chattering, a frantic, percussive rhythm in the quiet. Her face, caught in the glow of his own lamp, was drawn and tight with pain.

“How is he?” Mark asked, his voice a strained croak.

Sarah leaned over the gurney, her light dancing across Leo’s still form. He was wrapped in the single thin blanket they had managed to keep, now utterly soaked. It offered no warmth, only a clammy, heavy weight. “He’s not good, Mark. His breathing is shallow. We need to get him warm. We need to get out of this water.”

Her words were a statement of the impossible. The water was all there was. It stretched before them and behind them, a liquid tomb.

“We will,” Mark said, the lie tasting like rust and stagnant water in his mouth. “Just a little further.”

They pushed on. The sounds of their struggle were amplified in the enclosed space. The rhythmic slosh, slosh, slosh of their legs. The grating shriek of the gurney’s wheels. The frantic, wet gasps of their own breathing. And beneath it all, the incessant, chattering drum of Leo’s shivering. It was the sound of a life fading away.

Mark tried to focus on the task, on putting one leaden foot in front of the other. He swept his headlamp beam from side to side, painting the corridor in fleeting strokes of light. The walls were stained with a dark, oily residue up to a high water mark far above their heads. This section had been flooded before. Perhaps it was designed to be flooded. Wires hung from the ceiling in thick, tangled loops. Most were dead, but his light caught a flicker from a junction box ahead, a tiny, intermittent spark that made his stomach clench.

“Sarah, stop,” he said, his voice low.

They came to a halt, the water swirling around them before settling. The sparking was more obvious now. A thick bundle of cables had been torn from the ceiling, and several of the severed ends dangled just inches above the surface of the water. One of them, its copper heart exposed, spat a tiny blue-white arc every few seconds. It sizzled as it kissed the water, a sound like a snake’s hiss.

“We can’t go through that,” Sarah whispered, stating the obvious. The corridor was no wider than three meters. There was no going around.

Mark stared at the deadly wires. His mind raced, sifting through their pathetic inventory of tools. The pry bar. His headlamp. The gurney. Nothing. They were trapped between a sealed door with a monster behind it and a wall of electric death. Despair, cold and sharp, pricked at the edges of his resolve. He could feel it, the urge to just stop, to let the freezing water take him, to let the silence win.

He looked at Leo, whose shivering had almost stopped. That was worse. Much worse. He looked at Sarah, her eyes wide and fixed on the sparking wire, her face a mask of exhausted fear. No. He couldn’t stop.

“The pry bar,” he said again. It was his only answer to everything.

He unhooked it from his belt. It felt heavy, solid, real. “I’m going to try to push them up. Pin them against the ceiling.”

“Mark, no,” Sarah said, her voice sharp. “If you touch the water while you’re holding that, you’re dead. We’re all dead.”

“The bar is coated in rubber on the handle,” he reasoned, though he wasn’t sure how much protection it would actually offer. “And I’ll be careful. I’ll stand on the gurney. Get me higher.”

“It’s too risky.”

“It’s riskier to stay here and freeze,” he shot back, his fear making him harsh. He regretted it instantly. “Look, it’s the only way. Help me get up.”

She hesitated, her light fixed on his face, searching for a confidence he did not feel. Then she nodded, a single, sharp movement. Together, they maneuvered the gurney until it was directly under the lowest point of the dangling cables. Mark handed his pry bar to Sarah and carefully, clumsily, hauled himself onto the gurney, next to Leo. The flimsy mattress squelched under his weight, and the whole structure wobbled precariously.

“Easy,” Sarah cautioned, grabbing a rail to steady it.

Mark lay on his stomach, his chest pressed against the cold, wet blanket covering Leo. He could feel the faint, terrifyingly slow rhythm of the boy’s heart against his own ribs. “Okay,” he said, his voice muffled. “Give it to me.”

Sarah passed him the pry bar. He took it, his grip tight on the rubber handle. He was acutely aware of the metal, of the water just below him, of the fatal connection he was about to attempt to break. He took a deep breath, the foul air filling his lungs. He raised the bar slowly, the flattened end pointing up at the tangled mass of wires. The sparking continued its irregular, deadly rhythm.

He extended his arm, his muscles screaming from the cold and the awkward position. The end of the pry bar touched the bundle of cables. He pushed up. The bundle was heavier than it looked. For a terrifying second, nothing happened. Then, with a groan of stressed insulation, the mass of wires began to rise. He pushed harder, the muscles in his shoulder and back bunching into tight, painful knots. The sparking end danced wildly, spitting and hissing as it swung back and forth, perilously close to the water’s surface.

“Higher, Mark!” Sarah urged.

He grunted, putting all his strength into it. The wires scraped against the concrete ceiling. He angled the bar, trying to find a pipe or a bracket to wedge it against. His light, fixed to his head, illuminated the small, desperate battlefield. He saw a metal conduit running along the ceiling a few inches away. Perfect.

He pushed the bundle of wires toward it, trying to hook the pry bar over the conduit to hold the wires up. The gurney shifted beneath him. He slipped, his hand sliding down the pry bar, his fingers touching the cold, bare steel. The world dissolved into a blinding white flash and a sound like a thunderclap inside his skull.

His body convulsed, a horrific, violent spasm that threw him backward. He landed in the water with a huge splash, the pry bar clattering against the concrete floor. The darkness returned, absolute and total. He was under the water, disoriented, his limbs jerking uncontrollably. The electric fire was gone, but its ghost remained, a phantom current coursing through his nerves. He didn't know which way was up. His lungs burned.

Then hands were on him, grabbing his jacket, pulling. He broke the surface, gasping, choking on filthy water. Sarah was holding him up, her face a pale blur of terror.

“Mark! Mark, are you okay?” she cried.

He couldn't speak. He could only cough and shudder, his entire body humming with the aftershock. His headlamp was gone, swallowed by the black water. He was in Sarah’s beam now, a specimen pinned by the light.

“The wires,” he managed to rasp, looking up.

The bundle had fallen back down. But in the chaos, the sparking wire had swung and hooked itself around another, deader cable. It was now held a good half meter above the water, twitching but safely clear. In his failure, he had accidentally succeeded.

“You did it,” Sarah said, her voice choked with relief. “Oh God, Mark, I thought you were dead.”

“Me too,” he wheezed. He tried to stand, but his legs wouldn’t obey. They were jelly. Sarah helped him, her arm around his waist, her strength surprising him. They stood together in the waist deep water, two small, exhausted figures in a vast and hostile world. The silence was broken only by their ragged breathing and the soft lapping of the water.

And then another sound.

A soft scrape. From somewhere in the water ahead of them.

They both froze. Sarah’s headlamp beam shot forward, cutting a sharp cone through the darkness. It swept back and forth, illuminating the stained walls, the floating debris, the endlessly repeating corridor.

Nothing.

“What was that?” she whispered, her voice trembling.

“Debris,” Mark said, trying to convince himself as much as her. “Something shifting.”

But it hadn’t sounded like debris. It had sounded deliberate. It had sounded like a claw scraping on concrete.

The fear was back, colder and sharper than the water. The creature behind the door was a known quantity. This was different. This was the terror of the unseen. What else lived in this place? What else had the Company locked away in its subterranean prison? What things thrived in the dark, flooded corners of this facility?

“We have to go,” he said, his voice urgent. He found his legs again, forcing them to work. “Now.”

He didn't need to say it twice. They scrambled back to the gurney. Mark felt along the floor for the pry bar, his numb fingers closing around it. A small victory. He hooked it to his belt. He felt for his headlamp, but it was gone, lost to the murky depths. Now they had only one.

They began to move again, faster this time, their caution thrown to the freezing wind. Sarah took the lead, her beam cutting a frantic path ahead of them while Mark pushed the gurney from behind, his eyes scanning the dark water on either side. Every floating oil drum, every submerged crate, became a monster in the periphery. Every time something brushed against his legs, a jolt of pure panic shot through him. He kept imagining long, pale limbs reaching for him from the depths, a blank, eyeless face rising from the black water.

The corridor began to curve gently to the right. The water seemed to get deeper, rising to his chest. The effort of pushing the gurney was monumental. His muscles burned, and a wave of dizziness washed over him, a parting gift from the electric shock.

“I see something,” Sarah said, her voice tight.

Up ahead, at the edge of her light, was a shape. Not a door. It was a platform, a raised section of walkway built against the wall, about ten meters long and rising a meter out of the water. It was dry.

It was a sanctuary.

“Come on,” he grunted, and together they put their last reserves of strength into a final, desperate push. The gurney’s front wheels scraped against the edge of the platform. It was too high.

“Lift!” he yelled.

He got his shoulder under the front of the gurney, and Sarah pushed from behind. With a collective, agonized groan, they heaved the front half of the gurney onto the platform. Then they scrambled up themselves, their numb hands finding purchase on the slick metal grating. They were out of the water.

They collapsed onto the platform, shivering violently, their bodies screaming in protest. The relief was so immense it was painful. For the first time in what felt like an eternity, they were not fighting the cold, relentless drag of the water. Mark lay on his back, staring up at the oppressive darkness of the ceiling, his chest heaving.

Sarah was already at Leo’s side. She tore off the sodden blanket and began checking his vitals, her movements efficient and practiced, despite her shaking hands.

“He’s hypothermic, Mark. Badly,” she said, her voice grim. “His pulse is weak and thready. If we don’t get him warm, he’s not going to make it.”

Mark pushed himself up into a sitting position. The world swayed. His head throbbed. “What can we do? We have nothing.”

Sarah was already stripping off her own soaked outer jacket. “We have body heat. It’s not much, but it’s something. Help me.”

She unzipped her fleece layer underneath, which was damp but not completely saturated. Mark did the same, his fingers clumsy and stiff. His own fleece felt like an icy sponge. They laid their meager offerings over Leo’s thin frame, then huddled close, pressing their own bodies against his, trying to create a pocket of warmth, a tiny island of life in the vast, cold deadness of Section 9.

Leo didn't respond. He was limp, a dead weight between them. Mark put his hand on the boy’s chest. He could feel the faint, fluttering beat of his heart. It was a fragile candle flame in a hurricane.

They stayed like that for a long time, huddled together on the metal platform in the dark. The only light was Sarah’s headlamp, which she had set on the floor, pointing away from them to conserve its power. The only sound was the lapping of the water below and their own chattering teeth.

“What was in the water, Mark?” Sarah asked after a long silence, her voice barely a whisper.

He didn't want to answer. He didn't want to give the fear a name. “I don’t know.”

“It’s still out there, isn’t it?”

He didn’t answer. He just stared out into the darkness beyond the platform, his eyes straining to see what he knew could not be seen. He thought he saw a ripple on the surface, a disturbance where there should be none. A trick of the light. It had to be a trick of the light.

“We can’t stay here,” he said finally, his voice raw. “As soon as we can move, we have to keep going.”

“Going where?” Sarah asked, her voice hollow with despair. “Into a sewer filled with poison gas? How is that better than this?”

“It’s not in the water,” Mark said simply. “It’s the next step. It’s all there is.”

He felt a profound, soul-crushing weariness. Every step forward only led to a new and more elaborate form of hell. They had escaped the creature only to be trapped in a flood. They had escaped the flood only to face electrocution. They had escaped the electricity only to be stalked by something in the depths. And their reward, if they survived all that, was a gas-filled sewer. It was a machine designed to kill them, slowly and methodically.

He closed his eyes. He saw the blank face of the creature from Section 8. He felt the jolt of the electricity. He smelled the grave-stink of the water. He heard Leo’s heart beating its slow, final retreat.

He was so tired.

A metallic clink from below snapped his eyes open.

It came from directly beneath the platform.

Sarah heard it too. She went rigid beside him. She reached over and grabbed her headlamp, her movements swift and silent. She aimed the beam down, through the metal grating of the platform.

The light illuminated the black water below. At first, there was nothing. Then, a shape coalesced out of the murky depths. It was long and pale, like a slug the size of a man. It had no discernible features, no eyes, no mouth that they could see. It was just a writhing tube of slick, white flesh. Several thin, filament-like tendrils drifted from its body, exploring the underside of the platform. One of them had touched a metal support beam. That was the sound they had heard.

As they watched, frozen in horror, a slit opened on the creature’s front end. It was a mouth, but not a mouth. It was a circular, perfectly round orifice lined with rows upon rows of tiny, needle-like teeth, like the inside of a lamprey. The orifice pulsed, contracting and expanding, tasting the water.

It was directly below them. It knew they were there.

Slowly, silently, Mark reached for the pry bar at his belt. His hand closed around the rubber grip. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic prisoner trying to beat its way out. The creature below drifted, its tendrils caressing the metal supports. It was looking for a way up.

They had traded one nightmare for another. And now, the new nightmare had found them.
