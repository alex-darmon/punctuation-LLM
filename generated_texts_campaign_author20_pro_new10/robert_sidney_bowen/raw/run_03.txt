The crash had thrown Leo clear of the cockpit, but he’d landed hard in a patch of shimmering blue dust that immediately began to crawl up his legs with a faint hissing sound. He was thrashing weakly, his hands clawing at the visor of his helmet, when Jake found him. Jake grabbed the front of his suit and yanked him backward with a grunt. The blue dust clung for a moment, then fell away as though it had lost interest. Jake dragged him behind the twisted metal of the stabilizer, his heart pounding like a piston.

"Leo! Are you alright?" Jake shouted, his voice tinny over the suit's comm. He fumbled with the clasps on Leo's helmet and pulled it off.

Leo groaned and shook his head, sending a spray of sweat across Jake’s glove. "My head feels like a drum, Jake! What a landing! I thought we were done for." He squinted at the wreckage of their scout ship. "Good heavens, you saved the ship! Well, most of it."

"Saved it?" Jake laughed, a short, harsh sound. "I pancaked it, Leo! I got cocky trying to dodge that Kraylor patrol fighter. I should have let you take the controls through the asteroid field."

"Nonsense!" Leo retorted, pushing himself into a sitting position. "Any other pilot in the Colonial Fleet would have been vaporized. We’re alive, aren’t we? Not a scratch on us! You did brilliantly, Jake, absolutely brilliantly!"

"Thanks," Jake managed a weak smile. "Pure luck, I tell you. And I was scared silly. Thank goodness their gunners couldn't hit the broad side of a cargo hauler. Now, let's see if we can salvage the emergency beacon and...." Jake stopped talking and froze. A group of figures was scrambling down the rocky ridge above them. They carried long-barreled rifles, and their movements were quick and silent like predators.

The truth struck Jake like a physical blow. Those weren't Colonial Marines. They were locals, prospectors by the look of their patched environmental suits, and they thought Jake and Leo were with the Kraylor Empire that had been harassing their moon for months. Even as the thought formed, one of the figures dropped to a knee and leveled his rifle. The sharp crack of the weapon echoed across the barren landscape, and a chunk of rock exploded just inches from Jake’s boot.

He jumped in front of Leo, who was still trying to get his bearings, and held up his empty hands. "Hold your fire!" he yelled, his voice sounding thin in the alien air. "Don't shoot! We're not Kraylor! We’re Colonial Fleet! For the colonies! Don't shoot!"

The prospectors swarmed down to them, their rifles leveled at Jake's chest. They were a grim looking crew, made tougher by the hard life on a frontier moon. Their faces were chapped and windburned, visible through their own scratched visors. Their suits were scuffed and repaired with synth-leather and metallic tape. Their rifles were the only things about them that gleamed.

A tall man with a graying beard and sergeant’s stripes painted on his shoulder plate stepped forward until the muzzle of his rifle was nearly touching Jake's helmet. "Kraylor spies!" he snarled, his voice a gravelly rasp over the open comm channel. He jabbed his rifle toward the wrecked scout ship. "We saw your ship. You'll get no information from us. We'll...."

"Wait, you've got it all wrong!" Jake pleaded, his mind racing. "We're not spies. He's from Triton, and I'm from Earth. We just escaped a Kraylor prison barge. We have to get to your command post. We have vital intelligence."

The sergeant hesitated, a flicker of doubt in his hard eyes. His men did not look convinced. They growled and shuffled closer, their trigger fingers tense. Jake opened his mouth to say more, but Leo suddenly shoved past him. The smaller boy, who had spent his youth on a dozen frontier worlds, knew how to talk to men like these.

"Listen here, you dust-eaters!" he snapped at them, his voice sharp and commanding. "My friend is telling the truth! We broke out of a Kraylor brig and we have information that could save this whole miserable rock you call a home. Now take us to your leader, on the double! Do we look like the Kraylor? Use your eyes! Of course not! Have you no sense? We risked our necks to get here! Take us to your commanding officer right now. He might even see fit to give you a commendation. Do you understand me? Take us to your CO, immediately!"

Slow, tired grins spread across the faces of the weary prospectors. The sergeant let out a rough chuckle and lowered his rifle. "The little one's got a laser for a tongue," he muttered, nodding his head. "Alright, I don't think you're Kraylor. Not with that much nerve. But you had a close call, my friends. We're not in a trusting mood these days. The Kraylor have seen to that. Come on. I'll take you to the Captain."

"What's the situation?" Leo asked, his tone softening. "Can't you fight them off? Where's the Colonial Fleet?"

The sergeant shrugged, the motion weary and defeated. "The Fleet?" he grunted. "They're a hundred systems away fighting bigger battles. We're on our own out here. The Kraylor send their patrols whenever they like. We have blasters, but they have cruisers. And fighters? Look at the sky! Nothing but Kraylor ships. Yes, it looks bad, little one. But we haven't given up yet. No!" The man shrugged again, then gestured with his head and began trudging back toward the ridge, his rifle held loosely in one hand.

Jake and Leo fell in line with the others. The only sounds were the crunch of their boots on the gravel and the distant, low hum of a Kraylor cruiser in high orbit, a sound like static from a broken transmitter. Jake leaned over to Leo. "Wow, you can really lay it on!" he whispered. "I thought for sure they were going to shoot first and never ask questions."

"Yes," Leo said, his voice flat. "It’s bad, Jake. I wonder if the Fleet even knows what’s happening here."

Jake didn't have an answer. He suddenly felt the weight of the last three days settle on him like a lead blanket. His energy was gone, and his hope was wavering under the crushing reality of their situation. The image of the massive Kraylor prison barge, a city of steel and despair, was burned into his memory. It was like a monstrous shark in an ocean of stars, and they were just two small fish who had managed to slip through its teeth.

At the top of the ridge, the prospectors led them into a narrow canyon that opened into a massive, hollowed-out cavern. It had once been a mine, but now it was a makeshift military base. Men and women in mismatched gear were setting up heavy repeater cannons, checking power conduits, and patching the armor on worn-out cargo haulers.

The sergeant stopped before a woman with short gray hair and captain's bars etched onto her worn chest plate. She was staring at a tactical map projected onto a cave wall. The sergeant saluted, and after a short exchange, the captain turned to face them. Her eyes were exhausted but sharp as diamond shards.

"What's this I hear?" she asked, her voice calm but firm.

Jake let Leo do the talking. The younger boy spoke for several minutes, recounting their capture, their time on the prison barge, and their escape in the stolen scout ship. The Captain listened without a word, her gaze fixed on Leo. When he finished, she pointed a finger at Jake.

"You said you had intelligence. What is it?"

Jake reached into a sealed pocket on his suit and pulled out a small, metallic data crystal. "While we were aboard the barge, sir," he said, "we overheard officers talking. They thought we were just dumb colonists. They were planning something big. A major fleet movement. We managed to copy their primary staging coordinates from a terminal before we escaped. It's all on this crystal."

The Captain took the crystal and stared at it for a long moment. Then she looked from Jake to Leo, and a new light entered her bleak eyes. "I'm sure you know the value of what you're holding," she said. "I'll have our comms officer send this through a tight-beam transmission to Fleet Command immediately. It might be the very thing we need. You are very brave boys."

Leo flushed slightly. "We just want to help stop the Kraylor, Captain," he said.

The Captain's tired lips curved into a faint, grim smile. "I believe you," she said. "And you just might have."

The Captain, whose nameplate read E. Rostova, turned and gestured for them to follow. "Sergeant, see these boys get something to eat. Then find them a place to rest. They've earned it." She strode deeper into the cavern, the data crystal still clutched in her fist.

The Sergeant grunted an affirmative and led them away from the command area, his heavy boots crunching on the dusty floor. "This way. Mess is open twenty four seven. Doesn't mean the food's any good, but it's hot."

They moved through the heart of the makeshift base. The air was thick with the smell of ozone, hot metal, and the damp, mineral scent of the cave itself. Cables as thick as a man's arm snaked across the floor, feeding power to arrays of jury-rigged equipment. A man with a welding torch was sealing a crack in the hull of a large cargo hauler, the bright blue-white light of his work casting long, dancing shadows. People moved with a purpose that was both hurried and weary, their faces smudged with grease and grime, their eyes holding the same exhausted vigilance as their captain's. This was not a spit and polish military operation. This was a nest of survivors, holding on by their fingernails.

The mess hall was a smaller, adjacent cavern, lit by strings of bare lumens that gave everything a sickly yellow cast. A dozen or so people were scattered among long metal tables, eating in near silence from dented trays. The Sergeant pointed them toward a serving line where a tired looking man was ladling a thick, grayish stew into bowls. "Get a tray," the Sergeant said. "Anya will find you when you're done. Show you where to bunk." He gave them a curt nod and disappeared back into the main cavern.

Jake and Leo moved through the line like ghosts. The stew was bland and gritty, but it was hot, and Jake realized he was starving. He hadn't had a real meal since before their capture. He found a spot at the end of a long table, and Leo sat opposite him. For several minutes, they just ate, the rhythmic clinking of their spoons against the metal bowls the only sound between them.

Finally, Leo put his spoon down. "She believed us," he said, his voice a low hum of excitement. "Captain Rostova. She said we were brave."

"She said the intel might be what they need," Jake corrected, not looking up from his bowl. "There's a difference."

"It's the same thing, Jake. We did it. We got the information out. Fleet Command will know what the Kraylor are planning. They'll be able to stop them." Leo's eyes were bright, reflecting the dim lights of the cavern. He was alive with the possibility, the triumph of their small victory.

Jake scraped the last of the stew from his bowl. He felt none of Leo's elation. All he felt was the deep, aching exhaustion in his bones and a cold knot of dread in his stomach. "Maybe," he said. "Or maybe the transmission won't get through. Or maybe it'll be too late. Or maybe the Fleet is too far away to do anything about it. We don't know, Leo."

Leo’s face fell. "Why do you have to be like that? We just pulled off something impossible. We escaped a prison barge, stole a ship, and delivered intel that could save millions of lives. Can't you be happy about that for five minutes?"

"I'm not unhappy," Jake said, his voice quiet. He looked up at Leo, at his friend's youthful, unlined face. He looked so much younger than his seventeen years. "I'm tired. And I'm scared. That Kraylor cruiser is still up there. We're in a cave full of people with scavenged weapons. We aren't safe, Leo. We just traded one cage for a slightly larger one."

The image of the prison barge returned to him, the endless corridors, the cold, indifferent cruelty of the guards, the vacant eyes of the other prisoners. They had escaped, yes. But the feeling of being trapped, of being hunted, hadn't left him. It clung to him like the fine red dust of this forsaken moon.

Before Leo could answer, a woman with a practical haircut and a rifle slung over her shoulder stopped at their table. "You the new arrivals? Jake and Leo?"

They both nodded.

"I'm Anya," she said. "Sergeant told me to find you a place to sleep. Come on."

She led them to a narrow tunnel branching off the main cavern. The air grew cooler here. The tunnel opened into a series of small, darkened alcoves carved directly into the rock. In one of them, two bedrolls were laid out on the flat stone floor.

"This is it," Anya said. "It's not the Fleet barracks, but it's dry. Latrine is at the end of the tunnel. Try to get some sleep. You look like you've been through a grinder." She paused, her expression softening for a fraction of a second. "The Captain was right. What you did… it took guts. We're all glad you made it." With that, she turned and walked away, her footsteps echoing until they faded.

Leo sat down on one of the bedrolls. "See? Even she gets it."

Jake didn't answer. He just unslung his pack and dropped it to the floor. He sat on his own bedroll and leaned his head back against the cold stone wall. He closed his eyes, but sleep wouldn't come. The low frequency hum of the base's generator vibrated through the rock, a constant reminder of where he was. He could hear distant clangs of metal on metal, the occasional bark of a command, the low murmur of voices. This place never slept.

Leo was quiet for a long time. Jake thought he had finally fallen asleep. Then, his voice came out of the darkness, small and uncertain. "Are you mad at me, Jake?"

Jake opened his eyes. He could just make out Leo's silhouette in the gloom. "No. Why would I be mad at you?"

"Because I'm not as scared as you are. Because I think we can win."

Jake sighed. He sat up, his joints protesting. "I'm not mad, Leo. I'm just… older. I remember before the Kraylor came. I remember what it was like to not have to look at the sky and wonder if you were about to be vaporized. You barely do. This war is all you've ever really known."

"That's why we have to fight," Leo insisted. "So that maybe someday, other kids won't have to know it."

There was a simple, powerful logic to it that Jake couldn't argue with. It was the same logic that had driven his own father to enlist in the colonial militia years ago, a decision that had left him a faded picture on a mantelpiece. Jake wanted to protect Leo from that same fate, but he was beginning to realize he couldn't. They were already in it, deeper than he had ever imagined.

"Just get some sleep, Leo," he said, his voice softer now. "Anya's right. We need it."

He lay back down, pulling the thin blanket up to his chin. He stared into the darkness, listening to the sounds of the resistance, until finally, a crushing exhaustion pulled him under.

He was woken by a hand on his shoulder. He sat up with a start, his heart hammering in his chest, his hand instinctively going for a weapon that wasn't there.

Captain Rostova stood over him, her form backlit by the dim light from the main tunnel. "Easy, son," she said, her voice a low rasp. "Didn't mean to spook you."

Jake’s eyes darted to the other bedroll. Leo was still fast asleep, curled up on his side. "What is it?" Jake whispered. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. Not yet." She beckoned with her head. "Walk with me."

Jake slipped on his boots and followed her out of the alcove and back into the main cavern. It was quieter now, though still far from silent. The maintenance crews were still at work, but their movements were slower, more methodical.

"The transmission went out," Captain Rostova said as they walked. "Our comms officer, Milo, confirmed it. A Fleet deep range listening post on the edge of the Cygnus sector acknowledged receipt just before we cut the beam."

A small measure of relief washed over Jake. "So they got it. Fleet Command has the coordinates."

"They have it," she confirmed. "But sending a tight-beam transmission, even a short one, isn't like whispering in a crowded room. It's like lighting a flare in the dark. The Kraylor have listening posts too. They might not have been able to decrypt the message, but they picked up the signal's origin. A high energy burst from a 'geologically unstable' moon that's supposed to be uninhabited."

The cold knot of dread in Jake's stomach tightened again. "So they know we're here."

"They know someone is here," she corrected. "And they don't like it. Long range sensors just picked up a Kraylor sweeper detachment breaking from its patrol pattern. Three assault corvettes. They're heading for this system. ETA is about six hours."

Six hours. The words hung in the air like a death sentence. "What are you going to do?" Jake asked.

"What we always do. We prepare. We're too dug in to run, and they're not sending a capital ship. Not yet. A sweeper team's job is to scout and sanitize. If they find us, they'll call in the big guns. Our job is to make sure they don't find us."

They had reached the command center, the section of the cavern where the tactical map was still projected against the wall. The map now showed their moon, a red circle indicating their position, and three blinking green icons moving steadily toward it.

"We'll go to silent running," the Captain continued, her eyes fixed on the map. "Power down everything but the most essential life support and our perimeter sensors. We become a rock again. But if they decide to do a low-altitude pass, or drop a ground scanner team… they'll find us. Our only active defense is a series of plasma repeaters emplaced at the canyon mouths. But powering them up is like setting off another flare. We can only do it if we're sure we've been spotted. It's an all or nothing defense."

She finally turned from the map and looked at Jake. Her eyes were piercing, seeming to look right through him. "I didn't wake you up just to tell you we're all about to die, son. I woke you because I have a problem, and you might be part of the solution."

"Me? How?"

"My comms officer, Milo. The one who sent your message. He's good. One of the best. But he's one man. Right now, he's trying to rig a bypass for the defense grid's power conduits. If we have to activate the repeaters, we need to do it instantly, all at once. Our primary routing panel is shot. It's a delicate job. He says another pair of hands, someone who understands a schematic and knows which end of a hydrospanner to hold, would cut the time in half." She paused. "You copied Kraylor fleet data from a secure terminal while on a prison barge. That tells me you're not an idiot when it comes to technology."

It was a statement, not a question. "I can find my way around a circuit board," Jake admitted.

"Good. That's what I need." She looked back at the slowly advancing green icons on the map. "Leo is young, but he's fast and he's observant. In a silent running situation, we can't use our internal comms. We use runners. He can do that. He can ferry messages and supplies between posts without making a sound."

Jake felt a surge of protectiveness. "He's just a kid."

"He's a kid who's already been to hell and back," Rostova countered, her voice hardening slightly. "There are no kids in this war, son. Only soldiers and victims. I'm offering him a chance to be the former. The safest place for both of you when those ships arrive is to be busy, to be useful. Hiding in a hole, waiting for the end… that's what breaks people."

She was right. He knew she was right. Hiding in the alcove while the world shook apart around them would be a thousand times worse than facing the danger head on.

"Alright," Jake said, his own voice sounding firmer than he felt. "We'll do it."

A flicker of something, maybe approval, crossed the Captain's face. "Good. Go wake up your friend. Tell him what's happening. Then report to Milo in the comms center. I'll have someone brief Leo on the runner routes."

Jake nodded and headed back toward the sleeping alcoves, his exhaustion forgotten, replaced by a sharp, cold rush of adrenaline. He found Leo just starting to stir.

"Leo, wake up," he said, keeping his voice low.

Leo sat up, rubbing his eyes. "Jake? What's going on? Is it morning?"

"No," Jake said grimly. "The Kraylor are coming."

He explained the situation quickly, the sweeper team, the silent running, the jobs the Captain had for them. He watched Leo's face as he spoke, expecting to see fear. Instead, he saw the same determined light he'd seen in the mess hall. Leo wasn't afraid. He was ready.

"A runner?" Leo said, already pulling on his boots. "I can do that. I know I can."

"Just be careful," Jake said. "No heroics. You do exactly what they tell you, you keep your head down, and you stay safe. Understood?"

"Understood," Leo said, his expression serious. "You too, Jake. Don't go blowing yourself up."

They met in the main cavern. A grizzled quartermaster was already handing Leo a data slate with a map of the base and a pouch for carrying message cylinders. Captain Rostova was there, overseeing the preparations. The whole base was in motion, but it was a quiet, controlled ballet of activity. Heavy blast doors were being manually lowered over smaller tunnel entrances. Power conduits were being disconnected, plunging whole sections of the cavern into near total darkness. The quiet hum of the generator was gone, replaced by an unsettling silence broken only by muffled footsteps and whispered commands.

Rostova saw them and pointed Jake toward the comms center. "Milo's waiting for you. Good luck, both of you."

Jake gave Leo one last look, a silent exchange that said everything they couldn't, and then he headed for the comms center. He found the tech, Milo, hunched over a complex panel of wiring that had been pulled from a wall. He was a thin man, younger than Jake expected, with wild, dark hair and smudges of carbon scoring on his face.

"You're Jake?" Milo asked without looking up. "Good. Grab that light. Hold it here. Don't move it."

For the next few hours, Jake was Milo's hands. He held lights, passed tools, stripped wires, and rerouted power couplings according to Milo's clipped instructions. The work was intense and focused, demanding his full concentration. It was a relief. He didn't have time to think about the three Kraylor ships drawing ever closer. He only had time to think about the next connection, the next soldering point, the next diagnostic check.

"Okay," Milo breathed, leaning back from the panel. "That's the main bypass. Now for the sub-relays. If my calculations are right, we can divert auxiliary power from the life support scrubbers for the two point three seconds it takes to charge the repeater capacitors. It'll make the air taste like metal, but it's better than getting vaporized."

Just as he said it, a low, deep vibration started, felt more than heard. It resonated through the rock floor, a subsonic thrum that made the teeth ache. Jake looked at Milo. Milo's eyes were wide.

"They're here," Milo whispered. "They're in orbit."

The entire base held its breath. The silence was absolute now. The vibration grew stronger, the unmistakable sound of powerful engines in low atmosphere. A ship was passing overhead. Jake's blood ran cold. He pictured it, a sleek, gunmetal gray corvette, its belly bristling with sensors, methodically scanning the rocky terrain below. He felt as exposed as if he were standing on an open plain.

The vibration peaked, and then slowly began to fade as the ship moved on. Milo let out a long, shaky breath. "One pass. Maybe they're just doing a visual sweep."

But then the vibrations returned, this time from a different direction. And then another. The three ships were bracketing their position, drawing a tightening net. Suddenly, a red light began to blink on one of Milo's sensor consoles.

"Damn it," Milo hissed. "Gravimetric scanner. They're pinging the subsurface. They've detected the cavern. The empty space."

A voice, barely a whisper, came from the runner's tunnel. It was Leo, his face pale in the gloom, his chest heaving. "Captain says they've launched a probe. It's descending toward the main canyon."

Milo looked at the panel, then at Jake. "We're out of time. They know we're here. We have to light them up." He slammed his hand down on a large, covered button on his console. An alarm, a harsh, blaring klaxon, suddenly screamed through the caverns, shattering the silence. The main lights flickered back on. "Power to defensive grid!" Milo yelled into a comm unit. "Now!"

The cavern roared to life. Jake saw men and women sprinting to the heavy repeater cannons, their faces grim and set. The moment of hiding was over. The fight had begun.

"The relays!" Milo shouted at Jake, pointing to a series of junction boxes along the wall. "The power surge is causing fluctuations! We have to stabilize them manually or the guns will cut out! Follow me!"

Jake ran after him, his heart pounding in time with the screaming alarm. They were no longer fish who had slipped through the shark's teeth. They had been found, and the shark was circling back.

The relays were a nightmare of sparking wires and overheating conduits. Milo didn't even seem to see the chaos, his hands moving with a surgeon's precision inside the open panel, rerouting thick, insulated cables. The air crackled with ozone, a sharp, clean smell that overlaid the rising stench of sweat and fear. The floor shook constantly now, not with the deep thrum of orbiting engines, but with the violent, rhythmic cough of their own defensive cannons firing into the sky.

"The third one!" Milo yelled over the din, not looking at Jake. "To your left! The pressure valve is about to blow! You need to bleed it!"

Jake scrambled over a tangle of temporary power lines to a junction box identical to the two they had already wrangled. A small, glass-covered gauge was deep in the red, its needle vibrating frantically. A high pitched whistle emanated from a copper valve on its side. Jake grabbed the wheel shaped handle. It was searingly hot, even through the thin material of his gloves. He gritted his teeth and turned.

A jet of superheated steam shot out with a deafening shriek, blasting against the rock wall opposite. It felt like trying to hold back a dragon. His muscles strained, the heat radiating through his arms, his face. The gauge needle trembled, then began to fall, slowly, agonizingly, back toward the yellow.

"Hold it!" Milo shouted. "Just a few more seconds! The gunnery team needs a clean power cycle for a lock!"

Jake held on, his vision blurring from the strain. The entire cavern was a symphony of destruction. The klaxon's scream, the percussive slam of the repeaters, the shouts of men and women, and the high pressure hiss of the steam he was barely containing. He felt a tremor run through the very rock beneath his feet, a different kind of vibration. A deep, gut churning impact that felt close. Too close.

"Lock confirmed!" a voice blared from a nearby comm speaker. "Firing solution uploaded! Firing on the probe!"

"Now!" Milo yelled at Jake.

Jake slammed the valve shut. The hissing stopped, leaving a ringing in his ears. He stumbled back, shaking, his hands raw and aching. He looked up just as Milo pointed toward a large tactical screen mounted on the far wall of the command cavern. It showed a topographical overlay of the canyons outside. A single red icon, the Kraylor probe, was descending steadily. Three green icons, the repeater cannons hidden in camouflaged emplacements on the canyon ridges, swiveled to track it.

"Come on, come on," Jake whispered, his breath fogging in the cool cavern air.

Three brilliant lances of energy, impossibly fast, shot across the screen from the green icons. They converged on the red dot. For a split second, there was nothing. Then, the screen flared white, overwhelming the sensor display. A cheer erupted through the cavern, a raw, spontaneous wave of relief and triumph. Men and women clapped each other on the back, their grim faces breaking into wide smiles.

Jake felt a grin split his own face. He looked at Milo, who was wiping sweat from his brow with the back of his arm, leaving a greasy black streak. Milo caught his eye and gave a weary nod. "One down," the technician said, his voice raspy.

But the celebration was short lived. The tactical screen cleared, the whiteout fading. And then the entire world seemed to lurch sideways. It was not a vibration. It was a blow. A colossal, physical impact that threw Jake from his feet. He slammed into a metal support girder, the air driven from his lungs. The main lights in the cavern exploded in a shower of sparks, plunging them into the chaotic, flashing strobes of the emergency lighting. Dust and small rocks rained down from the ceiling. A deep, groaning sound echoed through the rock, the sound of a mountain in pain.

"Orbital strike!" someone screamed in the darkness. "They're targeting the main entrance!"

Jake pushed himself up, his head ringing. The air was thick with rock dust, making each breath a gritty ordeal. The emergency lights cast long, dancing shadows, turning the sprinting figures of the crew into frantic ghosts.

"Milo!" Jake coughed, trying to clear his lungs. "Milo, you good?"

He saw a shape move nearby. It was Milo, picking himself up from the floor. "They're not messing around," Milo said, his voice tight. "That was a kinetic penetrator. They're trying to crack us open like a nut." He staggered over to his console, which was miraculously still glowing, and his fingers flew across the keypad. "They're bracketing us. Firing on our power signature. They knew exactly where that probe was when we shot it."

"So much for hiding," Jake muttered, pulling himself fully upright. He felt a sharp pain in his side where he'd hit the girder.

Another impact, this one farther away, but still powerful enough to make the floor jump. A new chorus of shouts and damage reports flooded the comm channels, a cacophony of controlled panic.

"Containment breach in hydroponics bay three! Repeat, breach in hydroponics!"

"Medical team to section five! We have casualties!"

"The west ridge repeater is offline! I say again, Gun Two is down!"

Jake's training took over. The momentary relief was gone, replaced by the cold, hard calculus of combat. He found a working comm panel on the wall, the one Milo had been using. He toggled the channel to the command frequency. "This is Captain Jake Taggart at Engineering Command. Status report."

A moment of static, then a familiar voice cut through the noise, calm and unshakable. "Jake. I was about to call you." It was Captain Eva Rostova, the base commander. Her voice was an anchor in the storm. "The first strike collapsed the north access tunnel. The second one slagged Gun Two's power conduit. They're being precise. They're taking us apart piece by piece."

"What are your orders?" Jake asked, his knuckles white on the edge of the console.

"Milo is our priority," Eva said, her voice unwavering despite a nearby explosion that crackled over the comm. "Our ability to fight back lives and dies with him and his systems. But I need you to be my eyes and ears. The last strike was close to the main hangar. The doors are our last line of defense on the canyon floor. I need you to get there. Assess the structural integrity. If they breach those doors, this fight gets a lot more personal."

"Understood," Jake said. "We're on our way."

He turned to Milo, who was now frantically wiring a small, portable device. "Eva needs us at the main hangar. Damage assessment."

Milo grunted, not looking up. "One step ahead of you. I need to get a decoy emitter set up near there anyway. If I can project a fake power signature out into the canyon, it might draw their fire away from the generator. Might buy us a few minutes." He snapped a final connection into place and stood up, holding a metal box covered in dials and a single, ugly antenna. "Let's go. Stay low and stay fast."

They left the relative chaos of Engineering Command and plunged into the pandemonium of the main transit corridor. It was a scene from a nightmare. Emergency crews were trying to shore up a section of the ceiling that had buckled, metal struts bent like licorice. Medics hurried past, their faces grim, carrying stretchers with wounded colonists. A thin layer of rock dust covered everything, and the air tasted of pulverized stone and ozone.

The base, once a pristine, hidden sanctuary, was now a wounded animal, bleeding and fighting for its life. Jake's heart ached for these people. They were miners, scientists, families. They had come here to escape the very violence that was now raining down upon their heads.

Another massive tremor shook the corridor. This one felt different. A long, grinding shudder, followed by the screech of tortured metal. It was closer. Much closer.

"That was the hangar," Milo said, his eyes wide.

They broke into a run, dodging debris and frantic personnel. As they rounded the final bend, the scene stopped them cold. The main hangar was a vast cavern, large enough to hold the colony's two heavy cargo shuttles. Or it had been. Now, a huge, jagged crack ran from floor to ceiling in the rock wall next to the immense blast doors. The doors themselves, meters thick of reinforced alloy and rockcrete, were visibly warped. One of them had been knocked partially off its upper track, hanging at a sickening angle. Red hydraulic fluid dripped from its mangled piston housings, pooling on the floor like blood.

Through the gap, Jake could see the outside world for the first time since they had gone to ground. The sky was a terrifying canvas of dirty orange and gray, streaked with the engine trails of the Kraylor ships. The air that drifted in was hot and smelled of burnt rock.

"The seals are broken," Milo breathed, staring at the damage. "They can't open them, but they can't fully close them either. We're exposed."

Jake approached the door cautiously. He could hear the shriek of the wind in the canyon outside, a mournful, alien sound. He keyed his comm again. "Eva, this is Jake. The hangar doors are compromised. I repeat, the main doors are severely damaged. They're holding, but they won't take another direct hit."

"Copy that, Jake," Eva's voice returned, strained. "The orbital strikes have stopped."

A sliver of hope. Jake felt his shoulders relax a fraction. "Have they withdrawn?"

"Negative," she said, and the single word was colder than any Kraylor weapon. "Sensors are picking up multiple new atmospheric entries. Small signatures, clustered together. They're not kinetic rods." There was a pause, filled with the static of the comm and the thumping of Jake's own heart. Then Eva's voice came back, devoid of all emotion. "They're dropships, Jake. Ten of them. They're landing in the main canyon."

Jake's blood ran cold. He looked from the warped, broken doors to Milo, who was staring at his own handheld scanner, his face a mask of disbelief. The decoy emitter lay forgotten on the floor beside him. The orbital bombardment hadn't been the main attack. It had been the prelude. The can opener.

They weren't just trying to crack them open anymore. They were coming inside.

"Ground forces," Jake whispered, the words feeling like ash in his mouth. He looked back at the gap in the doors, at the sliver of hostile alien sky. He could almost hear them, the tramp of armored boots on the canyon floor, the clinical snap and click of weapons being readied.

Milo finally looked up from his scanner, his eyes meeting Jake's. The frantic energy was gone, replaced by a grim, chilling certainty. "They're not trying to blow us up, Captain," he said, his voice barely audible over the wind whistling through the broken door. "They're coming to take us."

Jake drew his sidearm. The pistol felt small and insignificant in his hand. He was a soldier. He had seen combat. But this was different. This wasn't a firefight on some forgotten moon or a boarding action in the cold of space. This was an invasion of their home. They were trapped, their backs against the wall, with nowhere left to run.

He keyed his comm one last time, his voice hard as iron. "Eva. Form defensive perimeters at all primary access points. The hangar is about to become the front line."

"Understood," she replied. A new alarm began to sound throughout the base, not the blaring klaxon of a power surge, but a steady, repeating chime. A call to arms. "Good luck, Jake."

"You too, Captain," he said. He looked at the handful of engineers and technicians who had gathered near the doors, their faces pale, some holding wrenches and power spanners like clubs. They were all that stood between the colony and the Kraylor.

The whistling wind from outside died down for a moment. In the sudden, eerie silence, Jake heard a new sound. A rhythmic, mechanical clanking. It was getting closer. He took a position behind a stack of cargo containers, leveled his pistol at the gap in the door, and waited. The shark was no longer circling. It was climbing aboard.

The clanking was a sound from a forge in hell. It was heavy, methodical, and without passion. It wasn't the sound of an animal's scuttling claws or a man's hurried footsteps. It was the sound of a machine built for a single, brutal purpose, and it was just outside. Jake’s knuckles were white around the grip of his pistol. The air in the hangar tasted of ozone and fear. The handful of colonists with him were a study in terror. A woman named Anya, a hydroponics specialist, clutched a heavy pipe wrench so tightly her hands trembled. Milo, his face pale as bone, was back on his feet, holding his scanner like a talisman against the dark. He had swapped the forgotten decoy emitter for a plasma cutter, a tool not a weapon, but its industrial heft seemed to offer some small comfort.

The rhythmic clank stopped. The silence that followed was worse. It stretched for three seconds, then five, then ten. Every person in the hangar held their breath, a collective, silent prayer. Jake’s eyes were locked on the ten inch gap in the blast doors, the vertical slit of bruised purple sky. He saw a flicker of movement.

A thin, metallic limb, jointed like a spider's leg but smooth and gunmetal gray, slipped through the gap. It paused, a multifaceted sensor on its tip glowing a soft, analytical blue. It swept the interior of the hangar, its gaze unhurried. Jake didn't move. He didn't breathe. He knew instinctively that it was mapping the room, identifying threats, calculating trajectories. The colonists were statues of salt.

The limb retracted as silently as it had appeared. For a heartbeat, nothing happened. Then, with a screech of tortured metal that set everyone’s teeth on edge, two of the spider like limbs slammed into the gap, one high, one low. They dug into the warped edges of the doors and began to pull. The groaning of the durasteel was a physical pain. The gap widened, inch by agonizing inch. A foot. Two feet. Through the growing opening, Jake saw them.

They were not the hulking brutes propaganda had sometimes painted them as. They were tall, impossibly lean, their bodies encased in segmented, chitinous armor the color of a deep space nebula, dark blues and purples swirled with cold, starlight silver. Their heads were narrow and elongated, featureless save for a cluster of glowing red optics. They carried long rifles that hummed with a barely restrained energy. They moved with the fluid, unnerving grace of insects, their triple jointed legs carrying them forward without a wasted motion.

The first Kraylor warrior stepped through the breach. It didn't rush. It didn't roar a battle cry. It simply entered, its weapon sweeping the room in a calm, professional arc. Two more followed, fanning out, their movements perfectly synchronized. They were a fire team, a unit of professional soldiers, and the hangar was their designated breach point.

"Contact," Jake said into his comm, his voice a low rasp. It was a stupid, pointless thing to say. Eva knew. The whole base knew.

The lead Kraylor’s optics fixed on the cargo containers where Jake was hiding. Its rifle came up. There was no time for tactics, no time for a plan. There was only the moment. Jake fired. The report of his pistol was a pathetic pop in the cavernous space. The bullet sparked harmlessly off the alien’s chest plate. The Kraylor didn't even flinch. It simply adjusted its aim.

A bolt of brilliant blue energy, thin as a needle, hissed past Jake's head and slammed into the container behind him. The metal glowed cherry red for a second before a fist sized hole melted through it. The air filled with the acrid smell of vaporized durasteel. Jake ducked, his heart hammering against his ribs. He was outgunned. They were all outgunned.

"Scatter. Find cover. Fire at will," he yelled, the command feeling hollow and inadequate.

To his right, a young engineer, barely twenty, broke from cover and made a mad dash for a different stack of crates. He was halfway there when a Kraylor swiveled, its movements too fast to track, and fired. The blue bolt caught the engineer in the chest. He didn't scream. There was a wet, sizzling sound, and he simply ceased to be, a cloud of superheated steam and ash marking where he had stood a moment before.

Panic, stark and absolute, broke the colonists' paralysis. Anya, the hydroponics tech, let out a strangled sob and swung her pipe wrench at the nearest Kraylor. It was a brave, futile gesture. The alien sidestepped the clumsy blow with contemptuous ease, its free hand lashing out. The back of its hand, armored and sharp, connected with her head. She collapsed in a heap, the wrench clattering to the floor. The Kraylor didn't even look at her again, its attention already back on the rest of the room. It hadn't killed her. A new, colder fear trickled down Jake’s spine. *They're coming to take us.*

Milo, however, did something unexpected. He screamed, a raw, furious sound, and leveled his plasma cutter. He thumbed the ignition, and a foot long tongue of white hot plasma erupted from the nozzle with a roar. It wasn't a weapon. It had no range, no accuracy. But it was terrifying. He swung the jet of energy in a wide arc. A Kraylor warrior raised its rifle to block the stream, and the plasma jet met the weapon's energy field. The air crackled. The alien was driven back a step, its weapon glowing, whining as it struggled to dissipate the raw thermal energy.

It was the opening Jake needed. He popped up from behind his container, aimed for the only unarmored spot he could see, the cluster of red optics, and squeezed the trigger twice. Pop. Pop. The first shot went wide. The second found its mark. One of the red lights shattered. The Kraylor staggered, a high pitched, chittering sound escaping it for the first time. It was a sound of pain. It brought its rifle up blindly. Jake was already moving, diving behind a new set of crates as another energy bolt scorched the air where he had been.

The hangar had descended into chaos. More Kraylor were pouring through the widening gap in the doors. The few colonists with security training were laying down sporadic, ineffective pistol fire. The others were either cowering or trying to fight with whatever they could grab. A maintenance servitor, a dumb machine on spindly legs, trundled into the path of a Kraylor squad and was casually kicked aside, its chassis crumpling with a sad crunch.

"Eva. Report," Jake grunted into his comm, reloading his pistol with shaking hands. He had seven rounds left.

"It's bad, Jake," her voice was tight, the professional calm shattered. "They're in. They're in everywhere. The west airlock is compromised. They've breached the hydroponics bay from the surface access vents. I'm reading combat signatures in the residential sector. They're not indiscriminate. They're bypassing empty quarters, targeting occupied areas."

*They're rounding us up.*

"Medical is down," Eva continued, her voice breaking for a second. "They hit it first. Stun weapons. Dr. Aris and his team… they're prisoners."

Jake’s blood ran cold. This wasn't a military conquest. It was a harvest. He looked over his cover. Milo was still holding a Kraylor at bay with his plasma cutter, but the tool was starting to sputter, its power cell failing. Two more Kraylor were flanking him.

"Milo. Fall back," Jake yelled.

The technician didn't seem to hear him. He was locked in his own private battle, his face a mask of primal fury. Jake knew he had seconds. He looked around desperately. Near him was a loading crane, its magnetic grapple hanging limp just a few feet off the floor. An idea, stupid and desperate, sparked in his mind.

"Eva. Can you give me local control of Hangar Crane Seven?"

"What? Jake, the network is a mess…"

"Do it," he snapped. "Now."

There was a pause. "I'm patching it through to your personal datapad. You'll have to be close. It'll be a weak signal."

"Copy." Jake crawled on his belly towards the crane’s control panel, energy bolts hissing over his head. The air was thick with the smell of burned plastic and something else, something metallic and alien, the Kraylor's version of blood. The chittering Kraylor he had shot was on the ground, thrashing, but its comrades ignored it, pressing their methodical advance.

He reached the panel, a dusty, grease stained console, and slapped his datapad onto the interface port. The screen flickered. A schematic of the crane appeared, overlaid with red error messages. He bypassed them, swiping frantically, his fingers slick with sweat. He found the manual controls. Activate Maglock. Swing Arm. Extend.

Milo’s plasma cutter finally died with a pathetic cough. The Kraylor he had been holding off took a step forward, its rifle leveled not at his chest, but at his head. A stun shot. It wanted him alive.

"Now," Jake whispered, and jammed his thumb down on the ‘Swing Arm’ and ‘Activate Maglock’ icons simultaneously.

Above the firefight, the huge magnetic grapple of the crane sparked to life with a deep thrum. The massive arm swung in a ponderous but powerful arc, the grapple, a two ton block of energized metal, hurtling right towards the Kraylor squad advancing on Milo. They looked up, their red optics widening in what might have been surprise. There was no time to dodge. The grapple slammed into the three of them with the force of a freight train.

The sound was a sickening crunch of armor and whatever was inside it. They were swept off their feet and smashed against a bulkhead fifty feet away. They didn't get up.

Milo stared, his mouth agape, at the mangled heap of aliens and at the swinging crane. He looked back at Jake, his eyes wide with a mixture of terror and awe.

"Get back here," Jake bellowed. "Move."

The shock of the crane attack had bought them a precious few seconds. The other Kraylor in the hangar paused, their insectoid heads tilting as they reassessed the situation. Milo scrambled back, diving behind the containers with Jake.

"I thought… I thought I was dead," Milo panted, his whole body trembling.

"Not yet," Jake said, grabbing him by the collar. "What's the status of the other access points to this hangar? The service tunnels."

"They're sealed from the inside," Milo said, his technical mind kicking back in. "Bio-seals. They wouldn't be able to get through without cutting."

"Good." Jake peeked over the crate. At least a dozen Kraylor were in the hangar now, methodically sweeping the area, their movements once again calm and coordinated. The element of surprise was gone. They would adapt.

"Eva, I need a new plan," Jake said into his comm. "We can't hold the hangar. We can't hold anything. What are they after?"

"I think I know," Eva's voice was grim. "Their movements aren't random. Every breach, every squad, they are all moving inwards. Converging. They're using our own base schematics against us, moving along the primary power and data conduits."

"Converging on what?"

"On me," she said, and the simplicity of the statement was chilling. "On Command and Control. And the data core. They want the colony's entire operational database. Our tech, our personnel files, our geological surveys… everything."

Jake understood. With that data, they could predict humanity's colonization patterns, find our weaknesses, locate other isolated outposts just like theirs. Hopewell was not just a target. It was a key.

"We can't let them have it," Jake stated.

"I agree," Eva said. "Which is why I'm initiating Protocol Scorch. I'm preparing to overload the fusion reactor."

Jake’s heart stopped. "Eva, that will vaporize the entire colony. A five kilometer radius."

"I know. But it's a failsafe. There is one other option. It's less certain, and far more dangerous for you. Underneath the data core, in the sub-level maintenance tunnels, is the primary energy conduit junction. If you can get to it, you can manually trigger a plasma cascade. It won't destroy the base, but it will fry every data system in a chain reaction, including the core. It will turn our entire digital infrastructure into slag. It will also knock out all primary power and life support. We'd be in the dark, but the data would be safe."

"And we'd be alive," Jake finished.

"For a time," Eva corrected. "The choice is yours, Jake. I can trigger the reactor overload from here on a five minute timer, or you can try to get to the conduit junction. The Kraylor are already pushing into the sector seven maintenance corridors. They're getting close. You're the only one in a position to intercept them."

Jake looked at Milo, who had overheard the transmission. The technician's face was ashen. He looked at the remaining colonists huddled behind crates, their faces streaked with grime and tears. He looked at the bodies of the fallen, both human and alien. The choice was no choice at all.

"We're going for the junction," Jake said. His voice was firm. He was a captain again, not just a survivor. "Send me the route. The quietest one you can find."

"Sending it now," Eva replied. "The service hatch is at the back of the hangar, behind the hazardous materials storage. It leads into the old ventilation network. It will be tight, and it will take you right under their main advance. Godspeed, Jake."

"You too, Captain," Jake said. He turned to Milo. "You heard the lady. You're with me. You're my key to getting through any locked systems we find."

Milo nodded, a grim determination replacing the fear in his eyes. He still held the dead plasma cutter.

"You won't need that," Jake said, taking his last spare pistol from his belt and pressing it into Milo's hand. "You know how to use one of these?"

"Point and shoot, right?" Milo asked, his voice shaking slightly as he took the weapon.

"Aim for the red lights," Jake said. He turned to the other survivors. Anya was stirring, a dark bruise forming on her temple. There were four others, all pale but alive. "The rest of you, stay here. Barricade this position. The Kraylor want the center of the base, not the edges. If you stay quiet, they might just pass you by. Keep this comm channel open."

They looked at him, their eyes full of fear, but they nodded. He was giving them an order, a purpose, and in the midst of chaos, that was a form of hope.

Jake led Milo towards the back of the hangar, staying low, moving from container to container. The Kraylor were still methodically clearing the main floor, their movements slow and deliberate. They were herding, not hunting. They were saving their lethal force for true threats.

They reached the hazardous materials storage, a cage of thick wire mesh filled with brightly colored canisters. On the wall behind it was a small, unassuming metal plate with a wheel lock in the center. Service Hatch 7-Delta.

"Get it open," Jake whispered.

Milo knelt, his hands working the wheel. It was stiff with disuse. He grunted with the effort. From the main floor came a series of sharp, cracking sounds. Not weapons fire. It was the sound of stun rifles. They were finding the others.

"Faster, Milo," Jake urged.

With a final, desperate heave, the wheel turned. A heavy clunk echoed in the small space, and the hatch popped open a few inches, revealing a dark, narrow shaft descending into the floor. A gust of cool, musty air wafted out.

"I'm in," Milo said, pulling the hatch fully open.

"Go," Jake said, pushing him toward the opening.

Milo swung his legs into the shaft and slid down the access ladder, disappearing into the blackness. Jake took one last look back at the hangar. A Kraylor warrior turned its head, its cluster of red optics seeming to stare right at him. He didn't wait to see if he'd been spotted. He swung himself into the hatch, pulled the heavy door closed over his head, and followed Milo into the dark heart of the machine. The clang of the hatch sealing them in was the sound of a tomb door closing. Outside, the sounds of the invasion faded, replaced by the dripping of unseen water and the frantic beating of his own heart. The hunt had just begun.

The ladder was cold and slick under his gloves. Jake descended into the absolute, pressing dark, guided only by the rhythmic clanging of Milo's boots on the rungs below him. The air grew thicker, heavy with the scent of stagnant water, ozone, and something else, a coppery, metallic smell that reminded him of old, failing machinery. It was the smell of the station’s guts, a place few ever saw and fewer still wanted to.

He reached the bottom, a concrete floor slick with a thin film of moisture. His boots made a squelching sound. Above, the hatch was an invisible square of slightly less intense black. Milo was a hunched shape nearby, breathing in short, sharp gasps. Jake unclipped the small illuminator from his vest. He didn't switch it on yet.

"Status, Milo," he whispered. His voice was a dead thing in the sound swallowing confines of the service tunnel.

"I'm here. I think. It's... small." Milo's voice was tight, strained.

"It gets smaller," Jake said, a promise more than a warning. "Stay close. No noise. Move like you're already a ghost."

He thumbed the illuminator, and a narrow, weak beam of light cut through the oppressive dark. It revealed a cylindrical tunnel, barely wide enough for two men to stand abreast, stretching away into blackness. Pipes of varying thickness lined the walls, weeping condensation that ran in dark streaks down the curved concrete. A river of murky water, ankle deep, flowed sluggishly down the center of the floor.

"We follow the flow," Jake said. "Main service conduits run downhill towards the primary power junctions near the core."

He took the lead, stepping into the slow moving stream. The water was shockingly cold, seeping instantly into his boots. He ignored it. Behind him, Milo let out a soft, involuntary gasp as he followed.

"Get used to it," Jake muttered, more to himself than to the tech. He kept the light pointed low, just a few feet in front of his boots, enough to see any immediate obstacles but not enough to advertise their presence for half a kilometer down the tunnel. They moved in silence for what felt like an eternity, the only sounds the soft slosh of their boots in the water and the steady, maddening drip, drip, drip from a thousand unseen leaks in the pipes above.

The tunnel was a disorienting, repetitive landscape. Every turn looked the same. Every junction was a choice between two identical mouths of darkness. Jake relied on instinct, on the faint memory of station schematics he had been forced to study years ago, and on the subtle gradient of the floor. Down. Always down. Towards the heart.

"Jake," Milo's whisper was sharp, cutting through the rhythmic sounds of their passage. "Wait. Listen."

Jake froze, killing the light instantly. They were plunged back into total blackness, a sensory deprivation so complete it felt like a physical pressure against his eyeballs. He held his breath, straining his ears. At first, there was nothing but the dripping water. Then he heard it.

It wasn't a human sound. It was a faint, rhythmic clicking, interspersed with a low, chittering noise, like cicadas made of broken glass. It was getting closer.

"Kraylor," Jake breathed, his hand moving to the grip of his rifle. "Off the path. Now."

He grabbed Milo's arm and pulled him towards the wall, pressing them both into a shallow alcove where a bundle of thick cables broke away from the main conduit. It was poor cover, but it was all they had. The cold, damp concrete pressed against his back. He could feel Milo trembling beside him.

"Don't move. Don't breathe," Jake whispered, his lips almost touching Milo's ear.

The clicking grew louder. It was accompanied by a soft, sweeping sound, and then a light appeared, a diffuse, angry red that painted the far end of the tunnel in demonic hues. It wasn't a beam, like Jake's illuminator. It was a glow, an aura, that emanated from the approaching figures.

Two of them. They moved with an unnerving, fluid grace, their spindly limbs carrying them through the water without a splash. Their heads swiveled, clusters of red optics sweeping the tunnel from side to side in a systematic search pattern. They were taller than a man, impossibly thin, their bodies a lattice of dark, chitinous plating and what looked like bundles of exposed wiring. One of them carried a long, articulated rifle. The other had a shorter, thicker weapon, the stun rifle they had heard in the hangar.

They were a hundred meters away. Then fifty. Then twenty. Jake felt his heart hammering against his ribs, a drumbeat that seemed loud enough to echo through the entire station. Beside him, Milo’s breathing was a ragged, hitched thing he was trying and failing to control. The safety on the pistol in his hand clicked audibly.

Jake’s hand shot out and clamped down on Milo’s wrist, hard. He gave a single, sharp shake. A clear message. Not yet.

The lead Kraylor paused directly in front of their alcove. Its head, a featureless wedge except for the seven points of red light, tilted as if listening. The chittering sound stopped. The world stopped. The red glow from its optics washed over them, a light that felt like it could see right through their skin, right into their terrified souls. Jake could see the intricate detail on its weapon, the way the water beaded on its slick, black carapace. He could smell it now, a sharp, sterile scent like burnt electronics and antiseptic.

He braced himself, ready to shove Milo deeper into the alcove and open fire, to sell their lives for a few precious seconds. But the Kraylor’s head tilted again, its attention drawn by something further down the tunnel. A louder drip, a falling piece of rust, a rat. It didn't matter. The alien chittered once to its companion, a sound like a burst of static, and they moved on.

Their red glow receded down the tunnel, the clicking and chittering fading until it was once again swallowed by the dripping water. Jake didn't move. He counted to one hundred, then to two hundred. He did not release his grip on Milo's wrist until the only sound was the frantic thumping of his own pulse in his ears.

"Okay," he finally whispered, the single word a massive exhalation of pent up tension. He let go of Milo, who slumped against the wall, his whole body shaking.

"I... I almost..." Milo stammered.

"I know," Jake said, his voice softer than before. "You didn't. That's what matters." He switched the illuminator back on, its weak white beam a comfort after the predatory red of the Kraylor. "Come on. They're heading the same way we are. We need to move faster."

They pushed on, their pace more urgent now. The fear was a new fuel, sharp and potent. The brief, terrifying encounter had stripped away any lingering sense of safety the tunnels might have offered. The enemy was not just on the main levels. The enemy was everywhere.

After another ten minutes of hurried, sloshing progress, the tunnel ended at a wall of rust stained durasteel. A heavy bulkhead door, sealed tight. In its center was a keypad and a retinal scanner, both dark.

"Dead end?" Milo asked, his voice still shaky.

"Service junction," Jake corrected, running his light over the door. He pointed to a manual release wheel set into the wall, much like the one on the hatch. "They seal these sections for maintenance or containment. The power's been cut." He grabbed the wheel with both hands and heaved. It didn't budge. He tried again, his muscles straining, the veins in his neck standing out. The wheel was frozen solid with rust and neglect. "Damn it."

Milo pushed past him, his eyes scanning the keypad. "No, no, not a dead end. Not for me." The fear in his voice was being replaced by a new tone, the focused energy of an expert in his element. "The lock's electronic, but the mechanism is magnetic. If the power's out, it should be in the default closed position. But there's a bypass. There's always a bypass."

He knelt and pulled a small, multi-purpose tool from his jacket pocket. He pried open the panel below the keypad, revealing a mess of wires and circuit boards.

"It's an old system. Sol-Core 3. Primitive, but reliable," Milo murmured, his hands moving with a new, confident dexterity. He used a pair of pliers from the tool to snip a small wire, then stripped two others. "They run a redundant charge through a capacitor to keep the bolts locked even during a power failure. The trick isn't to power it up. The trick is to drain it. All at once."

He looked at Jake. "This is going to make a noise."

"Just do it," Jake said, raising his rifle and aiming back down the tunnel from which they came.

Milo took a deep breath and touched the two stripped wires together.

The result was not the loud bang Jake had expected. It was a deafening, high pitched squeal, like a thousand tortured circuits screaming at once. A shower of blue sparks erupted from the panel, and every light in the tunnel, including Jake's illuminator, flickered and died, plunging them into darkness. The squeal stopped, replaced by the heavy, satisfying thunk of the magnetic bolts retracting.

The silence that followed was more profound than before.

"Milo?" Jake called out into the black.

"It worked," Milo's voice came from near the floor, tinged with pride. "Told you. My illuminator's fried though."

Jake's wouldn't turn back on either. The electromagnetic pulse had been stronger than he'd anticipated. "Mine too," he said. "No more light."

"No more light," Milo repeated. "So we're blind."

"No," Jake said, grabbing the edge of the heavy door and pulling. It scraped open with a groan of protesting metal. "We're almost there. I can smell it."

The air from the newly opened section was different. It was cleaner, colder, and carried the distinct hum of high energy power systems. The coppery smell was gone, replaced by the sterile, charged scent of a massive machine at work.

They stepped through the doorway and left the dripping, dilapidated service tunnel behind. This new corridor was different. The floor was grated metal, and beneath it, thick, glowing blue cables pulsed with barely contained energy, providing a dim, ethereal light. The walls were smooth, clean, and the air was still. They were in the substructure of the command and control center.

"Okay," Jake said, his eyes adjusting to the blue twilight. "From here on, everything is live. Every system is active. Be careful what you touch." He activated his comm bead. "Captain Rostova, do you read me?"

Static. Then, her voice, faint but clear. "...ake? Jake, is that you? Your signal is... breaking up. Where are you?"

"Sublevel three, approaching central command," Jake said, keeping his voice low. "We're blind. Kraylor patrol in the tunnels. We bypassed a locked bulkhead. We're close."

"Confirmed, Jake," Rostova's voice crackled. "I'm seeing your proximity on the deep system chart. You're right on top of it. But my tactical display just lit up. Heavy Kraylor concentration right where you're going. They're not just guarding the core, Jake. They're... plugged into it. They have interface units deployed. They're trying to brute force their way past the primary encryption."

"What are they after?" Jake asked.

"I don't know. Station defense codes? Navigational charts for the whole sector? A direct line to Fleet Command? Any of it would be catastrophic. The hard reset is more critical than ever. It will purge their hardware from the system." There was a pause. "Jake, the reset protocol isn't a button. It's a physical sequence. There are three coolant system release valves that have to be opened in a specific order to trigger the emergency flush. They're inside the core chamber itself."

"Understood," Jake said. "We're going in."

"Good luck, Jake. Rostova out."

They moved down the glowing blue corridor. It ended in a single, unadorned door. It was unlocked. Jake pushed it open slowly.

They were on a maintenance gantry overlooking a vast, cylindrical chamber. This was the heart of Outpost Epsilon. The Central Command Core. A colossal pillar of shimmering, crystalline data stacks rose from the floor far below and disappeared into the ceiling high above, glowing with a soft internal light. The air hummed, a palpable vibration that Jake could feel in his teeth.

And they were not alone.

On the main platform surrounding the base of the core, there were at least a dozen Kraylor. Three of them were not like the others. They were bulkier, their carapaces covered in complex electronic devices, and they were kneeling, their slender, three jointed fingers working on consoles they had attached to the base of the crystal pillar. Their interface units. Thick black cables snaked from their machines into open service ports on the core, siphoning its lifeblood. The other nine Kraylor were warriors, identical to the ones from the tunnel, standing guard in a loose perimeter, their rifles held at the ready.

"My God," Milo whispered.

"The valves," Jake said, his eyes scanning the chamber. He spotted them. Three large, yellow wheels, set into the wall at different points around the circular platform. Each was guarded by at least two Kraylor warriors. "There. Ten o'clock, three o'clock, and six o'clock."

"We'll never get to them," Milo said, his voice a knot of despair. "They'll see us the second we step out there."

"They won't be looking at you," Jake said, pulling his rifle into his shoulder. "They'll be looking at me." He turned to Milo, his face grim in the blue glow of the core. "The sequence. Do you know it?"

"I... I can pull it from the local network on my datapad, but it'll take a minute," Milo stammered, fumbling for the device.

"You won't have a minute. You'll have seconds," Jake said. "I'm going to make a lot of noise at the ten o'clock position. It'll draw them all in. When you see the opening, you run. You run for the six o'clock valve first, it's the furthest. Then three. Then ten. That's the order. Six, three, ten. Got it?"

Milo looked from Jake, to the Kraylor, to the terrifying open space of the core chamber. He was pale, his eyes wide with a fear so profound it was almost paralyzing. But then he looked at the pistol Jake had given him, still clutched in his hand. He looked at the aliens defiling the heart of his home. A flicker of something new, something hard, entered his eyes. He nodded. "Six, three, ten."

"Good man," Jake said. He gave Milo's shoulder a firm squeeze. "Don't stop, no matter what you see. No matter what you hear. Just run."

Without another word, Jake vaulted over the gantry railing, landing with a heavy thud on the main platform ten feet below.

The nearest Kraylor spun around, its head snapping up with a burst of chittering static. Its red optics widened. Jake didn't give it a chance to raise an alarm. He fired, a three round burst of plasma bolts that slammed into the alien's chest, throwing it backward with a shriek of tortured metal and vaporized fluid.

All twelve heads snapped towards him. A chorus of alarm screeches filled the chamber.

"Come on, you ugly bastards!" Jake roared, charging towards the ten o'clock valve and laying down a curtain of suppressing fire. "You want a piece of the station? Come and get it from me!"

The hunt was over. The fight had begun.

The Kraylor warriors moved with a chilling, inhuman grace. Their legs, jointed like a spider's, carried them across the platform in a fluid, scuttling motion that was terrifyingly fast. Red plasma bolts sizzled through the air, their heat washing over Milo's hiding spot on the gantry above. They were all focused on Jake. Every single one of them. He was a whirlwind of controlled violence, a single human figure against a tide of alien killers. He slid behind a thick conduit pipe, a volley of red energy splashing against it and sending molten metal flying. He popped out from the other side, his rifle barking, and another Kraylor staggered, its head exploding in a shower of green fluid and sparking electronics.

Milo’s heart was a trip hammer against his ribs. His lungs burned, but he wasn’t breathing. Jake’s voice echoed in his mind. Six, three, ten. Don’t stop, no matter what. He watched as Jake ducked another volley of fire and sprinted to a new piece of cover closer to the ten o’clock valve, drawing the remaining seven warriors with him like a magnet. The other end of the chamber, the six o’clock position, was completely exposed. It was now or never.

The pistol felt slick and alien in his sweaty palm. He took a breath that was more of a gasp and forced his legs into motion. He didn’t climb down. He simply rolled over the railing, a desperate, clumsy imitation of Jake’s earlier vault. He hit the deck plating with a jarring impact that sent a spike of pain up his shins. He stumbled, nearly falling, but the adrenaline was a fire in his veins. He pushed himself forward, his worn station boots slapping against the metal floor.

The sound was immense. The crackle of plasma fire, the high pitched shriek of stressed metal, the chittering screams of the Kraylor, and Jake's defiant roars. It was a symphony of death, and Milo was running right through the middle of it. The open space felt like a million miles. Every inch of his back prickled, expecting the searing impact of a plasma bolt. He didn’t look back. He just ran. Six, three, ten.

The yellow wheel of the first valve loomed before him. It was bigger up close, its paint chipped and faded. A single Kraylor warrior lay dead nearby, one of the two that had been guarding it, its limbs twisted at unnatural angles. Jake's work. Milo skidded to a halt in front of the valve, his hands grabbing the cold, heavy metal. He threw his entire body weight into it.

It didn't budge.

Panic, cold and sharp, lanced through him. It was seized. Rusted shut. Designed to be turned by maintenance drones or hydraulic assists, not by the trembling hands of a terrified network technician. "No," he breathed, the word lost in the din. He braced his feet, his jaw clenched so hard his teeth ached, and pulled again. A groan of protesting metal. It moved. Barely an inch. He could hear the battle raging behind him, the distinct sound of Jake’s rifle changing, a higher pitched whine indicating he was nearing the end of a power cell. Time was running out.

Milo gave a ragged sob of effort and desperation. He ignored the burning in his shoulders and the screaming of his muscles. He put every ounce of his fear, his anger, and his grief for his station into one monumental heave. The valve shrieked and began to turn. It was agonizingly slow. Each rotation was a lifetime. He kept pushing, his vision starting to swim with black spots. Finally, with a heavy, satisfying clunk, it locked into its fully closed position.

Instantly, the chamber’s ambient hum changed. The pure, steady blue light of the energy core wavered, and a deep, resonant thrumming began to vibrate up through the soles of Milo’s boots. It was working. The first stage of the cascade failure was initiated.

The change did not go unnoticed. One of the three Kraylor engineers kneeling at the crystal pillar jerked its head up. Its optical sensors, previously focused on its console, swiveled and fixed directly on Milo. It let out a series of sharp, clicking chirps, a sound completely different from the warriors' battle cries. It was an alarm. A specific one.

Two of the warriors fighting Jake immediately broke off their attack. They spun, their red optics locking onto Milo's position across the vast chamber. The diversion was over. They knew what he was doing.

"Milo, move!" Jake's voice, strained and hoarse, cut through the noise.

Milo didn't need telling. He was already sprinting, his legs pumping harder than he thought possible. His target was the three o'clock valve, a quarter of the way around the circular chamber. The two Kraylor were faster. Their scuttling gait ate up the distance with terrifying efficiency. The air to his left exploded as a plasma bolt hit the floor where he had been a split second before. The heat washed over his face. He dodged right, slipping on a patch of slick coolant, his arms pinwheeling for balance. He stayed upright. Another bolt sizzled past his head, so close he could smell the ozone.

He risked a glance back at Jake. The soldier was pinned. The remaining five warriors were pressing him hard, their fire so concentrated it was a miracle he was still standing. He was trying to give Milo cover, firing desperate bursts towards the two aliens chasing him, but they were ignoring him. Milo was the priority target now.

The three o’clock valve was just ahead, tucked behind a bank of humming relay servers. It was his only hope for cover. He dove, a clumsy, sprawling slide across the metal deck, and crashed behind the server bank just as another plasma volley tore through the air above him. The servers exploded, showering him with sparks and shards of plastic. He scrambled to his feet, ears ringing, and clawed at the second yellow wheel.

Like the first, it was stiff. He heard the clicking, scuttling sound of the Kraylor rounding the server bank. He had seconds. He pulled the pistol from his belt. He had never fired a weapon in his life. His hand trembled so violently he could barely aim. A spindly, three fingered hand appeared around the edge of the server. Milo screamed and pulled the trigger. The pistol bucked, and a bolt of blue energy, so different from the Kraylor’s red, hit the wall ten feet to the left of his target. But the sound, the flash, it made the alien recoil for a fraction of a second.

It was enough. Milo dropped the pistol, grabbed the wheel with both hands, and pulled. This time he knew what to expect. He roared, a raw, primal sound of pure desperation, and threw his back into it. The valve turned, faster than the first. Clunk.

The effect was immediate and violent. The deep thrumming intensified into a bone shaking shudder. The lights in the chamber flickered wildly, and the massive crystal core at the center began to pulse with an angry orange light, the blue completely gone. Red emergency strobes kicked on around the platform's perimeter, painting the chaotic scene in flashes of crimson and darkness. A synthesized voice, calm and feminine, echoed through the chamber. "Warning. Core containment failure imminent. All personnel evacuate immediately."

The two Kraylor chasing him shrieked in frustration and raised their rifles. But before they could fire, a storm of plasma from across the room slammed into one of them. The alien convulsed, its carapace shattering, and collapsed in a heap. Jake. He had managed to take down another of his assailants and was now giving Milo the slimmest of covering fire. "The last one, Milo! Go!"

The last valve. Ten o'clock. Right next to where Jake was making his stand. It was the shortest distance, but it was also the most dangerous. He had to run directly into the heart of the firefight. The remaining Kraylor, now four warriors and the three engineers, were all converging on the center. Their plan was ruined. The station's core was about to blow. Their expressions were unreadable, but their movements screamed panic. They were abandoning their posts, some trying to get to Jake, others seemingly trying to escape.

Milo ran. He ran through a hellscape of strobing red lights, sizzling plasma bolts, and the constant, jarring shudder of the dying station. He dodged the twitching corpse of one alien, leaped over a tangle of thick black cables torn loose from the core, his lungs on fire. The air was thick with the smell of burnt plastic and vaporized alien blood.

He saw Jake. He was crouched behind a scorched control panel, his left arm hanging limp and his uniform stained dark. He was reloading his rifle with his good hand, his movements slow and clumsy. One of the Kraylor warriors saw its chance. It ignored Milo and charged Jake, its rifle discarded, its sharp forelimbs raised like scythes for a killing blow.

"Jake!" Milo shrieked.

Jake looked up, his eyes wide. He was out of time. He wouldn't bring his rifle up fast enough.

Without thinking, Milo did the only thing he could. He still had the heavy pistol in his hand, though he had forgotten it was there. He raised it, both hands to steady his aim, and fired. He didn't aim. He just pointed it at the charging alien and pulled the trigger again and again. The first shot went wide. The second hit the Kraylor in the leg, causing it to stumble. The third caught it square in the back as it staggered. The alien went down in a clatter of chitin and metal, landing just feet from Jake.

Milo was still pulling the trigger, the pistol clicking uselessly, the power cell depleted. He stared, dumbfounded, at the fallen alien. He had done that. He had actually hit it.

"The valve!" Jake yelled, snapping him out of his trance.

Milo turned to the final yellow wheel. It was right there. He lunged for it. But as his fingers brushed the metal, one of the Kraylor engineers, bulkier than the warriors, slammed into him from the side. The impact drove the air from his lungs and sent him sprawling. The alien stood over him, its face a complex mask of electronics and optical sensors. It raised a multi jointed limb, tipped with what looked like a fusion cutter, and brought it down.

Milo threw his arms up to shield his face. He braced for the searing pain. But it never came. He heard a sickening crunch and a wet tearing sound. He opened his eyes. Jake was there, standing between him and the engineer. He had driven the butt of his empty rifle into the side of the alien's head, and now he had his combat knife, a wicked piece of dark metal, and was plunging it deep into the joint where the Kraylor's neck met its torso. Green fluid gushed from the wound. The engineer shrieked, a sound of static and agony, and swatted at Jake with its heavy arms, but its movements were already failing. It collapsed onto its knees and then fell face first onto the deck.

Jake stumbled back, breathing in ragged, painful gasps. His face was a mask of blood and grime. "Milo," he rasped, pointing with the bloody knife. "Finish it."

Milo scrambled to his feet. His entire body was a tapestry of pain. The remaining Kraylor were in disarray. The core was now pulsing a violent, blinding crimson, and the shudders were so strong it was hard to stand. Pieces of the ceiling were starting to rain down. He grabbed the final valve. He didn't know where the strength came from. It was pure, unthinking instinct. He turned the wheel. It spun freely, the locking mechanisms already damaged by the stress.

Clunk.

For a moment, there was absolute silence. The klaxons died. The thrumming stopped. The pulsing crimson light of the core held steady for one, long, terrible second, and then it simply went out. The chamber was plunged into an abyssal darkness, broken only by the faint, pathetic glow of a few emergency power strips on the floor. A wave of invisible energy, silent and powerful, washed through the room.

Milo heard the Kraylor scream, a chorus of agony that was abruptly cut short. There was a series of pops and cracks as their electronic systems and bio-mechanisms overloaded. The two engineers still standing at the central pillar exploded in a flash of green and blue light as their interface units backfired, the stolen energy of the core turning on its thieves. Then, silence. A profound, ringing silence that was more deafening than the chaos that had preceded it.

The only sound was Milo's own frantic, ragged breathing. And Jake’s.

"Jake?" Milo called out into the darkness, his voice trembling.

A weak groan came from a few feet away. "Here."

Milo fumbled for the datapad still clipped to his belt. Its screen flickered on, casting a small, weak circle of light. He found Jake slumped against the ruined control panel. He was alive, but barely. The entire left side of his body armor was shredded and blackened, and the arm beneath was a mess.

"You did it," Jake breathed, a faint, pained smile touching his lips. "You actually did it, you crazy bastard."

Milo knelt beside him, his hands hovering uselessly over the wounds. "We did it," he corrected, his voice thick with emotion. He looked around the darkened, ruined chamber. The great crystal was dark and inert, like a shard of dead glass. The bodies of the Kraylor were strewn across the deck, their lights extinguished, their menace gone.

He had run. He had fought. He had faced down the monsters that had taken his home, and he had won. He looked at his own hands, still shaking, but not from fear anymore. They were steady. He was no longer Milo the technician. He wasn't sure what he was, but he was different. He was a survivor.

"The flush... it should purge the Kraylor from the primary systems," Milo said, his mind already shifting back to technical familiarity, a comforting anchor in the madness. "But the station... the station is dead, Jake. We're on minimal emergency power. Just life support in a few sealed sectors."

Jake coughed, a wet, rattling sound. "One problem at a time." He looked at Milo, his eyes surprisingly clear in the dim glow of the datapad. "First, you get me out of here. Then, we find a way off this rock."

Milo nodded, a newfound resolve hardening his features. The fight was over. But the struggle to survive had just begun. He looked from Jake's wounded form to the dark, silent hallway they had come from. They were alone, deep in the guts of a crippled space station, but they were alive. For now, that was enough.

The silence in the core chamber was a physical presence, a heavy blanket smothering the echoes of violence. Milo’s own heartbeat was a frantic drum against his ribs. He took a deep, shuddering breath, the air thick with the metallic tang of ozone and the sickeningly sweet smell of cooked alien biology. He had to move. He had to get Jake out.

"Okay," Milo said, his voice a raw whisper that was swallowed by the cavernous dark. "Okay, we're moving."

He tried to shift Jake, to get him into a position where he could be lifted or at least dragged. A gasp of pure agony from his friend stopped him cold.

"Don't," Jake rasped, his eyes squeezed shut. "Anything moves... I think I'll come apart."

Milo’s light from the datapad played over the injury again. The armor was more than shredded. It was melted, fused in places to the flesh beneath. A dark, viscous fluid, too thick for human blood, oozed from the edges of the wound. It was some kind of Kraylor bio-weapon residue, or perhaps a coolant from the backfiring interface. It was eating away at him.

Panic, cold and sharp, tried to claw its way back up Milo’s throat. He forced it down. One problem at a time. Jake couldn't be moved easily. So Milo needed to find something to move him on.

"Stay here," Milo said, the command utterly redundant. "Don't move. I'll be right back."

Leaving Jake was like severing a limb, but he had no choice. He stood, his own legs protesting, and swept the datapad's weak beam across the chamber. Ruin. Twisted metal, shattered crystal, and the grotesque, motionless forms of the Kraylor. Their insectoid limbs were frozen at odd angles, their multi-faceted eyes dark and vacant. He had done this. The thought was both terrifying and exhilarating.

Near the central pillar, where the two engineers had disintegrated, he saw it. A maintenance trolley, a simple flatbed cart with mag-lock wheels, used for hauling power cells and diagnostic equipment. It was on its side, one wheel bent, but it was serviceable.

He wrestled it upright, the screech of metal on the deck grating in the profound quiet. He checked the wheels. The mag-lock was fried, like everything else, but they still rolled. It would have to do. He pushed it back toward Jake, the cart's movement unnervingly loud.

"Found you a chariot, old man," Milo grunted, trying for a lightness he did not feel.

Jake opened his eyes, a flicker of his old humor present. "Does it have a beverage holder?"

"Top of the line model," Milo replied. "Now, this is going to hurt. A lot. I'm sorry."

"Just... get it over with," Jake breathed.

The next two minutes were a blur of controlled effort and muffled pain. Milo used every bit of strength he had, leveraging Jake's body millimeter by millimeter onto the low cart. He tried to be gentle, but with an injury that severe, every touch was torture. Jake didn't scream. He made a low, guttural sound deep in his chest, his teeth clenched so hard Milo thought they would crack. Finally, he was on the cart, lying on his good side, the ruined half of his body facing the ceiling. His face was slick with sweat, his skin a pale, clammy gray in the datapad's glow.

"Okay," Milo said, his own breath coming in ragged bursts. "Okay. We're on our way. Medbay. Sector Gamma. You just hang on."

He clipped the datapad back to his belt, angling the screen so it cast a cone of light ahead of them. Then he gripped the trolley's handle and began to pull. The weight was immense. Jake was not a small man, and the cart's damaged wheel made it drag and slew to one side. Every inch was a struggle.

They left the core chamber, the tomb of their victory, and entered the main access corridor. Here, the darkness was even more complete. The emergency strips on the floor provided the barest orientation, a faint green trail leading into an infinite black. The station was a ghost ship. The familiar hum of the life support, the thrum of the gravity generators, the whisper of the air recyclers, all gone. The only sounds were the scrape and wobble of the trolley, Milo’s strained breathing, and the wet, shallow gasps coming from Jake.

They passed the crew quarters for the engineering deck. The doors were all sealed, emergency bulkheads having slammed shut when the power failed. Milo knew there were people behind them. His friends. His colleagues. Trapped in the dark, waiting for a rescue that might never come. He felt a pang of guilt. He had saved the station from the Kraylor, but he had doomed its inhabitants to a slow, cold death unless he could get things working again.

"Pressure doors... sealed," Jake mumbled, his voice slurred. "Power routing... must be a cascade failure... from the core flush."

"Don't talk," Milo ordered, his voice tight. "Save your energy."

"Tell me something, Milo," Jake said, his eyes unfocused. "Did we... did we get them all?"

Milo looked back into the darkness they had left. "The ones in the core, yeah. The flush should have wiped any of them tied into the main network. Fried them in their shells."

They rounded a corner, the weak light illuminating a scene of carnage. Two station security officers lay on the deck, their pulse rifles still clutched in their hands. A few feet away was a Kraylor warrior, its carapace cracked and smoking, a deep gash from a plasma burst bisecting its torso. The humans had put up a fight. They just hadn't been enough.

Milo averted his eyes, a knot tightening in his stomach. He knew these men. He had played cards with a security guard named Corporal Thorne two nights ago. He thought one of them might be him. He couldn't bring himself to look closer. He just pulled the cart past them, his focus narrowing to the green line on the floor. One problem at a time. Get Jake to the medbay.

The journey was a nightmare crawl through the station's dead guts. They had to navigate around debris, overturned supply crates, and bodies. In one section, a conduit had ruptured in the ceiling, spraying cryogenic coolant that had frozen a whole section of the corridor in a thick layer of ice. The cart slipped and slid, and Milo had to fight to keep it from tipping over completely.

As they passed through the junction to Delta sector, a flicker of movement caught his eye. He froze, his hand instinctively going for the service pistol holstered at his hip. He slowly panned the datapad's light toward the source.

It was a Kraylor. But not a warrior. This one was smaller, more slender, with delicate, multi-jointed manipulators instead of heavy claws. It was one of their technicians, or what was left of it. The energy wave from the core had caught it halfway through interfacing with a wall panel. It was fused to the console, its upper torso and head still twitching spasmodically. Blue sparks jumped from its exposed circuitry to the wall, and a low, warbling sound emanated from its vocalizer, a repeating loop of alien agony.

Milo’s blood ran cold. It was no threat, not really. It was just a dying machine, a grotesque monument to their victory. But the sight of it, the sound of it, was deeply unsettling.

"Milo?" Jake whispered, his voice weak. "What is it?"

"Nothing," Milo said, his voice hard. "Just a ghost."

He could not leave it like that. It felt wrong, a lingering stain of the invasion that he had to wipe clean. He drew his pistol. The weapon felt heavy, alien in his hand. He had fired it once before, in the frantic firefight on the way to the core. Now, in the cold silence, it felt different. This was not combat. This was an execution. An act of mercy, perhaps. He raised the pistol, the beam of his datapad light catching the sights. He aimed for the creature's head, a complex knot of optics and antennae. He squeezed the trigger.

The shot was deafening in the enclosed space. The pistol kicked, and the high energy slug slammed into the Kraylor's head, blowing it apart in a shower of green fluid and shattered electronics. The twitching stopped. The warbling sound cut out. Silence rushed back in, more profound than before. The smell of burnt circuits joined the cocktail of odors in the air.

Milo stood there for a long moment, the pistol smoking in his hand. The echo of the shot seemed to roll on and on down the dead corridors. He had just extinguished a life. A miserable, half-dead, alien life, but a life nonetheless. He felt nothing. Not remorse, not satisfaction. Just a vast, hollow emptiness. He holstered the pistol and grabbed the trolley's handle.

"Let's go," he said to the silence.

They finally reached the main concourse of Sector Gamma. The medbay was at the far end. The concourse was a wide, open space, usually bustling with personnel. Now it was a debris field. A firefight had clearly happened here, a major one. The bodies of both humans and Kraylor were scattered across the floor. An automated cleaning drone lay on its back, its wheels spinning uselessly.

Milo’s heart sank. The main doors to the medbay were buckled and twisted, a massive dent in the center suggesting something had tried to ram its way through.

"Damn it," he hissed.

He pushed the trolley into a relatively clear corner and knelt by Jake again. Jake's breathing was even more shallow now. His skin had a bluish tint around his lips.

"Jake. Jake, stay with me. The main door is jammed. Is there another way in? A service hatch? Anything?"

Jake’s eyes fluttered open. It took him a moment to focus. "Hatch... yes. Around the side. Diagnostic access. For the... bio-scanners. Panel... 7B."

Milo nodded, patting his friend's good shoulder. "Okay. 7B. I'll get it open. You just hang on. You hear me? You hang on."

He found the panel, a small, unassuming square of metal flush with the wall. The manual release was, of course, fried. He pulled his multi-tool from his belt, the familiar weight a small comfort. He pried at the edges of the panel, his fingers straining. Metal groaned. With a final, desperate heave, the panel popped off, clattering to the floor.

Behind it was a mess of wiring and a small keypad, dark and inert. He needed to trip the locking mechanism manually. He shone the datapad light into the recess. He could see the magnetic locks. He just needed to short them out. He unspooled a length of conductive wire from his tool, stripped the ends with his teeth, and carefully touched them to two specific terminals he recognized from the schematics he had practically memorized over the years.

Sparks flew, and with a heavy clunk, the locks on the main door disengaged. The door didn't slide open, it was too damaged for that. But it was loose.

He went back to the door and put his shoulder into it. It moved a few inches with a screech of tortured metal. He pushed again, and again, grunting with the effort, his muscles burning. He managed to create a gap just wide enough to squeeze the trolley through.

The medbay was an oasis of eerie calm. The emergency power was working better here, a dedicated system. The lights were dim but steady, casting long shadows from the rows of bio-beds and medical consoles. It was a mess, supplies scattered, an autostretcher overturned. But it was intact. And it was sterile. The air smelled clean, antiseptic.

He pushed the trolley next to the primary diagnostic bed, a large, intimidating piece of machinery surrounded by articulated arms bearing scanners and injectors. The main screen was dark. "No main power," he muttered to himself.

"Milo," Jake’s voice was barely a whisper now. "The... trauma kits... under the bed."

Milo dropped to his knees and found a sealed compartment. He hit the release, and a drawer slid out, containing two large, military grade trauma kits. He tore one open. Inside were synth-flesh applicators, coagulants, bio-foam injectors, pain suppressants, and sterile wraps. It was a treasure trove.

But he was a technician, not a doctor. He looked at the array of medical tools, his mind blanking. He could rebuild a fusion injector from spare parts in the dark, but this was a foreign language.

"Okay, Jake, talk to me. What do I do?"

Jake’s eyes were closed. "Scanner... portable scanner... in the kit."

Milo rummaged and found a handheld device that looked vaguely like his own datapad. He powered it on. A simple interface appeared. He held it over Jake's wound. The device whirred, and a holographic display shimmered into existence above it, showing a detailed, color coded schematic of Jake's arm and torso. Red and orange pulsed angrily over the damaged areas. Text scrolled on the scanner's screen.

"Compound fracture... severe tissue damage... foreign contaminants detected... immediate application of cauterizing agent and broad spectrum bio-foam recommended."

Milo's hands were shaking again. Cauterizing agent. That meant burning the wound closed. He looked at Jake, then back at the scanner. He took a deep breath. He could follow instructions. It was just another technical procedure. A very, very messy one.

He found the cauterizer, a pen-like device. He took the pain suppressant injector, a hypospray, and pressed it against Jake’s neck. There was a hiss, and Jake’s tense body relaxed slightly, his breathing evening out just a fraction.

"Alright, Jake," Milo whispered, more to himself than to his unconscious friend. "This is it."

He worked for what felt like hours. He followed the scanner's instructions with a religious focus, his technician's mind for detail taking over. He cleaned the wound, removing shards of melted armor with a pair of forceps. He applied the cauterizer, the smell of searing flesh making him gag. He then injected the bio-foam, which expanded to fill the wound, stabilizing the tissue and bone fragments within a sterile, semi-rigid matrix. Finally, he wrapped the entire area in sterile bandages.

When he was done, he sat back on the floor, his body trembling with exhaustion and adrenaline withdrawal. Jake's chest rose and fell in a slow, steady rhythm. The bluish tint was gone from his lips. The bleeding had stopped. The bio-foam was holding. He was stable.

Milo felt a wave of relief so profound it almost made him dizzy. He had done it. He had kept his friend alive.

He looked around the quiet, dimly lit medbay. This was their sanctuary, for now. A tiny island of stability in a vast, dead station. The fight was over, but the war for survival had just entered a new, quieter, and perhaps more desperate phase.

He stood up and walked over to a wall-mounted station schematic, its screen dark. He held up his datapad, its light illuminating the intricate blue lines of the station's layout. His finger traced a path from their current location in Sector Gamma. He bypassed the hangar bay, its outer doors were almost certainly sealed and without power. He traced a line upwards, through service shafts and maintenance crawlways, to the highest point of the station.

To the long range communications array.

It was their only real hope. A long shot. The array was probably damaged, its power systems certainly offline. But it was a problem he could understand. It was a machine. And Milo knew how to fix machines.

He looked from the schematic back to Jake's sleeping form. He had saved one life. Now he had to find a way to save them both. The silence of the station pressed in on him, no longer a blanket, but a challenge. He was no longer Milo the technician, or even Milo the survivor. He was the station's keeper. Its ghost. And its only hope. He had a long, dark road ahead. He pulled a chair over next to Jake’s bed, sat down, and began to plan.

The silence was the first thing he noticed when he woke. It was a physical presence, a weight in the air that pressed on his eardrums. He had fallen asleep in the chair, his head lolled back at an uncomfortable angle, his neck a knot of stiff muscle. For a confused moment, he thought it was all a nightmare. The alarms, the chaos, the creature, Jake’s wound. Then his eyes focused on the medbay bed, on the steady green line tracing its heartbeat across the monitor. It was real. All of it.

He pushed himself up, his joints protesting, and stumbled over to Jake’s side. The bandage on Jake’s shoulder was clean, with no sign of new bleeding. The bio-foam was holding. He reached out a hesitant hand and laid it on Jake’s forehead. It was warm, but not feverish. Jake’s breathing was deep and even, the peaceful rhythm a stark contrast to the ragged, desperate gasps of before. Milo let out a breath he did not realize he had been holding. The relief was a fragile thing, a thin sheet of glass over a deep chasm of fear. It was holding, for now.

He turned his attention to their sanctuary. The medbay’s emergency lights flickered, casting long, unreliable shadows that danced and twisted like living things. The main power grid was dead, and the bay was running on a local battery backup. He checked the status on a small wall panel. Twenty one percent. It would last for a while, enough to keep the life support monitor running, but it was a finite resource. Everything was finite.

He felt a pang of thirst, a dry, scraping sensation in his throat. He found a small hydration station and filled a cup with purified water. It was lukewarm and tasted of metal, but it was the best thing he had ever tasted. As he drank, his mind began to work, a familiar cascade of logic and procedure. Survival was a sequence of problems to be solved.

Problem one: Immediate sustenance. He rummaged through the medbay’s emergency supply lockers. He found a box of nutrient paste tubes and several packs of water purification tablets. It was bland, utilitarian, but it was fuel. It would keep them going.

Problem two: The larger plan. The communications array. His datapad lay on the console where he had left it, its screen a beacon in the gloom. He picked it up and sat back down in the chair, pulling it close to his chest like a holy text. He had traced a route, but a line on a map was a very different thing from a journey through a dead, broken space station. He needed specifics. He needed a list.

His fingers flew across the screen, his technician's mind finding comfort in the familiar act of creating order.

First, mobility. Jake could not walk. Milo looked over at the heavy medical gurney in the corner. It was their only option. But it was bulky. The service shafts and maintenance tunnels he had planned to use would be too narrow. He would have to find a different route, one wide enough for the gurney. That meant main corridors. And main corridors were more likely to be blocked by debris or sealed by blast doors. Which led to his next point.

Second, tools. His standard technician’s kit was in his belt pouch, but it was for delicate work, for circuit boards and data ports. To get through a sealed blast door, he would need something more persuasive. A plasma cutter. A hydraulic spreader. A portable power source strong enough to run them. All of that would be in Engineering, or one of the maintenance workshops.

Third, environment. The station's life support was a ghost. It worked here, in the medbay, and it might work in other sealed sections. But he could not count on it. A single hull breach, a single failed pressure door, and a corridor could be a vacuum. He needed vac-suits. For both of him. And they needed to have power.

Fourth, navigation. The datapad was his guide, but its battery was not infinite. He needed a more permanent solution. He could download the schematics to a portable data chip, or better yet, find a printed schematic somewhere. Redundancy was the key to survival.

The list grew, a litany of needs. Power cells. Cutting torches. Spare parts. Medical supplies to replenish what he had used. A weapon. The thought made his stomach clench. He was not a soldier. He had never even held a pulse rifle. But the creature that had attacked them, the thing of twisted metal and sharp edges, was still out there somewhere. He had seen one. Were there more? The possibility was a cold knot in his gut.

He had to leave the medbay. He had to leave Jake.

The thought was terrifying. The medbay was safe. It was known. Outside was a vast, dark unknown, filled with potential horrors. He looked at Jake’s peaceful face. Staying here was a slow death. The battery would fail. The food would run out. Leaving was a risk, but it was the only one that came with a sliver of hope.

"I'll be back," he whispered, the words sounding small and foolish in the quiet room. He stood, his decision made. He would make a preliminary run. A scouting and supply mission. He would go to the nearest maintenance workshop, just two levels up. According to the schematic, it was Sector Gamma’s primary tool depot. It was a good first objective.

He checked Jake’s monitor one last time. All green. Steady. He found a spare datapad on a charging station, a low-end model, and typed a quick, clumsy note. ‘Went to get tools. Be right back. Don’t go anywhere.’ He smiled grimly at the absurdity of the last sentence and placed the datapad on the small table next to the bed, its screen glowing faintly. It was a silly, sentimental gesture, but it made him feel a little less like he was abandoning his friend.

He clipped his own datapad to his belt, secured his small tool pouch, and took a deep, steadying breath. He walked to the medbay door. The control panel was green. He pressed it. With a soft hiss of equalizing pressure, the door slid open, revealing a corridor of absolute blackness. The emergency lights from the medbay spilled out a few feet, a fragile pool of safety, but beyond that was nothing.

He hesitated. The silence outside was different. It was not the contained quiet of the medbay. It was vast, ancient, and hungry. He took a single step out, and the door hissed shut behind him, plunging him into darkness.

For a panicked moment, he was blind, his heart hammering against his ribs. He fumbled for his datapad, his fingers clumsy with fear. He turned on its flashlight function. A bright, narrow beam cut through the black, revealing the corridor. The emergency lighting strips on the floor and ceiling were dark. The air was frigid, carrying a faint, acrid smell of burnt wiring and cold metal. Dust motes danced in the beam of his light, the only movement in this frozen world.

His own breathing sounded like a storm in the stillness. He forced himself to move, his boots making soft, shuffling sounds on the metal deck plating. He kept the light beam low, scanning the floor ahead of him. The schematics had shown a clear path, but schematics did not show the chaos of a catastrophe.

He passed crew quarters, their doors sealed. One was slightly ajar. He swept his light inside. He saw an overturned table, a scattered deck of cards, a datapad lying face down on the floor. A scene of everyday life, violently interrupted. He felt a profound sense of trespassing, of walking through a graveyard. He was the ghost here.

He reached the first intersection. According to the map on his datapad, he needed to take a service lift up two levels. The lift was, as he expected, dead. The call button was dark and unresponsive. But next to it was a maintenance hatch. A small, square panel with a simple mechanical latch. He pried it open with a screwdriver from his kit. It revealed a ladder, disappearing upwards into a black, vertical shaft.

The climb was strenuous. The rungs of the ladder were cold and slick with a thin film of condensation. He climbed with the datapad clenched between his teeth, its beam pointing down, creating a dizzying, shifting circle of light on the rungs below. The shaft was narrow, and the sounds of his exertion echoed strangely, seeming to come from all directions at once. The scrape of his boots, the rasp of his breathing, the soft jangle of his tools. He felt like an insect crawling through the station’s metallic guts.

After what felt like an eternity, he reached the hatch for his destination level. He pushed it open and scrambled out into another corridor, panting. This one had intermittent emergency lights, red strobes that flashed on and off in a slow, hypnotic rhythm. The effect was deeply unsettling, making the shadows jump and twitch with every pulse of light.

The maintenance workshop was at the end of the hall. Its door was heavier, reinforced. The control panel was smashed, a spiderweb of cracks across the dark screen. This was his first real test. Brute force was not his way. He knelt and examined the door’s mechanisms. He could see the magnetic locks, powerful electromagnets that held the door sealed. With no power, they were just heavy blocks of metal. But they had a manual override. It was a safety feature, designed for emergencies like this. A small port, no bigger than his thumb, was hidden beneath a hinged cover. It required a specialized key.

He did not have the key. But his datapad had the station’s full technical specifications. He looked up the lock mechanism. The key was not electronic. It was mechanical, a specific shape designed to trip a series of tumblers inside the lock. He studied the diagram. It was complex, but it was just a machine. He opened his tool kit. He pulled out two long, thin metal probes. He had used them a hundred times to reseat microscopic circuits on logic boards. Now, he would use them to pick a lock.

He inserted the probes into the port, the flashlight from his datapad held steady in his other hand. He felt the internal tumblers, tiny metal pins. It was like surgery in the dark. He pushed one pin up, held it with the first probe, and used the second to feel for the next. The red lights in the corridor flashed, on, off, on, off. His hands were steady. This was familiar territory. A puzzle of metal and mechanics. He was not afraid now. He was focused.

There was a soft click. Then another. He worked patiently, feeling his way through the mechanism. He could feel the tension in the lock changing. After a minute that stretched into ten, he felt the final tumbler slide into place. There was a loud, resonant thud as the magnetic clamps disengaged. He pulled his probes out, a smile touching his lips for the first time in what felt like days. He had done it.

He pushed the heavy door. It scraped open a few inches, then stopped. Something was blocking it. He pushed harder, grunting with the effort, and managed to open it just wide enough to slip through.

Inside, he found the source of the blockage. A heavy tool cart had been knocked over, spilling its contents across the floor. The workshop itself was a state of ordered chaos. Tools were laid out on workbenches next to half-disassembled service drones. Diagnostic screens showed frozen lines of code. It was as if the technicians had simply vanished mid-task.

But it was everything he had hoped for. He saw a charging rack with several large, portable power cells. He checked them with a diagnostic tool from his pouch. Three of them still held more than ninety percent of their charge. He hefted one. It was heavy, but manageable.

He found a tool locker against the far wall. It was unlocked. Inside was a treasure trove. A full-sized plasma cutter, its power pack still attached. A set of hydraulic spreaders. A comprehensive tool roll that dwarfed his own small kit. He felt a surge of something like joy. These were the keys. These were the tools that could carve a path through this metal tomb.

He gathered what he could carry. He found a sturdy canvas bag and loaded it with one of the power cells and a selection of hand tools. The plasma cutter was too large for the bag. He would have to carry it. It was heavy, awkward, its weight a promise of both salvation and struggle.

As he was about to leave, his light fell on a datapad sitting on a workbench, next to a cold mug of coffee. It was open to a personal comm log. He knew he should not look, but he could not stop himself. The last message was a video file, unsent. The thumbnail showed a smiling woman and two young children.

He stood there for a long time, the weight of the plasma cutter digging into his hand. These were not just empty corridors and silent rooms. This was a place where people had lived. People with families, people who drank coffee and sent messages to their loved ones. He was walking through the echoes of their lives. The realization settled on him, colder and heavier than any fear. He was not just fighting for himself and Jake. He was a witness. He was the last one left to remember.

He turned off the datapad's screen, leaving the family to their frozen, smiling moment. He could not carry their memories for them, not all of them, but he could carry the responsibility of his own survival.

He shifted the heavy plasma cutter to his other shoulder and slipped back through the door, pulling it shut behind him. The thud of the lock engaging echoed in the pulsing red corridor. The journey back was harder. He was weighed down by tools and by thoughts. Every shadow seemed to hold a memory, every flicker of red light a warning. His anxiety for Jake returned, a sharp, insistent pang. How long had he been gone? An hour? Two? It felt like a lifetime.

He clambered back down the service ladder, the canvas bag banging against his back, the cutter’s metal casing cold against his neck. The darkness of the shaft was somehow less threatening this time. It was just a space to be traversed, another problem to be solved.

When he finally reached the medbay level, he almost ran down the corridor. He fumbled for the door panel, his hands shaking slightly. The door hissed open.

The pool of light from inside was welcoming, a haven. And in the center of it, on the bed, Jake was stirring. His eyes were half open, unfocused. His lips moved, but no sound came out.

Milo dropped the cutter and the bag with a clatter and rushed to his side. "Jake? Jake, can you hear me?"

Jake’s eyes tried to focus on him. A flicker of recognition. A weak, raspy sound escaped his throat. "Milo..."

Tears pricked Milo's eyes, hot and sudden. He grabbed Jake’s hand. It was warm. "I'm here, buddy," he said, his voice thick. "I'm here. I’ve got you."

Jake’s eyes drifted shut again, but his breathing remained steady. He had spoken. He was still in there.

Milo sank into the chair, the exhaustion hitting him like a physical blow. He looked at the tools he had brought back, the heavy power cell, the gleaming metal of the plasma cutter. They were no longer just tools. They were the first few words of an answer. The first steps on a long, dark road. He had left the sanctuary and returned, and in doing so, he had pushed back the darkness, if only by a few feet. He was tired, he was scared, and he was utterly alone, but for the first time since the station died, he felt the undeniable, powerful spark of hope.

The hope was a fragile thing. A flickering candle in a hurricane. Milo sat there, watching the slow, rhythmic rise and fall of Jake's chest, and felt the weight of that flame in his own heart. It was his to protect. His to feed. If it went out, everything went out with it. He forced himself to stand, his joints cracking in protest. The adrenaline that had carried him through the darkened corridors was gone, leaving behind a deep, aching weariness. But there was work to do. Always more work to do.

First, Jake. He leaned over the bed, his shadow falling across his friend's pale face. Jake’s breathing was shallow, but it was even. That was something. Milo gently checked the bandage on his head. It was clean enough. He had no fresh supplies to change it with anyway. He looked at the medbay's main console. It was still running on the dregs of the station's emergency power, its screen displaying a low power warning in pulsing amber letters. The diagnostic functions were limited. It could show a heart rate, a respiration pattern, a body temperature. Vital signs, but not the whole story. Not the why. Not the what next.

Milo’s eyes fell upon the heavy power cell he had lugged back from the cargo bay. It sat on the floor, a block of dark metal and complex connectors. It was a standard auxiliary unit, designed to power field equipment or supplement a primary system in an emergency. The medbay had its own auxiliary power input. He had seen it. A recessed panel near the floor, behind the main console. He had never thought he would need to use it. No one ever thought they would be the ones to live through the end of the world.

He dragged the cell across the floor, the metal scraping against the composite decking. It was heavier than he remembered. He knelt by the panel, his fingers tracing the outline of the access hatch. It was sealed tight. A simple magnetic lock, but it required a maintenance key. A key he did not have. He could spend hours, days, searching for it in the labyrinthine station. Or he could use the other tool.

He looked at the plasma cutter. It was a beast. A tool of controlled destruction, designed for slicing through hull plating and stubborn support beams. Using it on a delicate electronics panel felt like performing surgery with a chainsaw. One slip, one miscalculation, and he could fry the entire medbay console. He could sever the very systems that were keeping Jake alive. The risk was immense. But the alternative was worse. The alternative was sitting here, watching Jake fade away, while the answer sat inert on the floor.

He took a deep breath. He had to try. He had to.

He positioned the cutter, its nozzle just millimeters from the edge of the access panel. He adjusted the power setting, dialing it down to the absolute minimum. He had seen engineers use these for fine work, etching lines with a surgeon's precision. He was no engineer. His hand trembled. He steadied it with his other, pressing his wrist against the wall for support. The hum of the cutter's pre-ignition cycle was a high, whining sound in the quiet room. It set his teeth on edge. He squeezed the trigger.

A brilliant, needle thin jet of white hot plasma erupted from the nozzle. The air hissed and smelled of ozone. He moved the cutter slowly, painstakingly, along the seam of the panel. The metal glowed cherry red, then white, then parted with a soft, sizzling sound. Molten droplets spat onto the floor, cooling instantly into tiny, misshapen beads of metal. He was sweating, his focus absolute. The world narrowed to the tip of the plasma beam and the line he was tracing. He cut one side. Then the top. Then the other side. He left the bottom hinged, not wanting the panel to drop onto the delicate wiring behind it.

He switched off the cutter. The silence that rushed back in was deafening. He set the tool down, his arm aching from the tension. He gently pried the panel open. The metal was still hot, searing his fingertips, but he barely noticed. Behind it was a nest of wires and a single, large, multi pin connector. The auxiliary input. It looked intact. Untouched. He had done it.

The next part was almost as nerve wracking. He maneuvered the heavy power cell into place. He lined up the thick cable's connector with the input socket. It was a clumsy, awkward fit. He had to lie on his side, his head pressed against the cold floor, to see what he was doing. He pushed. It resisted. He pushed harder, wiggling it, feeling for the moment it would align. There was a solid, reassuring click as the connector seated itself.

For a moment, nothing happened. Milo's heart sank. Had he broken it? Had he come all this way for nothing? He stared at the dead console, the amber low power warning still blinking its sad, steady rhythm. Failure. Cold and complete.

Then, a flicker.

The lights in the medbay, which had been a dim, functional white, suddenly blinked out, plunging the room into the hellish red of the emergency corridor lighting that bled under the door. Milo’s breath caught in his throat. But a second later, they flashed back on, brighter than before. A clean, sterile, brilliant white that banished every shadow. A series of soft chimes echoed through the room as systems began to reboot. The main console screen went black, then lit up with a corporate logo, followed by a stream of diagnostic text scrolling too fast to read. The low power warning was gone. In its place, the auxiliary power icon glowed a steady, healthy green.

A deep, powerful hum vibrated up through the floor, the sound of life returning to the machine. Milo pushed himself up, a wide, incredulous grin spreading across his face. It worked. It had actually worked. The hope inside him, once a candle, was now a roaring fire.

He stumbled over to the console. The home screen was now fully accessible. He tapped the "Patient Diagnostics" icon. A submenu appeared. He selected "Full Spectrum Scan." A new screen loaded, displaying a rotating, three dimensional wireframe of a human body. "Initiating scan," a calm, synthesized female voice announced from the console's speaker. "Please ensure patient remains stationary."

A low hum emanated from the medical bed itself. A faint blue light passed over Jake’s body, from head to toe, like a slow, silent wave. Milo watched, his hands clenched, as data began to populate the screen. Red and yellow indicators flared up on the wireframe model, clustering around Jake's head and torso. The scan took less than a minute, but it felt like an eternity. When it was finished, the wireframe resolved into a detailed anatomical chart, covered in notations and warnings.

Milo leaned closer, trying to decipher the medical terminology. His heart hammered against his ribs. The news was not good.

Cranial trauma. Subdural hematoma. Significant intracranial pressure. Three fractured ribs. A punctured lung. Internal bleeding, slow but persistent. The list went on. It was a miracle Jake was alive at all. The automated system had administered a coagulant and a broad spectrum painkiller when he was first brought in, which had likely saved him. But these were temporary measures. The intracranial pressure was the most critical threat. The scanner projected a timeline. Without intervention, it would become fatal within forty eight hours.

The hope that had soared so high crashed back to earth. He had power. He had information. But the information was a death sentence.

"Recommended treatment," the synthesized voice said, "immediate surgical intervention to relieve cranial pressure. Main surgical suite required. Prognosis with intervention: 47% survival. Prognosis without intervention: 0%."

Surgical intervention. Milo looked at his own shaking hands. He was a junior botanist. He dissected plant tissue, not human brains. He felt a wave of nausea. He backed away from the console and sank into the chair, burying his face in his hands. The bright, clean light of the medbay suddenly felt accusatory. The hum of the machines sounded like a ticking clock. Forty eight hours.

He stayed like that for a long time, lost in a spiral of despair. He had done all this just to be told, in perfect, clinical detail, exactly how his friend was going to die. It was a cruel joke. The universe was laughing at him.

But then he thought of the family on the datapad. Frozen in their last happy moment. They had no choices left. He thought of the empty corridors, the silent rooms. He was the witness. He was the one who had to remember. And he thought of Jake, who had spoken his name. Who was still in there, fighting. He could not give up. He would not.

He lifted his head. Zero percent. Forty seven percent. The numbers swam in his vision. Forty seven percent was not zero. It was a chance. It was a sliver of a chance, but it was there. He would not be the one to let it slip away.

He went back to the console. "Surgical intervention." He could not do that. Not the way the computer meant. But what did relieving cranial pressure actually entail? He accessed the station's medical database, a vast library of procedures and information. He typed in his search, his fingers clumsy on the touch screen. He read through dense articles, his mind racing to absorb the information. He learned about burr holes, small holes drilled into the skull to allow fluid to drain and relieve pressure. It was a crude, ancient procedure, but a life saving one in emergencies. The station's surgical suite would have an advanced laser drill for such a thing. A tool of microsurgery precision. He did not have that.

He looked at the plasma cutter.

The thought was insane. Utterly, terrifyingly insane. To use that tool, that monster, on his friend's skull. It was a tool for brute force, not for delicate work. But he had managed to cut the panel. He had controlled it. Could he control it enough? Could he modify it? Reduce the power output even further? Create a beam so fine, so focused, that it could do what needed to be done?

He did not know. But it was the only option he had. It was the forty seven percent.

First, he needed supplies. The scanner had listed them. A sterile field kit. A bone sealant. More coagulants. Specific antibiotics to prevent infection. The medbay's small pharmacy cabinet was nearly empty. He had used the last of the useful supplies on Jake already. But the station had a central medical storage facility. Pharmacy Prime, it was called. It was on the other side of the habitat ring. A long, dark walk away.

He looked at Jake again. His face was so still. Milo reached out and touched his arm. "I'll be back," he whispered. "I promise."

This time, the preparation was different. It was not a desperate scramble. It was a mission. He had a target. He had a list. He found another canvas bag in a storage locker and began to fill it. He took a portable datapad and downloaded the list of required medicines and the station map. Pharmacy Prime was two sectors away. Sector Gamma and Sector Delta. He was in Sector Beta. The route would take him through the main habitat spine, past the crew quarters, past the recreation deck. Places where people had lived.

He checked the plasma cutter again. Its power indicator showed it was still nearly full. Good. He would need it. The internal doors between sectors were designed to automatically seal in a full scale emergency. They would be locked down. Hard sealed.

He slung the bag over his shoulder. He picked up the cutter. It felt different in his hands now. Not just a tool, but a weapon. A key. A terrible, necessary instrument. He stood at the medbay door, looking back at Jake, lying in the pool of clean, white light. He was leaving the sanctuary again, this time to go deeper into the dark. But he was no longer just running from the shadows. He was running toward a solution.

He pressed the door panel. The door hissed open, revealing the pulsing red gloom of the corridor. The contrast was stark. The safe, bright world of the medbay versus the dark, dead world outside. He hesitated for only a second. He had a clock now. Less than forty eight hours. Every second mattered.

He stepped out into the red. The door hissed shut behind him, plunging him back into the familiar twilight. The hum of the medbay was a faint, muffled promise behind the sealed door. He was alone again. But he was not lost. He had a map. He had a purpose. He turned and began to walk, his footsteps a steady, rhythmic beat in the silence.

The journey through Sector Beta was familiar territory. He passed the service ladder he had climbed, the junction where he had first seen the living quarters. He moved with more confidence now, his light cutting a clean path through the oppressive red. He reached the main concourse of the habitat spine, a wide, sweeping corridor that curved gently into the distance, following the rotation of the station. Here, the emergency lights were spaced farther apart, creating deep pools of shadow between them. The ceiling was high, vaulted, with dead sky-lights that once would have shown a simulated sky. Now they were just black rectangles.

He passed the entrance to the mess hall. The doors were ajar. He could see overturned tables and scattered trays, frozen in the moment of chaos. He did not look for long. He kept walking. Every doorway was a ghost story he did not have time to read.

He reached the end of Sector Beta. A massive bulkhead door blocked the way. SECTOR GAMMA, the lettering above it read. As he had expected, it was sealed. A heavy steel blast door, with a thick locking mechanism visible in its center. A small panel next to the door was dark. No power. No way to override it electronically.

This was what the cutter was for.

He set his bag down and hefted the tool. This was different from the small access panel. This was a foot of reinforced alloy. He cranked the power setting up, much higher this time. The whine of the pre-ignition cycle was louder, more aggressive. He braced himself and pulled the trigger.

The plasma beam that erupted was thick and furious. It hit the door with a deafening roar, a sound like a continuous thunderclap. The air filled with the shriek of tortured metal and the acrid smell of vaporized steel. Sparks rained down around him, a deadly, beautiful firework display. He was cutting not a fine line, but a gaping hole, right through the center of the locking mechanism. The heat was intense, washing over him in waves. He had to squint against the blinding light. The cutter bucked in his hands, fighting against him as it devoured the metal.

It took minutes. Long, grueling, arm-shaking minutes. Finally, with a groan of protest, the lock gave way. He had melted a hole a foot wide straight through the mechanism. He switched off the cutter, his ears ringing in the sudden silence. The air was thick with smoke. Through the jagged, glowing hole in the door, he could see the red corridor of Sector Gamma stretching out beyond.

He used the cutter’s casing to push the mangled remains of the lock out of the way, then squeezed his body through the opening. The edges of the cut metal were still glowing, radiating a fierce heat. He was through.

Sector Gamma felt different. Colder. The air was still and held a different quality of silence. Here, the damage seemed worse. A ceiling panel had fallen, trailing a shower of wires. A side corridor was completely blocked by debris. It was as if the station's death throes had been more violent here.

He checked his datapad. Pharmacy Prime was at the far end of Sector Delta. He had to cross all of Gamma first. He pushed onward, his footsteps echoing in the vast, empty spaces. He passed the recreation deck. A zero G squash court was visible through a large window, the ball floating motionlessly in the exact center of the room. He passed the library, its door shattered, revealing rows and rows of datapad charging stations, all dark.

It was then that he heard it.

It was not the hum of the station. It was not the echo of his own movements. It was a sound. A distinct, metallic scrape. It came from somewhere ahead, down the long, curving corridor.

Milo froze. Every muscle in his body went rigid. He held his breath, straining to listen. The sound had been faint, but it had been undeniable. He was not alone.

He quickly ducked into the doorway of the library, pressing his back against the wall, his heart pounding a frantic, wild rhythm. He peered out into the corridor. The red lights pulsed, casting long, moving shadows. The corridor was empty. Silent.

Had he imagined it? The strain, the exhaustion, the fear... was his mind playing tricks on him? He waited. One minute. Two. The silence was absolute, broken only by the sound of his own blood roaring in his ears. It must have been his imagination. The station settling. A piece of loose debris shifting. That had to be it.

He took a slow, calming breath and was about to step back out into the corridor.

And then he heard it again. Scrape. Clank. Closer this time.

Scrape. Clank. Closer this time.

The sound was brutally real. It was not a figment of a stressed mind. It was heavy. It was wrong. Milo’s breath hitched in his throat. He pressed himself further into the library’s doorway, trying to make himself smaller, trying to merge with the shadows. The rhythmic pulse of the red emergency lights was a slow, agonizing heartbeat counting down to something terrible. He risked another look, a sliver of his vision angled down the long, curving corridor.

At first, there was only the moving darkness, the shifting geometry of the station’s architecture under the crimson strobes. Then, a shape began to resolve itself from the deeper shadows at the corridor’s bend. It was not human. It was low to the ground, maybe a meter high, and wide. It moved with a lurching, uneven gait. One side seemed to glide smoothly on hidden wheels, but the other side dragged, creating the scraping sound. The clank was the impact of a jointed metal limb, a kind of articulated arm, hitting the deck plating with every lurch.

It was a station maintenance drone. A 'Scrubber' unit, designed for heavy duty cleaning and waste reclamation. Or at least, it had been. This one was horribly damaged. A large portion of its upper casing was caved in, exposing a chaotic nest of severed wires that sparked erratically. The arm that should have held a polishing buffer or a suction hose was instead a jagged mess of twisted metal, ending in what looked like three sharp, pincer like claws. Its optical sensor, a single large red eye in the center of its chassis, swept back and forth, a baleful, unblinking cyclops. It was not cleaning. It was hunting.

Milo’s blood ran cold. He had seen what these things could do to compacted waste blocks. They were designed with immense hydraulic strength. A malfunctioning unit, its safety protocols fried, was a mindless, powerful killing machine. It continued its slow, inexorable advance down the center of the corridor. Scrape. Clank. Scrape. Clank. It seemed to be following a preprogrammed path, but its sensor eye was constantly scanning, searching.

Milo held perfectly still. He was a statue carved from fear. The drone drew level with the library entrance. Its lurching passage was directly in front of him, no more than ten feet away. He could feel the vibration through the soles of his boots. He could smell the ozone from its sparking internals. The big red eye swept across the corridor. It passed over his doorway once, then swept back. This time, it stopped. The eye locked onto the doorway. Onto him.

There was no thought process. No analysis. Just a pure, electric bolt of terror that galvanized him into motion. He threw himself backward, deeper into the library, just as the drone’s damaged arm shot out. The pincer claws slammed into the doorframe where his head had been a second before, gouging deep furrows into the metal. The sound was a deafening shriek of tortured steel.

Milo scrambled on his hands and knees over a pile of scattered datapads, their screens shattered. The library was a wreck. Shelving units had toppled like dominoes, creating a maze of metal and debris. The drone was not subtle. It simply pushed its way into the room, its wide body bulldozing a rack of charging stations out of its path. The noise was a cacophony of groaning metal and shattering plastic.

He had to get away. He lurched to his feet and ran, his breath coming in ragged, painful gasps. He vaulted over a low, overturned reading table, his boot catching on a trailing cable, sending him stumbling. He caught his balance, his heart threatening to explode from his chest. The drone was coming. It was faster than its lurching gait suggested. Its smooth side allowed it to pivot and accelerate with terrifying speed.

He ducked behind a tall, still standing server bank that housed the library’s central index. He pressed his back against the cool metal, trying to control his breathing, trying to silence the frantic hammering in his ribs. The scrape and clank grew louder, echoing horribly in the confined space of the library. It was methodically moving through the wreckage, its red eye sweeping, its damaged arm twitching. It was searching.

Milo knew he could not stay here. The server bank was cover, but it was not a fortress. The drone would find him. He needed a plan. He needed an escape. He risked a peek around the edge of the server. The drone was across the room, its claws intermittently snapping as it pushed a pile of debris aside. It seemed to be following some broken logic, clearing a path, its original programming twisted into a destructive parody.

His eyes darted around the room, desperately seeking a way out. The main entrance was blocked by the drone itself. He scanned the walls. There. On the far side of the library, partially obscured by a fallen shelving unit, was a service hatch. It was small, probably leading to a maintenance crawlspace or a Jefferies tube. It was his only chance.

But it was on the other side of the room. He would have to cross the open space. He would have to expose himself.

The drone suddenly stopped its methodical clearing. It went utterly still. The only sound was the faint hum of its power core and the sizzle of its exposed wiring. Its red eye began to sweep the room in a more rapid, deliberate pattern. It knew something was here. It was actively hunting him now.

Milo’s mind raced. He could not outrun it in a straight line. He could not fight it. He had the plasma cutter, but it was heavy, unwieldy, and took time to power up. By the time it was hot enough to even mark the drone’s chassis, those claws would have torn him apart. He had to be smarter.

He looked down at his feet. The floor was littered with broken datapads. An idea, desperate and thin, began to form. He crouched down, his hands shaking, and picked up one of the thicker pads. It was a heavy, older model. He weighed it in his hand.

He waited. He watched the drone's sweeping red eye. It moved left. It moved right. Its pattern was predictable. It was a machine. It followed its programming, however corrupted. He waited for the eye to sweep to the far left of its arc.

Now.

Milo took a deep breath and hurled the datapad with all his might. He did not throw it at the drone. He threw it high and deep into the far corner of the library, behind another toppled shelf. The datapad flew in a perfect arc and crashed into a metal wall with a loud, echoing clang.

For a heart stopping second, nothing happened. Then, the drone’s head snapped toward the sound. Its red eye glowed with a sudden intensity. It emitted a series of sharp, digital chirps, a sound of discovery, and immediately began to move toward the noise. Scrape. Clank. Scrape. Clank. It moved with renewed purpose, its claws twitching in anticipation.

This was his chance.

Milo broke from cover. He did not run. He moved in a low crouch, staying behind the lines of fallen shelves, using the wreckage as a shield. He was silent. He was a shadow. The drone was a good thirty yards away, its back to him, completely focused on the source of the sound it had heard. It reached the corner and began to attack the wall with its claws, tearing long strips of paneling away, searching for the anomaly it had detected.

Milo reached the fallen shelf blocking the service hatch. He clambered over it, the metal groaning under his weight. He prayed the drone did not hear. He dropped to the floor on the other side, his gaze locked on the hatch. It was a simple wheel lock mechanism. He grabbed the wheel with both hands and pulled.

It was stiff. Decades of disuse had fused it tight. He grunted, pulling with all his strength, his muscles screaming in protest. The sound of the drone destroying the far corner of the room was his only cover. "Come on," he hissed through gritted teeth. "Come on!" He gave one last, desperate heave, putting his entire body weight into it.

With a painful, groaning screech, the wheel began to turn. It was slow, fighting him every inch of the way. Quarter turn. Half turn. The sound of his effort was drowned out by the drone’s continuing rampage. Finally, with a dull thud, the locking bolts retracted. Milo did not waste a second. He grabbed the edge of the hatch and pulled it open. It swung inward, revealing a dark, narrow shaft. The air that wafted out was cold and smelled of dust and ozone.

He scrambled inside, feet first, his back scraping against the upper edge of the opening. He found purchase for his feet on a rung ladder set into the wall of the shaft. He was about to pull the hatch closed when he paused. He looked back across the ruined library. The drone was still there, tearing at the wall, a mindless engine of destruction. He had escaped it, for now.

He pulled the heavy hatch closed. The sound of the drone was instantly muffled, reduced to a distant, rhythmic banging. He was plunged into absolute darkness. The silence was a physical pressure on his eardrums. He was alone again, in a tight, black space. He fumbled for the light on his wrist cuff. A small, weak beam cut through the darkness, illuminating a narrow, vertical maintenance shaft. Ladder rungs disappeared up into the blackness and down into the void.

He took a moment, his back pressed against the cold metal of the hatch, his head resting against a rung. His body trembled with adrenaline and exhaustion. The close call had left him drained, his nerves frayed to the breaking point. He needed to get his bearings. He needed to remember why he was doing this.

He pulled out his datapad. The screen flickered to life, its glow a small comfort in the oppressive dark. He ignored the map of the station for a moment and swiped to a saved image. It was a picture of a little girl, no older than eight, with his same dark hair but with her mother’s bright, mischievous eyes. She was smiling, a gap where a tooth had recently fallen out. Elara.

The image was a stab to his heart, both painful and fortifying. He could almost hear her laugh, a sound he had not heard in weeks. She was back in their small habitat in Sector Beta, in the infirmary, her body fighting a viral phage that was sweeping through the station’s lower decks. The standard antivirals were useless. The station’s auto docs had analyzed it, synthesized a report. There was only one thing that could stop it, a custom synthesized retroviral agent. And the only supply, the only machine capable of formulating it, was in Pharmacy Prime. On the other side of the station.

He had promised her he would be back. He had promised.

That promise was the fuel that burned inside him. It was more powerful than his fear, stronger than his exhaustion. He looked at her smiling face one last time, memorizing it, branding it onto his soul. He switched back to the station map.

The maintenance shaft, according to the schematic, ran parallel to the main corridor of Sector Gamma. It was a shortcut, of a sort. A dangerous, claustrophobic shortcut, but it bypassed the main thoroughfare where the drone was now patrolling. He could follow it for a few hundred meters and hopefully find another access hatch that would put him closer to the Sector Delta checkpoint.

He clipped the datapad back to his belt and took a deep breath of the stale, metallic air. He had a choice. Up or down. According to the map, the corridor sloped gently downwards toward Sector Delta. So down it was.

He began to climb, his movements slow and deliberate. The rungs were cold and slick with a thin film of condensation. The beam from his wrist light bobbed in front of him, revealing only the next few feet of the shaft. It was a disorienting, endless descent into nothing. The only sounds were his own breathing and the soft scrape of his boots on the metal rungs.

He descended for what felt like an eternity. Ten meters. Twenty. Thirty. The shaft was perfectly uniform, a monotonous tube of black steel. He passed other hatches, their wheels rusted solid. He tried one, just in case, but it would not budge. He had no choice but to continue downward.

The air grew colder. He could see his breath fogging in the small cone of light. A new sound began to reach him. A faint, low hum. It was not the ambient noise of the station. This was different. It grew louder as he descended, a deep, resonant thrum that vibrated through the ladder rungs and into his bones.

He stopped, listening. The humming was coming from below. He pointed his light downward, but the beam was swallowed by the darkness. He kept climbing down, his curiosity now mixed with a fresh dose of apprehension. The hum grew into a powerful drone, the kind of sound made by massive power converters or environmental systems.

After another twenty meters, his light caught something. The bottom of the shaft was not solid. It was a grate. And below the grate, there was a faint, pulsing blue light. The humming was emanating from down there.

He reached the last rung and carefully lowered himself onto the metal grate. It felt solid enough. He knelt, angling his wrist light down through the openings. What he saw made him stop breathing.

He was looking down into a massive cavern, a vast, open space that was not on his datapad’s schematic. This was a sub level, a place for the station’s guts. Huge, thick coolant pipes, frosted with ice, ran like frozen rivers across the floor. And in the center of the cavern, the source of the hum and the light, was a colossal sphere of machinery. It was the station’s primary fusion core. Or what was left of it.

It was supposed to be shielded, contained within layers of lead and concrete. But the housing was cracked wide open, like a shattered egg. The core itself, a roiling ball of contained plasma, pulsed with a sick, unstable blue light. Arcs of raw energy, like miniature lightning, licked up from its surface, striking the walls of the cavern and leaving glowing scars on the metal. The station was not just damaged. It was dying. This core was critical. It was a bomb, waiting for the right moment to go off.

He understood now. The power fluctuations, the failing systems, the life support flickering in and out. It was all tied to this. The catastrophe that had crippled the station had started here. He was staring into the abyss, into the very heart of the disaster.

He had to get away from it. The radiation down here must be staggering. He looked up, back the way he came. Climbing all the way back up to the library, to face the drone again, felt like a suicide mission. He checked his datapad again, his fingers fumbling. He zoomed in on his location. The schematic flickered, trying to reconcile his position with its known data. Then, a new pathway resolved on the screen. A dotted line. A tertiary service conduit, leading from the core chamber. It was marked ‘Hazardous: High Energy Particulates’. But it led, eventually, to a junction that connected with the lower levels of Sector Delta.

It was a gamble. A terrible, awful gamble. Stay here and be cooked by radiation, go back and be torn apart by the drone, or push forward into an unknown hazard.

He looked at the picture of Elara one more time. Her smiling face.

There was no choice at all.

He found the access panel for the conduit on the far side of the core chamber, just as the map indicated. It was a long, terrifying walk across a narrow catwalk suspended over the chasm. The heat from the failing core washed over him, and the low hum was a physical presence that shook his teeth. He did not look down. He just kept his eyes fixed on the panel, his hand gripping the railing until his knuckles were white.

The panel was unlocked. He wrenched it open and dove through, pulling it shut behind him. He was in another tight tunnel, this one circular and ribbed with power cables. The air tasted sharp, electric. But he was through. He was moving forward.

He ran, half stumbling, down the conduit. The path was dark, lit only by his small wrist light. He ran until the hum of the core faded behind him, replaced once more by the sound of his own desperate breathing. He ran toward Sector Delta. Toward Pharmacy Prime. Toward his daughter. He did not know what new horrors lay ahead. He only knew that he could not stop. He would not stop.
