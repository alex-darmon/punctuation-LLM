He stood before a large control panel of his own design, a formidable array of bakelite knobs, heavy-duty switches, and glowing meters. “I am going to introduce a low-power carrier wave first. I need you to tell me how the oscillator responds. I am looking for its baseline frequency, the pure note beneath the noise.”

“Be ready,” the Traveller said. “Applying an external field could exacerbate the fracture.”

“I am aware of the risk,” Edevane replied curtly. He threw a large knife switch. A deep, resonant hum filled the laboratory, growing in intensity. The lights in the room dimmed significantly, and the vacuum tubes on the amplifier bank glowed a cherry red. On the workbench, the air around the four electromagnets began to shimmer.

“I feel it,” the Traveller said, his form flickering wildly. “It is pushing back. The frequency is… it is high. Terribly high. Far beyond what you could generate.”

“I am not trying to match it,” Edevane grunted, his hand steady on a large rheostat. “I am trying to find a harmonic. A lower octave it can lock onto.” He slowly turned the dial. The hum from the equipment rose in pitch. The waveform on the oscilloscope began to change, the jagged peaks stretching and contorting.

Suddenly, a pen on his desk lifted into the air, hovered for a second, and then shot across the room, embedding itself in the plaster of the far wall.

“Doctor!” the Traveller cried out.

“Almost there!” Edevane shouted over the noise, his eyes glued to the screen. The chaotic line was beginning to show the faintest hint of a repeating pattern, a ghost of order within the madness. “It is resonating! I have found a sympathetic frequency!”

He reached for a second switch. “This will engage the primary containment field. If my calculations are wrong, it will likely overload every electrical system in a quarter-mile radius and possibly tear a hole in…” He trailed off. The alternative was erasure. The choice was simple.

He threw the switch.

The world dissolved into a maelstrom of sound and light. A deafening roar, like a thunderclap inside the room, erupted from the workbench. A blinding arc of blue-white energy leaped between the electromagnets, forming a crackling, shimmering cage of pure power around the time machine. The lights in the laboratory exploded in a shower of sparks, plunging the room into a darkness illuminated only by the terrifying energy field and the frantic, strobing light from within the box.

Edevane was thrown back against a bookshelf, the impact knocking the wind out of him. Books and papers rained down around him. Through ringing ears and spots in his vision, he stared at his creation. The energy cage was holding, a violent, beautiful sphere of contained power. And at its center, the box sat silently.

He staggered to his feet, grabbing a torch from a drawer. He shone its beam on the oscilloscope. The screen glowed a faint green in the darkness. The line was no longer a chaotic mess. It was a smooth, perfect sine wave. A pure, clean signal.

The laboratory was a wreck. Smoked glass littered the floor. The air was thick with the smell of ozone. But the oppressive, unnatural chill was gone. The faint, insistent hum of the box was silenced, replaced by the steady, powerful thrum of his own machinery.

He turned the torch beam towards the armchair. The Traveller’s form was clearer, more stable than he had ever seen it. The spectral translucence was gone, replaced by the solidity of a man, albeit one who still shimmered at the edges.

“You did it,” the Traveller breathed, his voice filled with an awe that transcended time and space. “You mad, brilliant, primitive man. You caged a storm with copper and glass.”

Edevane leaned against his desk, his body trembling with adrenaline and exhaustion. He looked at the humming cage of energy, at the tamed impossibility it contained. He had faced the ultimate unknown, not with a weapon or a prayer, but with the ruthless application of logic and a deep, abiding faith in the quantifiable universe.

“The signal is dampened,” Edevane said, his voice hoarse. “The echo they were tracking is contained, for now. But the crystal is still broken. The cage is drawing an immense amount of power. It will not hold forever. A few days at most, before my equipment fails.”

He shone the torch on his grandfather clock. The second hand was moving with perfect, metronomic precision. He had bought them time. He had pushed back the encroaching void.

“A few days is a lifetime,” the Traveller said, standing up from the chair. For the first time, his feet seemed to touch the floor. “It is more than I had. More than you had.” He looked at Edevane, his eyes, now clearly visible, holding a universe of experience and a newfound respect. “You were right, Doctor Edevane. It would have been a terrible waste.”

Edevane gave a weary nod, the grim smile returning to his face. The fear was gone, replaced by a cold, thrilling certainty. The game had changed. He was no longer a potential victim. He was a participant.

“Well then,” Edevane said, clicking off the torch and plunging them back into the semi-darkness of the humming containment field. “Let us not waste it. You need to get home. That means we need to find a way to make one, very specific, very controlled jump. Tell me, how does your machine handle targeted navigation?”

The Traveller’s new solidity did nothing to diminish the ancient, unreadable quality of his gaze. He considered Edevane’s question not as an engineer would, but as a philosopher considers a paradox. “My machine,” he said, his voice a low resonance that seemed to vibrate in the very air of the laboratory, “is not a machine in the way your generator in the cellar is a machine. It does not have levers and gears. It has principles. The primary principle is sympathetic resonance.”

Edevane’s brow furrowed. He picked up a fallen book, a heavy tome on electromagnetic theory, and placed it back on a miraculously intact shelf. “That is not an answer. That is poetry. I need mechanics, principles I can measure and manipulate. How do you steer?”

“I do not steer,” the Traveller corrected gently. “I attune. Imagine the universe not as a vast, empty space with points of interest, but as an infinite ocean of frequencies, a symphony of possibilities. Every location, every moment in time, has its own unique chord, its own song. My vessel, the box as you call it, is the instrument. The crystal is the musician.” He gestured with a hand that still trailed faint wisps of light towards the caged fury in the center of the room. “It listens for a specific song, the song of home, and then it joins the chorus. The journey is not a movement through space, but a changing of the note we are playing.”

Edevane processed this, translating the frustrating metaphor into something he could grasp. “So the crystal is a navigational computer and a propulsion system combined. It targets a destination by matching its own resonance frequency to the destination’s unique spacetime signature.”

“An elegant, if simplistic, summary,” the Traveller allowed. “But you see the problem. The musician has had a catastrophic seizure. The crystal is no longer playing one song. It is screaming every song ever composed, all at once, in a cascade of dissonance. That is the echo they follow. A multiversal shriek of a soul in torment. It is why I was untethered, drifting on the chaotic tides it produced.”

Edevane nodded slowly, his eyes fixed on the humming sphere of energy. The beauty of the sine wave on the oscilloscope was a lie, a carefully constructed illusion. It wasn’t a cure, it was a gag. “Your instrument is broken, and my cage is merely muffling the noise. So targeted navigation is impossible.”

“With that crystal, yes,” the Traveller confirmed. “To use it now would be to fling ourselves randomly into the symphony, a single discordant note in a tempest. We might arrive in the heart of a star, or an age before matter coalesced. The odds are not favorable.”

A heavy silence fell between them, punctuated by the steady thrum of the containment field. The initial triumph was souring into the cold realization of their true predicament. They had traded a swift execution for a slow, lingering death in a prison of Edevane’s own making.

“The power,” Edevane said, his voice flat. He walked over to a bank of heavy ceramic dials and brass gauges. His fingers traced the trembling needle on the primary ammeter. “The draw is astronomical. The cage is a brute force solution. It’s a dam holding back an ocean. But the ocean is rising, and the dam has cracks.” He turned, his face grim in the pulsing glow of the cage. “My generator can sustain this for perhaps two days, three if I bypass every non-essential system in the house and pray the fuel holds. Then the cage fails. The shriek returns. And your friends will find us.”

“They are not my friends,” the Traveller said with a chilling quietness.

“They’ll be at the door all the same,” Edevane countered. He ran a hand through his dishevelled hair. The adrenaline was wearing off, leaving behind a profound, bone deep weariness. But his mind, sharpened by the crisis, refused to rest. It gnawed at the problem, searching for a flaw, a lever, an angle of attack. “We cannot fix the crystal. We cannot use it. We cannot remain here.” He began to pace the narrow clear space between the desk and the fallen shelves. “Every option is a dead end. Therefore, one of my premises must be wrong.” He stopped, his gaze snapping back to the Traveller. “Let us re-examine the first premise. We cannot fix the crystal. Why not? What is it made of?”

The Traveller seemed to anticipate the question. “It is not made, Doctor. Not in the way your glass beakers are made. It is grown. Over eons, in the heart of a gas giant orbiting a neutron star, under pressures and radiation fields you cannot imagine. It is a lattice of spacetime itself, coaxed into a stable, programmable matrix. There is no element on your world’s periodic table that corresponds to its composition.”

Edevane felt a flicker of despair, but he stamped it out with practiced discipline. Logic. Stick to the logic. “Grown. That is a key word. It is not an artifact, it is an organism of sorts. A geological organism. Does it have analogues? Are there other, lesser forms? Primitives of its kind?”

“The universe is vast,” the Traveller mused. “The conditions for its formation are specific, but not unique. There must be others. Perhaps even… here.” For the first time, a flicker of something other than awe or weariness crossed the Traveller’s face. It was a nascent hope, fragile and faint. “With the crystal’s noise silenced, I can… listen. The background radiation of the cosmos is clearer to me now. The symphony is muted, but audible.”

He closed his eyes. The subtle shimmer around his body intensified slightly. The laboratory, with its smells of ozone and burnt wiring, seemed to fade around him. Edevane stood perfectly still, watching, feeling like a primitive man witnessing a shaman commune with the spirits of the world. He had caged a god’s heart, and now the god was attempting to hear the whispers of its kin.

Minutes stretched into an eternity. The only sounds were the thrum of the cage and the steady tick tock of the grandfather clock, a sound Edevane had not even noticed had returned to its normal, comforting rhythm.

“There is… a weakness in the house,” Edevane said suddenly, his voice a low growl of frustration. “The power is fluctuating. The cage’s resonance is unstable.” He did not wait for the Traveller to open his eyes. He turned on his heel and strode out of the laboratory, grabbing a heavy iron wrench from a toolbox by the door. His footsteps were heavy and purposeful on the old wooden stairs leading down into the darkness of the cellar.

The air grew cooler, thick with the scent of damp earth, engine oil, and cold iron. Edevane pulled a chain, and a single bare bulb flickered to life, casting long, dancing shadows across a cavernous space. This was the true heart of his home, the foundation of his work. A massive diesel generator, a beast of iron and copper painted a dull green, sat bolted to the concrete floor. It was a relic from a decommissioned naval vessel, a power plant capable of running a small village. Its low, rhythmic chugging was the house’s true heartbeat. Thick, insulated cables, as thick as a man’s arm, snaked from it into a complex switchboard of knife switches, ceramic insulators, and buzzing transformers that covered an entire wall.

Edevane saw the problem immediately. One of the main breakers for the laboratory feed was arching, spitting small, angry blue sparks. The immense, sustained power draw was overheating the connection. He could smell the acrid scent of melting insulation. Ignoring the danger, he grabbed a pair of thick leather gloves from a hook, then used the wrench to tighten the main busbar bolts. The sparking lessened, then stopped. He watched it for a long moment, his expression unreadable. He had built this system himself, had over engineered it for any conceivable need. But he had never conceived of this.

He moved along the generator, his hands checking fuel lines, his eyes scanning the gauges. The main fuel tank was half full. A hundred gallons of diesel. He did a quick mental calculation. At the current rate of consumption, it would last fifty hours. A little over two days. He looked at the secondary tank, a smaller fifty gallon drum he kept for emergencies. It was full. Seventy five hours in total. Three days, give or take. The timeline was absolute, written in fuel and physics.

He placed a hand on the warm metal casing of the generator, feeling the steady vibration. This was his territory. A world of understandable forces, of cause and effect. He could not commune with the cosmos, but he could bleed a fuel line and true a bearing. He could keep the lights on. For now, that would have to be enough.

When he returned to the laboratory, wiping grease from his hands with a rag, the Traveller had his eyes open. He looked drained, his form slightly more translucent than it had been before. But his eyes held a new, focused intensity.

“There is a voice,” the Traveller said, his own voice barely a whisper. “Not a shriek. A hum. A pure, single tone, buried deep in the bedrock of this world. It is very old, and very faint. Sleeping.”

Edevane felt a cold thrill run down his spine, a feeling he recognized as the proximity of discovery. “A crystal. Like yours.”

“A cousin,” the Traveller corrected. “Uncut. Unawakened. A raw, natural expression of the same principles. It has never been used to navigate, only to resonate with its own point of origin. It sings a simple, quiet song of its own existence.”

“Where is it?” Edevane demanded, crossing the room to a large cabinet. He pulled out a series of rolled up maps. Geological surveys of the British Isles, bathymetric charts of the North Atlantic, satellite composites of the European continent. He spread the first one, of Great Britain, across his cluttered desk. “Show me.”

The Traveller did not look at the map. “I cannot see it on your paper. I feel it. The direction is… that way.” He pointed a shimmering finger vaguely east. “But the dominant sensation is not direction. It is depth. It is pressure. It is the crushing, patient weight of mountains. It is encased in limestone, water, and time. A place that has not seen the sun in a million years.”

Edevane unrolled another map, this one of the Eurasian plate. He looked at the great mountain ranges. The Alps. The Urals. The Himalayas. “Be more specific. What else do you feel?”

“Cold,” the Traveller said, his eyes distant. “A profound, enduring cold. The drip of water shaping stone. A silence that is not empty, but full. Full of echoes and stillness. The rock is… porous. A network of hollow spaces. A stone honeycomb.”

Karst topography. Caves. Edevane’s mind raced. He pulled out a more detailed geological survey of Europe. “The Dinaric Alps? The Pindus Mountains?”

The Traveller shook his head slowly. “No. Further north. More… jagged. There is a memory in the rock of a great struggle. Of folding and faulting. A savage geological youth.” He paused, concentrating. “Carpathian. The name whispers in the stone.”

Edevane’s finger traced the long, arcing chain of the Carpathian Mountains. He unrolled an even more detailed sectional map. “It is a vast range. It crosses seven countries.”

“The echo is strongest in the south,” the Traveller murmured. “In the land you call Romania. Near a place of great gorges, where the river has cut a path through the mountains like a knife through flesh.”

Edevane’s finger stopped. The Southern Carpathians. The Bucegi Mountains. A region known for its sprawling, complex cave systems, many of them only recently discovered, and still largely unexplored. He looked up from the map, his eyes locking with the Traveller’s. The path forward was insane, a desperate scramble based on the esoteric visions of an alien being. It was also the only path they had.

“We would need equipment,” Edevane said, his mind already shifting from diagnosis to logistics. “Climbing gear. Caving lights. Surveying equipment. It is not a casual stroll.”

“And we would need to leave this house,” the Traveller added, stating the obvious and terrifying problem. “Leave the protection of your cage.”

“The box would have to come with us,” Edevane reasoned. “But the cage… the cage is this room. It is tied to my generator. It is immovable.”

They both looked at the sphere of contained energy. It was their shield and their prison. To leave it was to die. To stay with it was to die.

Edevane walked back to the oscilloscope, studying the perfect, clean wave. “Dampened,” he whispered to himself. “Not silenced.” His eyes darted around the lab, at the coils, the capacitors, the humming vacuum tubes. “The principle is sound. Containment of a chaotic energy field by generating a counter-frequency. My solution is large because my tools are crude. A sledgehammer to perform surgery. But what if… what if the cage did not have to be so large?”

He began to sketch frantically on a piece of paper, his charcoal pencil flying. He drew the box. Then he drew a series of much smaller electromagnetic coils, arranged directly on the surface of the box itself. He drew power couplings, a focusing lens array, a modulation unit.

“It is a question of efficiency,” he muttered, more to himself than to the Traveller. “My field projector is flooding the entire room with the dampening frequency. It is wasteful. Over ninety nine percent of the energy is spent containing empty space. If I could mount miniature projectors directly onto the box, focused inward, the power requirements would drop by several orders ofmagnitude. It could be powered by something portable.”

“You can build such a thing?” the Traveller asked, his voice filled with that same sense of awe he had shown when the first cage had activated.

“I can,” Edevane said with a grim confidence. “But I will need parts my laboratory does not have. Specialized magnetic conduits. A portable power source with a high discharge rate. Silver wiring for the emitters to reduce signal degradation.” He straightened up, his eyes hard and clear. “I will have to go into town. To my suppliers.”

The plan was crystallizing, a chain of desperate but logical steps. Build a portable cage. Travel to Romania. Find the cave system. Descend into the earth. Locate the raw crystal. And then what? Edevane had no idea how they would extract it, or how the Traveller would install it in his machine. One impossibility at a time.

He looked back at the main containment field. A flicker, almost too fast to see, disturbed its perfect surface. A ripple of static, like a stone dropped in a pond of light. On the oscilloscope, the sine wave trembled, a brief, ugly spike of noise marring its perfection before it settled again.

“The crystal,” the Traveller said, his voice tense. “It pushes back. It senses the cage. It is adapting.”

Edevane strode to the power monitors. The needle on the ammeter had crept higher. The drain was increasing. The generator in the cellar pitched up in frequency to compensate, its chugging growing more strained.

“My calculations were optimistic,” Edevane said, his voice dangerously quiet. “It is not a static load. It is a dynamic, escalating resistance. We do not have three days. We do not have two.” He looked at the grandfather clock. Its hand swept past the hour. “We have perhaps thirty six hours. At most. Before the generator fails, or the crystal breaks free.” The game had changed again. The board was the same, but the clock was ticking faster than ever.
