An odd sound, a sharp metallic clink, as if a brass gear had been dropped upon the slate floor. But Arthur Finch had dropped nothing. He stood quite still, his loupe dangling from his neck, and scanned the ordered clutter of his workshop. Ticking surrounded him, a hundred clocks in congress, each measuring the world with flawless precision. Then he saw it. A small escapement wheel, which had been resting on the edge of his workbench, slid from its perch and fell to the floor with a clink. The sound he had just heard. A distinct feeling of unease, a sort of intellectual vertigo, came upon him.

He bent to retrieve the wheel, his fingers closing around the cold brass. As he straightened, he heard a sigh of exhaustion, seemingly from the corner near the grandfather clock. All men, however dedicated to the rational mechanics of the universe, carry within them the seeds of superstition. “Who is there?” Finch called out, his voice sharper than he intended. No reply came, but the air stirred, carrying the scent of rain and scorched metal. He took a step forward, and then another. Suddenly, with a gasp, he perceived a tarnished silver locket hanging in mid air, its chain swaying gently between him and the clock.

He stared at it, his mind refusing the evidence of his eyes. It was a perfectly solid object, yet nothing held it. He would have reached for it, but a touch on his shoulder arrested him, a pressure that felt strangely delayed, as if the intent arrived before the flesh. A voice spoke, quite close to his ear, the words slightly slurring into one another. “Finch,” it said.

“What?” said Finch, turning on his heel to find nothing.

“Do not be alarmed,” said the Voice. “I am a man out of time.”

Finch made no answer for a moment, simply staring at the space where the voice had originated. “A prank,” he said. “Some sort of magnetic trickery.” The locket trembled and then fell, clattering on the floor. A hand appeared for an instant where the locket had been, and then vanished.

“No prank,” said the Voice, the sound now coming from near the door. “My name is Thorne. And I am in desperate need of your skills.”

The thought uppermost in Finch’s mind was of the article he had read that morning, debunking the very notion of temporal anomalies. “Out of time?” he said, with a scorn he did not truly feel. “Are you a ghost?”

“No,” said the man. A faint shimmer resolved itself into the shape of a person, a tall man in a soaked travelling coat. He looked haggard, his face pale, but otherwise entirely solid. It was his movements that were wrong. He took a step, and the sound of his boot on the floorboards came a full second before the foot itself landed. “My body is no longer synchronised. A laboratory accident.”

“Nonsense,” said Finch, backing away towards his workbench. “This is a waking dream.” He reached behind him, his hand closing on a heavy clock winding key. His fingers, extended towards the tool, met flesh. He recoiled, his heart hammering. The man’s arm was not where it appeared to be.

“Steady, Finch! For Heaven’s sake! I mean you no harm!” The man’s hand gripped his arm, the pressure sickeningly real, though his visible form was still two feet away. A frantic desire to break free seized Finch. He swung the heavy key. “Finch!” cried the Voice. “Stop!” and the grip tightened impossibly.

The world became a nauseating chaos of delayed sensations. Finch felt himself being pulled off balance, but the man pulling him seemed to stumble a moment later. He was flung sideways against a wall of clock faces, the cacophony of their chimes erupting at the impact. He tried to shout, but a hand clamped over his mouth, a hand he could not see, only feel. The man, Thorne, was upon him grimly, his visible body a strange, lagging after-image to his physical presence.

“Listen to me, will you?” said the dislocated voice, a whisper of breath against his ear. “You’ll make me hurt you!

“Lie still, you fool!” bawled the Man in Finch’s ear.

Finch struggled for another moment, the sheer wrongness of the experience overriding his terror. Then he slumped, defeated.

“If you shout, I’ll snap your neck,” said Thorne, his tone flat with exhaustion. He released his grip. “I am a man whose present is fractured. It’s no parlour trick. I am truly desynchronised. And I need your help. You are Arthur Finch, the finest horologist in the country. You understand time better than any man alive.”

“Let me stand,” said Finch, his voice a rasp. “I will not run. Let me see this properly.”

He pushed himself up, leaning against the workbench. Thorne stood in the centre of the room, unmoving. “My name is Elias Thorne,” he said. “Of the Royal Physics Institute. You may have heard of my work on chronometry.”

“Thorne,” said Finch, rubbing his neck. “The man who disappeared after the explosion in May.”

“The very same,” answered the Voice, though Thorne’s lips had not yet moved. They formed the words a moment later, a silent, ghostly echo of the speech. “I did not disappear. I was… scattered. My physical form now exists a fraction of a second ahead of its proper place in the stream. It is a torment, Finch. A constant, maddening torment.”

“It’s impossible,” Finch whispered. “But… the locket. Your touch.”

“It is impossible enough. But I am cold, and my head is splitting. Great God, Finch, you are a man of intellect. Look at me and understand. Let me have some brandy, and let me sit down.”

Finch watched as a wooden stool scraped across the floor, seemingly of its own accord. It came to rest, and then Thorne’s visible form walked over and sat upon it. He ran a hand through his hair, and the motion seemed to pull and distort the air around it. “This beats mesmerism,” Finch said, and laughed a dry, humourless bark.

“That’s better. Thank God, you have your wits about you.”

“Or I’ve lost them entirely,” said Finch. “Brandy. Yes. Where are you? If I hand you a glass, will I touch you?”

“Here,” said the voice. The stool creaked. “Hold it out. There. All right.”

Finch poured a measure of brandy into a glass and held it out. He felt the glass drawn from his fingers. His instinct was to hold tight, but he let it go. It came to rest in the air, poised a few inches from Thorne’s visible face. He stared at it, utterly perplexed. The brandy tilted and vanished, and a moment later Thorne’s head tipped back to drink.

“This is madness,” said Finch. “This must be a hallucination. You have drugged me.”

“Nonsense,” said the Voice.

“Listen to me.”

“I proved conclusively in my last monograph,” began Finch, “that time is a constant, that, ”

“Never mind your monograph! I am living proof that it is not!” said the Voice, a note of fury in it. “And I am starving. My metabolism burns at a furious rate.”

“Food?” said Finch.

The brandy glass lowered itself to the stool. “Yes,” said Thorne. “Have you any bread?”

Finch made a noise in his throat, a sound of profound intellectual surrender. He went to a small cupboard and produced half a loaf and a knife. “This do?” he asked.

The bread was taken from him. It hung limp for a moment, then a chunk of it was torn away with a ripping sound. “Never mind the knife,” said his visitor, and the floating piece of bread vanished, followed by the sound of chewing.

“Incredible,” said Finch, and sat down heavily on his own stool.

“I always feel more myself with something in my stomach,” said Thorne, with a full mouth. His voice and the motion of his jaw were hideously misaligned.

“Of all the damnable things.”

“Exactly. But it’s a stroke of luck, my finding you. I have been watching you for two days. This workshop, this perfect clockwork world. If time can be broken, you are the man to mend it. You must. It’s getting worse, Finch. The gap is widening. Soon I fear I may come apart entirely.” He reached over and secured the brandy bottle. Kemp stared at the devouring coat. “You will help me,” said Thorne. “You will build me an anchor. A chronometer to bind me back to the present.”

“An anchor?” Finch asked, his mind reeling. “How in God’s name would I do that?”

“You will find a way,” said Thorne, and the brandy bottle tilted in the air, pouring a second measure into the invisible glass. “You are a man of precision. I am a man of chaos. You will fix me. You have no other choice.”

Finch stared at the floating bottle, the amber liquid sloshing gently within its glass prison. His workshop, usually a sanctuary of predictable, comforting sounds, the gentle tick and tock of a hundred different mechanisms measuring out the world in reliable increments, now seemed a chamber of impossible acoustics. The slosh of the brandy, the rip of the bread, the disembodied voice. Each sound was an insult to the order he had built his life upon.

“I have no choice?” Finch repeated, his voice quiet but sharp, like the click of a well oiled pawl. “You appear in my rooms, a disembodied suit of clothes and a voice, you devour my bread and drink my brandy, and you tell me I have no choice? I believe I have several. The first and most sensible is to leave this room and fetch a constable.”

The brandy bottle was set down with a solid clink on the oak of the workbench. The sound was too firm, too definite for a trick of the light. “And tell him what?” Thorne’s voice was laced with a weary scorn. “That a hungry ghost has taken up residence and is demanding you invent a new branch of physics before he fades away entirely? They would put you in Bedlam, Finch, not me. I am already in a prison of my own.”

Finch stood up. He paced the narrow channel between his benches, his hands clasped tightly behind his back. Gears of every size hung from hooks on the wall, brass and steel discs that shone like captured suns and moons in the gaslight. He stopped before a great regulator clock, its enormous pendulum swinging in a slow, majestic arc. It was his masterpiece, a clock so accurate it lost less than a second a year. It was the heart of his workshop, the metronome of his life.

“This is a deception,” Finch said, not turning around. He spoke to the clock, to its steady, rational beat. “A complex and theatrical deception. You are a ventriloquist, a conjuror of some skill. The visual distortion is a chemical agent, no doubt. Something you introduced into the air. A vapour that affects the optic nerve.”

“Oh, do stop,” the voice sighed. The sound was closer now, just behind Finch’s shoulder. He flinched, but refused to turn. “Do you truly think your intellect is so vast that anything which confounds it must be a trick? That is not science, Finch, that is vanity. Look.”

An invisible hand seemed to reach past him. Finch saw the air shimmer, warping the reflection in the polished brass of the pendulum’s bob. The shimmering coalesced, and for a horrifying instant, Finch saw the faint, ghostly outline of fingers, long and thin, approach the swinging weight. The fingers did not touch it. They passed through it.

But the pendulum, that great, heavy, unassailable arbiter of time, faltered. Its rhythm broke. It stuttered in its arc, swinging back a fraction of a second too soon, then jerking forward as if to catch up. The sound of the workshop changed. The harmonious ticking fell into a discordant clatter. A dozen clocks, all slaves to the regulator’s precision, tried to adjust to the impossible signal. The gentle chorus became a frantic, panicked clicking.

Finch cried out and spun around, his hand instinctively reaching to shield his masterpiece. “Get away from it! You’ll destroy it!”

“I am not touching it,” said Thorne, his voice now trembling with effort. “But my presence… my state… it infects things. It poisons the steady flow. Do you see? The closer I am to a mechanism of order, the more my own chaos asserts itself. I am a walking paradox. A living heresy against the laws you hold so dear.”

Finch stared, his face pale. The pendulum was swinging wildly now, its period utterly destroyed. The escapement of the great clock was chattering like teeth. He felt a wave of nausea, a kind of vertigo, as if the very floor beneath his feet had lost its certainty.

He stumbled back to his stool. “How?” he whispered. “How is this possible?”

The pressure seemed to relent. The ghostly fingers vanished. Slowly, erratically, the pendulum began to settle, finding its old, familiar rhythm. The other clocks, one by one, fell back into line, the cacophony subsiding into the familiar, gentle ticking. The crisis had passed.

Thorne, or the voice of Thorne, gave a ragged cough. “The usual way these things happen. Arrogance. An experiment that went further than intended. I was a physicist, Finch. At the university. I theorised that time was not a river, but an ocean. That it had currents, tides, depths. I believed that with sufficient application of electromagnetic force, one could create an eddy, a place to stand for a moment, outside the current.”

The invisible man fell silent. The empty tweed coat seemed to droop.

“And?” Finch prompted, his scientific curiosity now a monstrous, hungry thing, wrestling with his terror.

“And I was a fool,” Thorne said, his voice flat. “I did not create an eddy. I fell out of the boat. I am not standing outside the current, I am adrift in it, being pulled in a dozen different directions at once. This… this misalignment,” the voice gestured with a sound of rustling fabric towards his mismatched face and jaw, “was the first sign. It was a matter of milliseconds then. Now, as you can see, it is more than a full second. My body, my very atoms, are stretched across a span of time. I am a chord, not a note. And the chord is becoming dissonant. Soon it will snap.”

Finch rubbed his temples. He felt a headache blooming behind his eyes, a throbbing that kept a strange, syncopated time with his regulator clock. “An anchor,” he said, the word tasting less like madness and more like a desperate diagnosis. “You want me to build a device to pull you back into a single point in time.”

“Precisely,” said Thorne. “A chronometer of such perfect, unyielding regularity that its resonance can overcome my chaotic state. It must be more than a clock. It must be a lodestone for the present moment. It must tick with such authority that it forces my own scattered existence to listen, to fall back into line. It needs to be worn. On my person. A chronometric heart to remind my own how to beat.”

“Worn on your person?” Finch looked at the empty clothes. “How would you even…?”

“The same way I wear these,” said Thorne. “The clothes, the food I eat, anything I hold for long enough… it becomes part of my temporal distortion. It is anchored to me, for a time. But it is not an anchor for me. It is just more flotsam I am dragging along.”

Finch stood and walked to his main workbench. It was a vast plane of scarred oak, littered with the viscera of his trade. Tiny screwdrivers arranged in order of size. A tray of minuscule jewels. Coils of hairspring wire, delicate as spun spider silk. A half assembled carriage clock lay under a glass dome, its gears a frozen waterfall of brass. He picked up a loupe, the small magnifier he wore in his eye socket, and polished its lens on his waistcoat.

“This is not a matter of mechanics,” Finch said, his mind beginning to work, to dissect the problem into its component impossibilities. “What you describe is a philosophical engine. A metaphysical device. You speak of resonance, of authority. These are not terms of engineering. A clock measures time, it does not command it.”

“Then you must teach it to command!” Thorne’s voice was sharp, a blade of desperation. “You are the finest clockmaker in London. Your work is not mere measurement. It is the pursuit of perfection. You seek to create a mechanism that is immune to the vagaries of the world. Heat, cold, motion, friction. You fight against chaos in your own way every day. I am simply asking you to fight a greater chaos. My chaos.”

Finch placed the loupe in his eye. He stared at the carriage clock, its components suddenly seeming crude, childish. He had spent his life making things that told the time. Now he was being asked to make something that *was* time.

“Even if I could conceive of such a thing,” he began, his thoughts racing, “the power source would be a problem. A mainspring unwinds. Its force is not constant. A weight drops. It must be rewound. The very act of powering a clock introduces an imperfection, a reliance on an outside agent. Your anchor would need a heartbeat of its own. A source of constant, unwavering power.”

There was a pause. The creak of the stool suggested Thorne had leaned forward. “My experiment,” the voice said, lower now, more conspiratorial. “It involved electromagnetism. And certain… resonant crystals. I was attempting to power my device directly from the background radiation of the earth itself. The theories were sound. The execution, as you can see, was flawed.”

“Resonant crystals,” Finch muttered, the phrase alien and alchemical on his tongue. He made clocks of brass and steel and ruby.

“I have them,” said Thorne. “What remains of them. They are in my coat pocket. They are the only things, besides my own damned self, that survived the accident.”

A shimmering appeared over the workbench. A small, heavy leather pouch materialised and dropped with a soft thud onto the wood. Finch stared at it. He did not want to touch it. It was a piece of the catastrophe that had unmade a man.

“You will need a new kind of escapement,” Thorne continued, his voice now taking on the urgent tone of a collaborator, a fellow inventor. “One that does not simply release power in measured beats, but one that attunes it. It must listen to the crystal’s resonance and amplify it. It must become the universe in miniature, Finch, with a single, perfect rhythm at its core.”

Finch reached out a hesitant hand and nudged the pouch with his fingertip. It was real. It was heavy. He untied the leather cord and tipped the contents out. Three crystals, each no bigger than his thumb, tumbled onto the bench. They were a milky, opaque quartz, but shot through with shimmering veins of what looked like captured lightning. They seemed to hum, not with sound, but with a potential that made the hairs on his arm stand on end.

He was a man of precision. A man of tangible things. Cogs and levers. Torque and friction. But as he looked at the impossible crystals next to the impossible man’s empty coat, a strange excitement began to kindle beneath his fear. His entire life had been a quest for an unattainable goal: perfect time. He had pursued it to the very edge of what was mechanically possible. And now, the universe, in a horrifying, roundabout way, had brought him a problem that went beyond the mechanical.

“The gap,” Finch said suddenly, looking up from the crystals towards the hovering face. “You said it was widening.”

“Yes,” said Thorne. “Every hour, I feel myself stretching further. It is becoming… difficult to cohere. Sometimes I find my thoughts arriving before I have had them. I remember things that have not yet happened in this room.”

“What things?” Finch asked, his blood running cold.

“I remember you dropping that screwdriver,” Thorne said.

Finch glanced down. His hand was resting next to a small, brass handled screwdriver. He had no intention of picking it up.

“I remember the sound it will make when it hits the floorboards,” Thorne’s voice went on, dreamy and distant. “A sharp little clatter. It will roll under the bench. You will have to get on your hands and knees to retrieve it.”

Finch snatched his hand away from the screwdriver as if it were red hot. “Stop it.”

“I cannot stop it,” the voice replied, and it sounded frayed, stretched thin. “It is happening. The past and future are… bleeding into my present. Soon there will be no present left for me at all. Just a smear of moments. An echo and a premonition.”

Suddenly, the distortion around Thorne’s visible head grew violent. It wasn’t a shimmer anymore, but a frantic, boiling turbulence. His face seemed to split, to double, to show three or four expressions at once. A grimace, a look of surprise, a placid emptiness. His voice fractured.

“Fix… please… Finch… it is… it is coming apart…”

The sounds came in a jumble. The word ‘please’ arrived a full second after ‘fix’. The empty coat sleeve twitched, and the sound of its rustling followed a moment later, a lazy ghost of an action already completed. The brandy bottle on the bench rattled, not because it was touched, but because the moment in which it had been set down was replaying itself, a temporal echo rippling through the present.

Finch stared, frozen in a state of pure, intellectual horror. This was not a man. This was a physical law in the process of failing. He saw the chaotic energy flickering around Thorne’s form, and he saw it reflected in the steady, placid face of his regulator clock. It was an assault. It was an obscenity.

And in that moment, he knew he had no choice. Not because Thorne had threatened him, but because the sight of this temporal chaos offended him to his very soul. It was a clock that was not just wrong, but fundamentally broken, and it was his nature, his entire purpose for being, to fix broken clocks. This was merely the grandest, most terrible repair job he had ever been offered.

He swept a space clear on his workbench with a sweep of his forearm, sending tiny screws and brass shavings skittering. He placed the three strange crystals in the centre of the cleared space. He picked up the screwdriver he had recoiled from, his hand steady now, his purpose clear.

“Tell me everything you know about the resonant frequency,” Finch said, his voice the calm, certain voice of a master craftsman about to begin his work. “We have no time to waste.”

The turbulence around Thorne’s head subsided slightly, the fractured voice knitting itself back together, though it was still strained. “Good,” it rasped. “Good man.”

Finch did not reply. He was already sketching on a piece of parchment, his mind a whirlwind of gears and escapements. He would need to invent a new kind of balance wheel, something that could oscillate not in two dimensions, but in three. He would need a hairspring wound from a material not yet conceived. He would need to build a cage for a bolt of lightning.

He looked from the impossible crystals to the empty suit of clothes, and for the first time, he did not see a ghost or a madman. He saw a client. And he saw the work. The great work. The workshop was silent now, save for the steady, reassuring tick of a hundred clocks, all of them waiting, it seemed, for him to begin.

The voice that answered Finch was not one voice, but several, layered like imperfectly stacked sheets of glass. It was Thorne, yet it was also the echo of Thorne from a moment ago, and the faint, tinny premonition of the Thorne who would speak in the next second. The effect was a discordant chord of a single man’s existence.

“Resonance,” the chorus of Thornes began, the words arriving not in a clean line but in a thicket. “Is the… state of being. Every object, every person, possesses a unique frequency. It is the vibration that holds the atoms together in their proper sequence. It is the keynote of a life, played consistently from its beginning to its end. My… my work… we were attempting to observe this frequency. To map the song of a life.”

Finch did not look up from his parchment. His charcoal pencil flew, sketching not a clock but a concept. A series of concentric circles, like the ripples in a pond, with equations scrawled in the spaces between. He was not merely listening to the words; he was listening to the timing, to the dissonant intervals between the echoed phrases. He was a physician listening to a failing heart, a tuner hearing a snapped string.

“What happened?” Finch asked, his voice sharp and devoid of sympathy. It was the voice of a man demanding a precise measurement.

The turbulence around Thorne’s form intensified again. A wave of heat washed through the workshop, and for a terrifying instant, every clock on the walls went silent. The sudden, absolute quiet was more jarring than any noise. A hundred pendulums froze in mid swing. Then, just as abruptly, they all started again, their collective tick a frantic, accelerated stutter before settling back into their proper rhythm. Finch’s hand tightened on his pencil, the charcoal snapping. The chaos was leaking. It was contagious.

“An accident,” Thorne’s fractured voice continued, the words now smeared with the sound of cracking glass and a low, humming buzz. “We did not merely observe the frequency. We… amplified it. We built a resonator. A great, crystalline chamber. To see if a moment could be… held. Sustained. Made to last longer than its allotted tick of the universal clock. I was inside. The amplification cascade… it was… it was too perfect.”

The word ‘perfect’ was a scream. It was followed by a moment of utter vocal silence, during which the empty clothes slumped, as if the force holding them upright had briefly ceased to exist.

“It did not sustain the moment,” the voice whispered, returning with a dry, dusty quality, like an old recording. “It shattered it. And me with it. I am not a man anymore, Finch. I am a collection of my own echoes. A life played on a cracked record, the needle skipping forwards and backwards. The past bleeds into the future. The future infects the present. These crystals… they are the only thing with any integrity. They are fragments from the resonator. They hold a stable moment within them. A perfect, single tick of time, frozen. They are my only anchor.”

Finch swept the broken charcoal from his parchment. An anchor. The word resonated with him. Not a cure, then, but a mooring. He looked at the three crystals. They were not gems. They were impossibilities. He picked one up. It was cool to the touch, impossibly smooth, with no discernible weight. It felt less like an object and more like a captured absence of space. He held it up to the gaslight. The light did not pass through it or reflect off it. The light simply stopped at its surface, as if the crystal had consumed it.

He placed it back on the bench and selected a fine, tempered steel probe from his rack of tools. He tapped the crystal gently. The sound was not a simple ‘tink’. It was a complex, layered chime that hung in the air for a full ten seconds. It was the sound of a bell ringing in a vast cathedral, the sound of a single drop of water hitting a placid lake, and the sound of a woman laughing, all at once. The sound had a past, a present, and a future contained within its brief existence. Finch’s eyes widened. These were not fragments of a machine. They were fragments of a specific, stable instant. Thorne was right. They were crystallized time.

His mind, accustomed to the elegant certainties of physics and mechanics, reeled. But the craftsman in him took over. An object was an object. It had properties. It could be understood. He spent the next several hours in a state of focused obsession, ignoring the groaning, flickering presence of Thorne entirely. He used tuning forks, attempting to find the crystal’s harmonic properties. He discovered that each crystal vibrated at an impossibly high frequency, a pitch far beyond human hearing that he could only detect by the way it made the delicate hairs on his arm stand on end. He brought a magnetic compass near them; the needle spun wildly, not pointing north or south, but seemingly pointing to a direction that did not exist, a temporal pole.

He tried to scratch one with a diamond stylus. The diamond, the hardest substance he knew, simply skidded off the surface, leaving no mark. It was as if the crystal’s surface did not exist in the same moment as the diamond’s point. They could not interact. Frustrated, he set up a series of lenses, attempting to magnify the crystal’s internal structure. What he saw made him pull back from the eyepiece with a gasp. There was nothing inside. No crystalline lattice, no occlusions, no structure at all. There was only a perfect, flawless, shimmering void. A piece of pure potential.

“It will not yield to force,” Thorne’s voice rasped from the corner. It was weaker now, more frayed. “It does not exist as matter does. It is a possibility that has been made… permanent.”

Finch ignored him, though he filed the information away. He was beginning to understand. He could not build a machine to contain Thorne. That was thinking in terms of cages and walls. He had to build a machine that could sing Thorne’s song back to him. A harmonic resonator, just as Thorne had described his disastrous machine, but this one would not amplify. It would gather. It would coax the scattered notes of Thorne’s life back into a single, coherent melody.

His sketches became more frantic, more inspired. He abandoned the idea of a traditional escapement, the mechanism that gives a clock its tick and tock. Thorne’s problem was an excess of ticks and tocks, colliding and cancelling each other out. He needed a continuous, smooth regulator. He began to draw a structure that looked less like a clock and more like a golden orrery. The three crystals would be at the centre, held in a gimballed frame of silver and brass. Around them, three interlocking rings would rotate, each at a different speed, each calibrated to a different harmonic property he had observed. These rings would not be driven by a coiled spring or a falling weight. He sketched in wires of spun copper, leading from the rings to a device he had only ever read about in speculative journals: a galvanic battery. He would use the steady, silent flow of electricity.

The work consumed him. Days blurred into a single, extended moment of creation, lit by the constant hiss of the gas lamps. He ate stale bread at his workbench, slept in short, dreamless bursts in his chair. The workshop, his sanctuary of order, began to reflect the temporal chaos of his client. He would reach for a screwdriver and his hand would close on empty air, only for the tool to appear in his grasp a second later. The great regulator clock in the corner began to lose time, then gain it, its majestic pendulum swinging with a nervous, erratic pulse. Once, he looked up to see that the light from the window showed not the grey afternoon sky, but a rich, impossible twilight of deep purple and orange, a sunset from some other day, or some other place. He blinked, and it was grey again.

Thorne’s condition was visibly worsening. The shimmer around his head was now a constant, violent storm. His voice was almost entirely gone, replaced by a collage of disconnected sounds: a snippet of conversation, the clink of a glass, a startled cry, the low thrum of a great machine. The empty suit would often freeze for minutes at a time, locked in a moment from its past, before lurching into sudden, jerky motion. Finch worked with a desperate urgency, not of fear, but of a craftsman racing against the ultimate deadline. The material itself was failing.

He crafted the interlocking rings with a jeweller’s precision, polishing the brass until it shone like old gold. He wound the copper coils, his fingers nimble and sure. The central gimbal that would hold the crystals was the most difficult part. It had to be perfectly balanced, frictionless, able to orient itself freely in any direction. It took him two days of filing and polishing a single silver bearing before he was satisfied. As he worked, he felt the presence of Thorne change. It was less of a tormented man and more of a raw, physical force. A pressure was building in the room, a sense of immense temporal energy straining against its nonexistent bonds.

One evening, as he was making a final adjustment to the gimbal, a profound and terrible silence fell upon the workshop. Not the brief, shocking silence of before, but a deep, velvet quiet that absorbed all sound. The ticking of the clocks did not stop; it was simply erased. Finch looked over at Thorne. The shimmering was gone.

In its place, for a single, horrifyingly clear second, was the man himself. He was not a ghost or an echo. He stood there, solid and real, a man in his middle years with intelligent, tired eyes and a face etched with a scholar’s gentle curiosity. He wore not an empty suit, but a laboratory coat. He was looking not at Finch, but at something to his left, a warm, genuine smile on his face.

“It’s beautiful, Elara,” he said, his voice clear and whole, filled with affection.

Then the vision collapsed. The man dissolved into a screaming vortex of light and shadow, a maelstrom of temporal fragments that lashed out into the room. A stack of clock faces on a high shelf aged a century in a heartbeat, yellowing, cracking, and turning to dust. A pot of lubricating oil on the bench boiled without heat, its contents vanishing in a puff of acrid smoke. The very air grew thin and cold. Finch was thrown back against the wall, the silver gimbal clutched in his hand.

He stared at the place where the man had been. Now it was just the empty suit again, slumped and lifeless in the chair. But something had changed. Finch had seen him. He had seen the soul in the broken machine. He had heard the name, Elara. This was no longer an abstract problem of physics. It was a rescue.

With renewed, almost feverish determination, he returned to the workbench. The vision, the name, it had given him the final piece of the puzzle. The device could not be impersonal. A keynote, Thorne had called it. A song. A song is not just a frequency; it has emotion, it has memory. The machine he was building needed a focus, a representation of the stable man he had just seen.

He took a small, perfectly round piece of ivory, a disc he had been saving for the face of a special commission. With his finest engraving tools, he began to etch a portrait into its surface. He worked from the memory of the face he had just seen: the tired eyes, the gentle smile, the lines of concentration and kindness. He was not a portrait artist, but he was a master of precision. Every line he cut was deliberate, perfect. He was not just carving a face; he was carving an idea, the idea of the man named Thorne, whole and coherent. He would place this tiny portrait at the very heart of the machine, within the gimbal, surrounded by the crystals. It would be the focal point for the harmonic convergence. It would be the sheet music from which his machine would play.

As he finished the last line around the eye, the turbulence around Thorne’s suit began again, weaker this time, a flutter instead of a storm. A single, garbled word leaked out of the distortion. “Elara…” It was a whisper, a question, and a prayer.

Finch carefully set the ivory disc into its silver housing. He placed the three time-frozen crystals around it, locking them into their delicate clasps. The central component of his chronometric anchor was complete. He looked at it, lying on the velvet cloth of his workbench. It was the most beautiful and terrifying thing he had ever made. It was a clock that did not tell time, but was meant to command it.

He began the final assembly. He connected the copper wires from the galvanic battery to the three outer rings. He mounted the central gimbal. He calibrated the rotation of the rings, tuning them not with gears, but with variable resistors, listening for a subtle hum in the air that told him he was approaching the correct frequencies. The workshop grew strangely cold. The gaslights flickered, their flames turning a pale, sickly blue. The remaining clocks on the walls began to tick backwards.

Thorne’s form was now little more than a dim, wavering outline within his clothes, a candle flame about to be extinguished. The sounds coming from him were elemental: the rush of wind, the crackle of fire, the slow grinding of rock. The man was dissolving back into the raw, unstructured chaos of his own history.

“It is time,” Finch said, his voice steady, though his heart hammered against his ribs. He stood before his creation, his hand on a large brass switch. “Whatever happens, do not fight it. You must let it pull you home.”

There was no reply but a faint, sighing sound, like the last breath leaving a body.

Finch took a deep breath, the air sharp and cold in his lungs. He looked at the chaos in the chair, and then at the impossible, beautiful order of the machine he had built to combat it. He was a clockmaker. This was his purpose. He closed his eyes, offered a silent prayer to the god of gears and gravity, and threw the switch.

A click, sharp and final, echoed in the sudden, profound silence. For a single, suspended heartbeat, nothing happened. The flickering gaslights held their sickly blue tinge. The clocks remained frozen in their backward march. The chaotic form in the chair was still a maelstrom of barely contained energy. Finch felt a cold dread creep into his soul. Had he failed? Had all his calculations, all his frantic, brilliant work, culminated in nothing more than a dead switch and an empty promise?

Then came the hum.

It was not a sound one heard so much as felt, a deep, resonant vibration that began in the floorboards and traveled up through the soles of his boots into his very bones. It was the sound of immense, orderly power awakening. The three copper rings of the chronometric anchor began to turn, slowly at first, then with increasing, silent speed. They moved in contrary motion, one clockwise, the next anticlockwise, the third on a perpendicular axis, weaving an intricate, invisible pattern in the air.

The air in the workshop grew colder still, a biting, sterile cold that carried the scent of ozone and distant snow. A fine layer of frost bloomed on the brass casing of the machine, tracing intricate, fernlike patterns across its polished surface. As the rings reached their optimal velocity, the three time frozen crystals at the heart of the device began to glow.

It was not a warm light. It was a pure, silver luminescence, a light that seemed to cast no shadows but instead to absorb them. It pulsed in perfect time with the hum, a steady, thrumming beat that seemed to impose its rhythm on the very fabric of the room. The backwards ticking of the clocks on the wall faltered, stuttered, and then stopped entirely, their hands pointing to a dozen different, meaningless times.

Finch stared, mesmerized. The light from the three crystals was not radiating outwards. It was focusing inwards, converging on the small silver housing at the very center of the gimbal. It passed through the polished quartz lenses he had so carefully ground, concentrating into three needle thin beams of pure, coherent temporality. The beams met precisely upon the surface of the ivory disc, on the face of the man named Thorne that Finch had carved.

The effect on the figure in the chair was immediate and violent. The elemental chaos that surrounded Thorne’s wavering form, the grinding of rock and the rush of wind, suddenly had a direction. It was no longer expanding; it was being pulled. A torrent of distorted sound and flickering, half formed images was torn from the suit and drawn towards the machine. It was a phantom hurricane, a tempest of fractured moments, all being funneled into the impossibly small point of light on the ivory portrait.

Finch could see glimpses within the storm. He saw a boy’s hand reaching for a steam powered toy, a young man’s face lit by the glow of a chemical reaction, a skyline of a city that did not exist, all twisted and torn apart like confetti in a gale. The sounds intensified into a deafening roar, a cacophony of unborn echoes and dead memories. The entire workshop shuddered, and the tools on Finch’s bench rattled and danced.

Thorne’s empty suit was being whipped about as if by an invisible force, the arms flailing, the helmet lolling on the collar. Through the roaring, a single, clear sound emerged once more, pulled from the vortex. “Elara!” It was not a question this time, but a call, a final, desperate cry of recognition across an impossible distance.

The ivory disc at the heart of the machine began to glow with a fierce, internal heat, the etched lines of Thorne’s face blazing as if forged in a star. The portrait was no longer a carving; it had become a lodestone, a singular point of coherence in a sea of temporal insanity. It was the anchor. It was pulling him home.

The silver light from the crystals intensified to a blinding whiteness. The hum rose in pitch until it was a piercing, pure tone at the very edge of hearing. Finch threw his hands up to shield his eyes as the entire machine was engulfed in a silent, brilliant flash that bleached the world of all color.

And then, as suddenly as it began, it was over.

The light vanished. The hum died away. The rings of the machine spun down with a series of soft, metallic clicks. A profound, ringing silence descended upon the workshop, broken only by the frantic pounding of Finch’s own heart. The air, which had been painfully cold, began to warm, and the frost on the machine’s casing melted, running down its sides in glistening rivulets like tears.

Finch lowered his hands, blinking, his vision filled with swimming, purple spots. He looked first at the machine. It was inert, its copper rings still, its crystals dark and milky. It looked like nothing more than an eccentric, overly complicated piece of brasswork.

Then, his breath catching in his throat, he looked at the chair.

It was empty.

The suit was there, slumped and lifeless, the helmet resting against the padded back. But of the man, of the chaos, of the wavering, candle flame presence, there was nothing. Not a whisper, not a flicker. Just an empty suit in an empty chair. The elemental storm was gone. The air was still.

Finch took a shaky step forward, then another. He reached out a trembling hand and touched the sleeve of the suit. It was just cloth, cool and inert. He walked around the chair, his eyes scanning the floor, the corners of the room, as if expecting to find some remnant, some trace of the man who had occupied it. There was nothing.

He had done it. He had sent Thorne… somewhere. Home, he prayed. He had folded up the chaotic map of the man’s life and sent it back to its point of origin. He felt a wave of dizziness, a mixture of giddy triumph and abject terror. He leaned against his workbench for support, his legs feeling weak and unreliable.

The clocks on the wall began to tick again. Not backwards, but forwards. One by one, they chimed, each announcing a different, incorrect hour. They had been reset, jolted back into their proper function, but their memory of the correct time was gone. Like him, Finch thought, they were witnesses to an event they could not properly measure.

He pushed himself upright and approached the chronometric anchor. It was warm to the touch now, a gentle, residual heat. He peered into the central gimbal, his heart thumping a nervous rhythm against his ribs. The three crystals were opaque, their inner light extinguished for now. And the ivory disc…

He carefully unlatched the silver housing and lifted the small disc out. He held it up to the now steady, yellow light of the gas lamps. The portrait of Thorne was still there, the lines as fine and precise as when he had carved them. But something was different. Across the left eye, the one he had finished last, was a new mark. It was not a scratch or a crack. It was a tiny, hairline fracture in the very substance of the ivory, a fissure so fine it was almost invisible. It looked like a scar. Or perhaps, a tear.

It was a response. A message sent back. It meant that the connection had been made. It meant that somewhere, or somewhen, Thorne had received his anchor. Whether it had saved him, or merely given him a moment of peace before the end, Finch could not know. But it was not nothing.

A wave of utter exhaustion washed over him, so profound it felt as if his bones had turned to lead. He had not slept in what felt like days. He had poured every ounce of his intellect, his energy, and his hope into this single, desperate act. He sank into his own worn work stool, the ivory disc clutched in his hand, and for the first time since Thorne had appeared, he allowed himself to feel the true weight of what he had done. He had tampered with the fundamental law of the universe, with the one constant that governed all things. He was a clockmaker who had broken the master clock.

He must have dozed, for he was startled awake by a sharp, authoritative rap on the workshop door.

Finch’s head snapped up. The light outside the grimy windows was the pale, grey light of dawn. He had been asleep for hours. The rapping came again, harder this time, the sound of a heavy ring against solid oak.

“Mr. Finch!” a voice called out, clipped and official. “Open up. City Constabulary.”

Finch’s blood ran cold. He scrambled to his feet, his mind racing. The machine. He had to hide it. But it was immense, bolted to the floor. He looked around his workshop, a chaotic scene of discarded tools, wires, and half finished projects. And in the center of it all, the empty suit.

“One moment!” he called out, his voice hoarse.

He rushed to the chair and began frantically trying to fold the strange, bulky suit. It was stiff and resisted his efforts. There was no time. He grabbed a heavy canvas tarpaulin from a corner and threw it over the chair, hiding the suit from immediate view. He then looked at the chronometric anchor. It was too beautiful, too strange, to be anything but suspicious. He draped another, smaller cloth over it, but its unnatural shape was still obvious.

The pounding on the door resumed, louder and more insistent. “Mr. Finch, I will not ask again.”

Fearing the door would be broken down, Finch took a deep breath, wiped his sweaty palms on his trousers, and walked to the door. He turned the key and pulled it open a few inches, trying to block the view of the room with his body.

Standing on his doorstep were two men. One was a uniformed constable, a large, impassive man with a thick moustache. The other was a slender man in a sharp, dark woolen suit and a bowler hat. He had a pale, intelligent face, with keen, analytical eyes that seemed to take in everything at once. He held up a small leather wallet, showing a silver badge.

“Inspector Davies,” the man said, his voice calm but firm. “I apologize for the early hour, Mr. Finch, but we are investigating a most unusual disturbance.”

“A disturbance, Inspector?” Finch asked, trying to keep his voice even. “I am afraid I heard nothing. I was… working late.”

Davies’s eyes flickered past Finch, into the workshop. “Indeed. We received dozens of reports starting shortly after three this morning. Of a tremor that shook the entire district. Of a flash of white light bright enough to turn night into day. And of every single public clock in a six block radius stopping at precisely the same second. The epicenter of all these phenomena, according to our surveyors, appears to be this very address.”

Finch felt a knot of ice form in his stomach. Of course. An event of that magnitude would not go unnoticed. He had been so focused on Thorne that he had not considered the consequences for the world outside his own walls.

“I… I can’t imagine why,” Finch stammered. “Perhaps a gas main explosion?”

Davies’s gaze was unsettlingly direct. He did not seem convinced. “There was no sound of an explosion, Mr. Finch. Only the light, the tremor, and the silence that followed. May we come in?”

It was not a request. Finch knew he had no choice. He stepped aside, pulling the door open. Davies and the constable entered, their boots clicking on the stone floor. The constable remained by the door, his presence a silent threat, while Davies began to slowly walk through the workshop.

His eyes missed nothing. He noted the clocks on the wall, all ticking, all showing different times. He sniffed the air. “Ozone,” he murmured to himself. “Like after a lightning strike.”

His gaze fell upon the large, canvas covered shape in the center of the room. “A new project, Mr. Finch?”

“A private commission,” Finch said quickly. “A… a navigational device for a wealthy patron. It is highly confidential.”

Davies walked around it, his expression unreadable. He reached out a hand and touched the canvas, his fingers tracing the strange shape beneath. “And that?” he asked, nodding towards the other draped object, the chair with Thorne’s suit hidden beneath.

“An old automaton I am restoring,” Finch lied, his heart hammering. “The mechanisms are very delicate. I keep it covered to protect it from dust.”

Davies seemed to accept this for a moment. He continued his slow circuit of the room. He stopped near the spot where the chair had been, where the machine had focused its power. He frowned.

“It is remarkably cold right here,” he said, looking down at the floor as if he could see the lingering stain of the event. He held his hand out, palm down. “Noticeably so. Can you explain that?”

“A draft, perhaps,” Finch offered weakly. “This is an old building.”

“There is not a breath of wind in here,” Davies countered smoothly. He looked directly at Finch, his eyes narrowed. “Mr. Finch, you are known as the finest clockmaker in the city. A man who understands precision, gears, pendulums. A man who understands the orderly progression of time. And yet, your own workshop is a temporal anomaly. Your clocks are in disarray, there is a lingering charge in the air, and there is a cold spot on your floor that defies explanation.”

He took a step closer. “I am not here about a broken gas main. I am here because something happened in this room tonight that violates the known laws of physics. And I believe you know exactly what it was.”

Finch stood his ground, his mind a whirlwind of fear and defiance. He could not tell this man the truth. The world was not ready for it. Davies would think him a lunatic, or worse, a charlatan. He would be ruined, his work confiscated, his life’s purpose dismantled and locked away in some government vault.

“Inspector,” Finch said, his voice finding a new strength born of desperation. “I am a humble artisan. I make things that tick. Perhaps one of my galvanic batteries overloaded. It can happen. A bright spark, a jolt. Nothing more.”

Davies held his gaze for a long moment. It felt like a contest of wills, a silent battle between the man who demanded order and the man who had just commanded chaos. Finally, with a small, inscrutable sigh, Davies broke eye contact.

“Very well, Mr. Finch,” he said, his tone shifting. “An overloaded battery. We will record that as your official statement.” He turned to the constable. “We are finished here.”

The constable nodded. Davies walked to the door, but paused on the threshold. He turned back, one last question in his eyes.

“Just one more thing,” he said. “We found this on your doorstep. It seems to have been dropped in some haste.” He reached into his coat pocket and produced a small object. It was a single, tarnished brass cufflink, intricately engraved with a pattern of overlapping gears.

Finch recognized it instantly. It was from the sleeve of Thorne’s suit. It must have fallen off when the man first stumbled into his workshop.

Finch’s face must have betrayed him, for a flicker of something, of understanding or confirmation, passed through Davies’s eyes.

“I see it is unfamiliar to you,” Davies said, though his tone suggested he believed the opposite. He pocketed the cufflink. “If you think of anything else regarding this… power surge… you know where to find us.”

With a final, lingering look at the covered machine, Inspector Davies turned and left, the constable following him out. Finch closed the door behind them, leaning his full weight against it, his body trembling with relief and residual fear.

He was safe, for now. But he knew Davies was not satisfied. The Inspector was a man who solved puzzles, and Finch had just handed him the most impossible puzzle of his career. He would be back.

Finch walked back to the center of his workshop and stood between his two great secrets: the empty suit of a man lost in time, and the impossible machine he had built to save him. He pulled the canvas from the chronometric anchor, revealing its beautiful, silent form. He then went to his workbench and picked up the ivory disc. He held it to the light again, his thumb gently tracing the tiny, new fissure over Thorne’s eye.

It was a scar. A sign of a battle fought and, perhaps, won. Finch looked from the disc to the machine. His work was not over. It had just begun. He was no longer just a clockmaker. He was the keeper of an anchor, a guardian of a bridge he had built across the ocean of time. And he would have to watch, and wait, for any more messages that might come back.
