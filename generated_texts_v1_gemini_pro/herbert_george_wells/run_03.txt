Professor Alistair Finch had the distinct sensation that a page had turned in the book lying open across the room. He had not turned it. He was, in fact, quite sure he had left the heavy folio open to the Peloponnesian campaign. Now it displayed a map of Sicily. He blinked, blaming the late hour and the single glass of port at his elbow. Finch was a man of history, a believer in cause and effect, not in mischievous spirits that read Thucydides in his study. He stood and walked to the book, his slippers whispering on the Persian rug. The page was undeniably turned.

He felt a sudden coldness, a primitive prickle of the skin. The feeling that is often called a premonition came upon him. He scanned the room, from the tiered bookshelves to the heavy velvet curtains. Nothing was out of place, save for the book. Then he heard it, a soft scraping sound from the direction of his writing desk. All rational men, however steeped in logic, carry a sliver of ancient fear. He took a heavy brass ruler from the mantelpiece. Suddenly, with a jolt, he saw the nib of his fountain pen lift from its inkwell, hover for a second, and then begin to write upon a fresh sheet of foolscap.

He stared, his mouth agape. It was not writing so much as a frantic, spidery scratching, impossibly swift. A single word appeared: HELP. He would have shouted, but a sudden pressure on his lips stopped him, and a voice whispered directly into his ear.

“Finch!” said the Voice.

“Wha?” said Finch, his heart hammering against his ribs.

“Do not make a sound,” said the Voice. “I am in a desperate state.”

“Who is there?” Finch managed, his own voice a dry rasp. “This is some absurd parlour trick.”

“It is Thorne,” said the Voice, and Finch’s mind flew back a decade. Julian Thorne. The brilliant, unstable student of physics who had been expelled for conducting unauthorised experiments. The man he had argued with a hundred times over the limits of theoretical science.

“Thorne is in Argentina,” Finch said, the statement sounding hollow even to himself. The pen on the desk scribbled again, a furious black line that tore the paper. “I am Thorne,” repeated the Voice. “And I have made a terrible mistake.”

The thought uppermost in Finch’s mind was Thorne’s last mad paper on temporal relativity. “What are you doing in my study?” he demanded. “Where are you?”

“Here,” said the Voice. “And everywhere.”

Finch stepped back sharply, his hand clenching the brass ruler. “This is madness. You’re a ventriloquist, a charlatan.” He swung the ruler wildly through the air in front of him. It met nothing but air for a moment, then it was snatched from his hand with such force that his fingers stung. The ruler clattered to the floor ten feet away. He recoiled, his face pale.

“Keep still, Alistair, for pity’s sake! I need your help. Stop!” A wind seemed to rush past him. His arm was seized in a grip that was not strong, but shockingly fast, like the snap of a trap. He cried out.

“Finch!” cried the Voice. “Finch! Be reasonable!” and the grip tightened, pulling him off balance. An overwhelming desire to escape seized him. He struggled, but it was like fighting a whirlwind. He was spun around and pushed firmly into his own wingback chair. He felt the pressure of hands on his shoulders holding him down.

“If you struggle, I’ll break your neck before you can blink,” said the Voice, close to his ear again. “It’s no trick. It’s physics. Horrible, ruinous physics. Do you remember what I was working on? Localised temporal acceleration.”

Finch stopped fighting and breathed heavily. “It was theoretical nonsense.”

“It was not!” said Thorne, the voice filled with a desperate pride. “I solved it. I can move… outside the normal stream. To you, I am a blur, a gust of wind. I am here, but I am living a thousand moments in the time it takes you to live one.”

“Let me see you,” said Finch.

“You cannot. The eye cannot register what moves so swiftly. I am just a man. A man you once knew. I am simply… fast.”

“Thorne?” said Finch, the name a question.

“Thorne,” answered the Voice. “The boy you called a reckless fool. The one who said reality was more flexible than you believed. Five foot ten, with a scar on his chin from a shattered beaker. Who nearly burned down the West Laboratory.”

“I am bewildered,” said Finch. “My mind is a muddle. What has this to do with breaking into my house?”

“I am Thorne.”

Finch thought. “It’s monstrous,” he said. “But what catastrophe could make a man move so fast?”

“It is not a catastrophe. It is a formula, a series of electromagnetic fields.”

“It’s monstrous!” said Finch again. “How in God’s name?”

“It is monstrous enough. But I am exhausted and starving. The energy consumption is… immense. Finch, you are a man of reason. Accept the evidence. Get me some food. And brandy. Let me rest here.”

Finch watched as the decanter of brandy on his sideboard seemed to lift of its own accord. The stopper was removed, and the liquid glugged into a glass, which then floated to a spot near the fireplace. An armchair gave a sudden creak, and its cushions depressed by an inch. He squeezed the bridge of his nose. “This is worse than ghosts,” he said, and gave a short, hysterical laugh.

“That’s better. Thank goodness, you’re seeing sense.”

“Or losing it,” said Finch, and rubbed his eyes. “You are truly sitting in that chair?”

“I am. Get the food. I’m fading, Finch.”

“It didn’t feel so. Where is the glass? If I walk over there will I bump into you? There! All right. Food? Here. Where do I put it?”

He went to a small cabinet where he kept biscuits and a cold meat pie. He returned with a plate. The chair creaked again, and Finch felt the plate drawn from his grasp. His instinct was to hold on, but he let go. It settled on an invisible lap. “This must be a dream. A waking nightmare induced by bad port.”

“Nonsense,” said the Voice.

“It’s preposterous.”

“Listen to me.”

“I proved in my last lecture,” began Finch, “that time is a constant.”

“Never mind your lectures! I am burning alive with hunger,” said the Voice, “and the world is a slow, tedious hell to a man in my state.”

“Meat pie?” said Finch. The pie vanished from the plate in three distinct, impossibly fast bites, with barely a sound. “Yes,” said the Invisible Man, a faint chewing noise now audible. “Have you a pipe and tobacco?”

Finch made a strangled noise in his throat. He walked to the mantel and retrieved his own briar pipe and a pouch of Cavendish. “This do?” he asked. They were plucked from his hand. The pouch hung in the air, untied itself, and a quantity of tobacco vanished into the bowl of the pipe, which then tipped and tamped itself down. “A light,” said the Unseen, curtly. “And the brandy.”

Finch fetched a box of matches and passed the brandy glass towards the chair. He came back with the matches, struck one, and held it out. “Never mind matches,” said his visitor, and the match was snatched from his fingers. A tiny, perfect ring of smoke appeared a foot from the bowl of the pipe, followed by another, and another. It was bizarre to see it; the smoke did not stream from a mouth, but simply bloomed into existence in the air, a visible exhalation from an invisible lung. “This damnable gift of tobacco,” he said, the voice now sounding more relaxed. “I am fortunate to have found you, Finch. You must hide me. I am in a terrible fix. I’ve been reckless, I think. The things I have seen! But we can put this right. Let me explain.”

He took a gulp of brandy; the level in the glass dropped by a third in an instant. Finch got up, looked wildly about, and poured a brandy for himself. “It’s mad, but I suppose I am allowed to drink.”

“You haven’t heard the half of it,” said the Voice from the armchair.

Finch took a shaky sip of his own brandy. The fiery liquid did little to steady his nerves. It felt absurdly normal, this act of drinking in his own study, while the laws of physics were being torn apart in the wingback chair opposite him. The pipe smoke continued to rise in lazy, impossible puffs from a point in space.

“The half of it,” Finch repeated, his voice thin. “The first half seems quite sufficient to drive a man to Bedlam. What, in God’s name, could be the rest?”

“The rest,” said the Voice, a new edge of bitterness in its tone, “is the price of genius. It is the stupidity of the world. It is being a man of flesh and blood, a man with needs and passions, who is treated as a gust of wind, a poltergeist, a draft from a poorly sealed window.”

The brandy glass on the invisible lap tilted and emptied. It was set down with a sharp click on the small table beside the chair, a table Finch had to consciously remember was now occupied. “You speak of it as if it were an ailment.”

“It is a state of being, Finch. A physical fact. I was a student of light. Not your dusty, theoretical light of blackboards and equations, but light as a substance. Light as a force that acts upon the world. I studied optics, biology, chemistry. I asked myself a simple question. What is visibility? It is nothing more than the reflection and absorption of light. An object is visible because it does not let light pass through it cleanly. It deflects it, it soaks it in, it changes its colour. A black cat absorbs the light. A white sheet reflects it. A pane of glass, for the most part, allows it to pass through. Do you follow me so far?”

“It is elementary physics,” Finch said, clutching his glass. “Of course I follow.”

“Good. Do not patronise me. Now, consider the human body. It is a thing of pigments. The haemoglobin in the blood that makes it red. The melanin in the skin, the hair, the iris. It is a complex, messy, opaque vessel. My theory was this. If one could bleach every pigment out of a living body, reduce every tissue to perfect transparency, and then, this is the crucial step, alter the refractive index of that tissue until it was precisely the same as that of air, what would you have?”

Finch stared at the empty chair. The logic, however insane, was horribly clear. “You would have… nothing. You would have a man who did not bend light. A man who was, for all intents and purposes, invisible.”

“Precisely!” The Voice was sharp with triumph. A puff of smoke shot towards the ceiling. “It took me four years. Four years of working in a squalid little room, living on bread and tea, stealing chemicals when my funds ran out. I experimented on cloth, on a cat from the neighbourhood. Poor creature. The process was… unstable at first. It went transparent, but its retinal pigments remained. Two glowing, disembodied feline eyes, floating in the dark. It drove me half mad with terror, but it proved the principle. The final step was myself.”

“You experimented on yourself?” Finch whispered. The audacity was as shocking as the result.

“Who else? Could I trust this to some simpleton? To a world that would clap me in irons or put me on display in a tent? No. I am the sole proprietor of my discovery. The process… well, the process was agony. A burning cold, a radiant fire that seemed to dissolve me from the inside out. For hours I was a shifting, ghostlike figure, a man of glass, of mist. And then, I was gone. I stood before the mirror and saw only the wall of my shabby room behind me. I had done it.”

The pipe was set down, tapping against the invisible saucer of the brandy glass. Finch could hear the faint scrape of ceramic on briarwood.

“But the world, Finch, the world is not designed for an invisible man. I was naked. Clothes, you see, remain perfectly visible. I had to discard them. I was thrust out into the cold, a naked man in the heart of winter, unseen but utterly exposed. Do you know what it feels like to have snow fall upon you when you are invisible? It does not melt at once. It outlines you. I became a walking snowdrift, a shimmering man of white flakes. Rain is worse. It coats you in a glistening film. Dust, mud, fog, they all conspire to betray me. To eat or drink is to perform a magic trick I cannot conceal; the food traces its path down my gullet, a visible digestion for all to see. I am a prisoner of my own triumph.”

Finch felt a sliver of something akin to pity. It was a monstrous tale, a tragedy of hubris that a Greek playwright would have relished. “So you came here. To London.”

“Not at first. I was in a village. Iping. I rented a room, wrapped myself in bandages head to toe, wore dark goggles, a false nose. I pretended I was the victim of a terrible accident. I needed a place to work, to find the means to reverse the process. But people are fools. They are suspicious. They poked and they pried. My money ran out. I had to… acquire more. A little light burglary. Who is to stop a man they cannot see? But I grew clumsy. Reckless, as I said. There was a fire. A great deal of shouting. I had to fight my way out. They saw things. A shirt moving on its own. A poker swinging in the air. The whole town is in an uproar. They are hunting for a ghost, a monster. They have sent word to the police. They will have sent word to London.”

The voice was closer now. Finch flinched as he felt a cold pocket of air move past him. The chair creaked as the unseen weight shifted.

“I walked for days, hiding in woods, stealing scraps of food from farmhouses. I am not a phantom, Finch. I am a man. I get cold, I get hungry, I bleed. A dog can smell me. A child can trip over my outstretched legs. I am more vulnerable than any other soul on this earth, and yet they think me a demon. I saw your name in a newspaper, an article about your lecture on the nature of time. You were described as a man of formidable intellect, a rationalist. I thought, here is a man who will not scream and run. Here is a man who will understand.”

F-Finch swallowed. “Understand what? That you have broken the laws of nature and are now a fugitive?”

“That I need sanctuary!” the Voice snapped, the desperation raw and clear. “I need a room. I need chemicals, equipment. My notes were lost in the fire. I must recreate my work, find the flaw, the missing step that will bring me back. You must help me, Finch. For science. For the greatest discovery in the history of mankind.”

Finch stood up and began to pace the small study. His heart hammered against his ribs. This was not a scientific curiosity sitting in his armchair. It was a desperate, unstable criminal. A man who admitted to theft, to violence. A man who could walk through any locked door, see any secret, commit any crime without being seen. To harbour him was madness. It was conspiracy. It was ruin.

“I cannot,” Finch said, his voice trembling. “It is impossible. The risk, the danger. You are asking me to hide a… a public menace.”

“I am asking you to aid a fellow scientist!” The air crackled with indignation. “Is your world so small, your mind so constrained by petty rules and fears of the local constable, that you would turn your back on this? I offer you a partnership in a discovery that will make Newton seem like a schoolboy with a prism!”

“And what happens when you are discovered here? In my rooms? What am I to tell the authorities? That I was assisting a disembodied voice with his experiments?” Finch gestured wildly at the empty space. “They would lock me away.”

“They will not find me,” the Voice said, its tone dropping to a low, conspiratorial whisper. “Who would ever think to look? I am the perfect secret, Finch. The perfect lodger. I require a bed I shall not be seen in, and food I shall eat in private. In return, I will make you immortal. Your name will be forever linked with mine. Griffin.”

“Griffin,” Finch repeated the name. It gave the terror a handle.

“Yes. Dr. Griffin. And you are Dr. Finch. Colleagues.”

Finch ran a hand through his already dishevelled hair. His mind, usually a fortress of logic and order, was a battlefield of panic and a terrifying, exhilarating curiosity. The scientist in him screamed that this was the opportunity of a lifetime. The rational citizen screamed that it was suicide. To have this creature, this Griffin, in his house, was to invite chaos in and bolt the door behind it.

Suddenly, from the street below, came a sharp, piercing sound. A police whistle.

Finch froze. Griffin fell utterly silent. The only sound in the room was the frantic ticking of the mantel clock, each second a hammer blow. The whistle blew again, closer this time, followed by the sound of running footsteps on the cobblestones and a distant shout.

“They’re searching,” Griffin’s voice was a strained hiss, devoid of its earlier arrogance. It came from a spot near the window now. Finch heard the faint creak of the floorboards. “A patrol. Looking for vagrants, undesirables. They might be going door to door.”

Finch’s blood ran cold. He pictured it. A knock. A pair of uniformed constables on his doorstep. Their polite questions. His own stammering replies. And the invisible, desperate man standing just a few feet away, a silent, deadly witness. What would Griffin do if he felt cornered? The image of a poker swinging in the air flashed in Finch’s mind.

He went to the window, his movements stiff with fear, and peered through the heavy velvet curtains. The gaslight below cast long, dancing shadows. He saw a constable standing on the corner, speaking to a man in a carriage. The search seemed general, not directed, but the threat was palpable. His quiet, ordered world had been breached.

He turned back to the room. “You must go,” he said, his voice a harsh whisper.

“Go where?” The voice was right behind him. Finch yelped and stumbled forward, his heart leaping into his throat. He had not heard him move. It was like being in a cage with an unseen tiger. “Out there? To be hunted in the streets? To be cornered in some filthy alleyway? I will not do it. I will not be taken.”

“But what if they come here?”

“Then you will deal with them,” Griffin stated, not as a request, but as a command. “You are a respectable man, Finch. A scholar. They have no reason to suspect you. A simple denial is all that is required.”

As if summoned by the words, a heavy, authoritative knock sounded on the front door downstairs.

It was a sound Finch had heard a thousand times, the postman, a delivery, a student. But this time it struck him like a physical blow. He felt the blood drain from his face. He looked at the armchair, then at the window, then at the empty space where the voice had come from. The silence from Griffin was absolute, and more terrifying than any threat.

The knock came again, louder this time, impatient.

“Answer it,” Griffin hissed from the darkest corner of the room. “Be calm. Be ordinary. Get rid of them.”

Finch’s legs felt like lead. He could refuse. He could open the door and shout for help. He could expose the entire fantastic nightmare. But what would happen then? Griffin was here, in his rooms. A struggle. An invisible, violent force. Who would the police believe? They would find a raving academic, smelling of brandy, talking of invisible men. His career, his reputation, would be shattered in an instant. And that was the best case. The worst case involved that swinging poker, and a constable lying bleeding on his Persian rug.

He was trapped. Griffin had not just entered his rooms; he had entered his life, and Finch could already feel the prison walls of complicity rising around him.

Taking a deep, ragged breath, he straightened his waistcoat and moved towards the door of the study. He walked down the short hallway, his footsteps echoing in the silent flat. With a trembling hand, he drew the bolt and opened the door.

Two constables stood on his landing. One was a large, red faced man with a thick moustache, the other younger, more alert. The older one held a lantern, which cast a flickering, accusatory light into the hallway.

“Dr. Finch?” the older man asked, his voice a low rumble.

“Yes. I am he,” Finch managed, trying to keep his voice level. “Is there a problem, officer?”

“Evening, sir. Constable Jones. We’re making inquiries in the area. There’s been some trouble over at a public house on the next street. A robbery. The suspect is a big fellow, they say, but strangely quick on his feet. Caused a right panic. We’re asking residents if they’ve seen anyone suspicious lurking about in the last hour or so. Anyone you don’t recognise.”

Finch’s mind raced. A robbery. Griffin had been out. He had needed money, of course. For chemicals, for food, for his grand experiment. The man in his study was not just a fugitive from Iping; he was an active criminal, right here in London.

He forced himself to meet the constable’s gaze. “No, officer. I have been in my study all evening. I have seen and heard nothing out of the ordinary. It has been perfectly quiet.”

The lie felt clumsy and foreign in his mouth. He was certain they could see the pulse hammering in his throat.

The younger constable craned his neck, peering past Finch into the dimly lit hallway. “You’re sure, sir? No strange noises? Shouts?”

“None at all,” Finch said, stepping slightly to block the man’s view. “I have been reading. I assure you, if there had been a commotion, I would have been aware of it.”

Constable Jones grunted, seemingly satisfied. “Right you are, sir. Sorry to have disturbed your reading. Just doing our rounds. Keep your door locked, eh? Nasty business.”

“I will. Thank you, officer. Good night.”

Finch closed the door, his hand shaking so badly he could barely slide the bolt back into place. He leaned his forehead against the cool wood, his eyes squeezed shut. The sound of the constables’ heavy boots descending the stairs was the most beautiful sound he had ever heard.

He stood there for a full minute, breathing deeply, before he turned and walked back to the study. He entered and closed the door behind him. The room was just as he had left it. The half empty brandy decanter on the table, the two glasses, the pipe resting in its saucer. The armchair was empty. The air was still.

For a wild, hopeful moment, he thought Griffin might have gone. That he had seized the opportunity to slip away in the confusion.

“You did well, Finch,” the voice said, startlingly close. It came from his own desk chair, behind him. “A little shaky, but convincing enough for those plodding simpletons.”

Finch spun around. He could hear the faint rustle of cloth as the unseen man shifted his position. He had been there the whole time, watching, listening.

“A robbery?” Finch’s voice was a choked accusation. “You were out committing a robbery?”

“A man must have capital,” Griffin said, unconcerned. “I took a few pounds from a till. It was laughably easy. They practically threw the money at me in their haste to get away from the ‘haunted’ cash box.” A dry, mirthless chuckle echoed in the quiet room. “Now. We have work to do. First thing tomorrow, I will need a list of chemicals. And books. I need to reacquaint myself with the literature. My memory of the exact formulae is… imperfect. And you, my dear Finch, will be my hands and my face. You will be my window to the world.”

Finch stared at the empty chair where the voice resided. The interview with the police had changed everything. Before, he had been a terrified spectator. Now, he was an accessory. He had lied to the law. He had chosen his side, not out of conviction or scientific zeal, but out of pure, animal fear. He was no longer Dr. Finch, the respected academic. He was the accomplice of the Invisible Man.

“And if I refuse?” he whispered, though he already knew the answer.

The air grew cold. The faint chewing sound had stopped. The relaxed tone was gone, replaced by something flat and hard as iron.

“You will not refuse,” said Griffin. “You are a man of logic, Finch. Consider your position. You know my secret. That makes you a liability. But you are also my only asset. I cannot allow you to become the former. We are partners now, whether you like it or not. Our fates are entwined. My success is your success. My discovery… well, my discovery will be your ruin.”

The words hung in the air, each a perfectly formed crystal of dread. My discovery will be your ruin. There was no argument to be made, no plea to be entered. It was a statement of fact, as absolute and inescapable as gravity. The faint rustle of cloth ceased. The room fell into a profound silence, a heavy, listening stillness that was more terrifying than any threat. Finch’s own breathing sounded like a blacksmith’s bellows in his ears. He was a man of logic. Griffin was right about that. And logic dictated a single, brutal conclusion: he was a fly caught in a web spun by an invisible spider. To struggle was to invite the final, fatal bite. To remain still was to be consumed at a slower, more agonizing pace.

“Very well,” Finch said, the words tasting like ash in his mouth. He did not look at the desk chair. He looked at the pattern on the Persian rug, tracing the intricate floral designs with his eyes as if they held the key to some forgotten, peaceful world. “I will… assist you.”

“I knew you would see reason,” Griffin’s voice was calm again, the iron fist sheathed once more in a velvet glove of academic civility. “It is the only rational course. Do not think of it as coercion, Finch. Think of it as a collaboration. The most important scientific collaboration in human history. You will be remembered.”

Remembered as what? Finch thought, a wave of despair washing over him. The madman’s lackey? The coward who enabled a monster? He felt a sudden, phantom chill, as if his name were already being carved onto a tombstone of infamy.

“Now,” the voice continued, brisk and businesslike. “Get some sleep. You look dreadful. We have a long day tomorrow. I will require you to be rested and sharp. Your nerves are a liability we cannot afford.”

The suggestion of sleep was a cruel joke. How could any man sleep, knowing an invisible, homicidal genius was sharing his rooms? Yet, the command had been given. Finch turned, his movements stiff and automatic, and walked towards the study door. He did not say good night. The civilities of his former life seemed obscene in this new reality. As his hand touched the doorknob, Griffin spoke again, a final, parting thought.

“Oh, and Finch. Leave the study door ajar. I find I dislike being enclosed. It’s a… recent aversion.”

Finch’s blood ran cold. The request was not about comfort. It was about control. Griffin wanted no barriers, no locked doors between them. He wanted Finch to know that at any moment, in any room of his own home, he was being watched. That there was no sanctuary, no escape. Finch pulled the door, leaving it open by a hand’s breadth, the sliver of darkness a gaping wound into his private life. He retreated to his bedroom and lay down on top of the covers, still fully dressed, staring into the oppressive blackness of the ceiling. Every creak of the old building, every distant shout from the street below, was the sound of Griffin moving, of Griffin coming for him. Sleep did not come. He merely drifted in and out of a state of exhausted, vigilant terror until the grey, watery light of a London dawn began to seep through the curtains.

He rose and performed his morning ablutions with the sense of a man going through the motions on the day of his execution. He shaved with a trembling hand, nicking himself twice on the chin. The sight of his own blood, small crimson beads welling on his skin, was grotesquely vivid. When he returned to the study, a glass of water in hand, the voice was waiting for him.

“Good morning, Finch. I trust you are ready to be useful. Take a seat. Pen and paper.”

The voice came from the armchair by the fire, where he himself had been sitting just last night. Finch obediently sat at his desk, took out a fresh sheet of foolscap, and dipped his nib into the inkwell. The scratching of the pen on the paper was the only sound for a moment.

“First, the books,” Griffin began, his tone that of a professor dictating a syllabus. “I need a current copy of Ganot’s Physics. And Valentin’s Practical Chemistry. Ensure they are the latest editions. My own knowledge is some years out of date, and I must be certain no new discoveries have rendered my calculations obsolete. Also, find me anything you can on optical density and refractive indices. Specifically regarding organic pigments. Check the university library. Use your credentials.”

Finch wrote, his handwriting tight and strained. The simple act of sourcing books, a task that had once been the gentle rhythm of his academic life, now felt like gathering supplies for a black mass.

“Now, for the more practical items,” Griffin continued. The voice seemed to be pacing now, moving from the armchair to the window and back again. Finch did not dare to look up. He kept his eyes fixed on the growing list. “I require a list of chemicals. You will procure these from a proper chemical supplier, not some local apothecary. Go to Jennings and Son on Fleet Street. They are accustomed to laboratory orders and will ask fewer questions.”

Finch’s heart hammered against his ribs. This was the true test. This was the errand of a co-conspirator.

“Ready?” the voice asked, impatient.

“Ready,” Finch croaked.

“Potassium permanganate, one pound. Sulphuric acid, concentrated, a pint. Strychnine hydrochloride… two ounces.”

Finch’s pen froze over the paper. Strychnine. A virulent poison. His mind recoiled. This was not for a reversal formula. This was for something else entirely. Something violent.

“Did you hear me, Finch?” The voice was sharp, cutting through his paralysis. “Two ounces. Do not get the sulphate by mistake. The hydrochloride is more soluble.”

“What… what do you need strychnine for?” Finch whispered, the question escaping his lips before he could stop it.

The pacing stopped. Finch could feel the unseen eyes boring into him. The silence stretched, thin and taut.

“For my work,” Griffin said at last, his voice devoid of any emotion. “A reagent. It has certain… properties I wish to investigate. You are my hands and my face, Finch, not my conscience. That is a luxury neither of us can afford. Now, write it down.”

Finch’s hand moved, compelled by a will that was no longer his own. He wrote the word ‘Strychnine’ and it looked like a death sentence on the page. The list continued, a litany of strange and caustic substances. Anhydrous ether. Chloroform. A selection of alkaline metals. By the time Griffin was finished, the page was nearly full.

“That will suffice for a start,” the voice said, sounding satisfied. “You will go today. Now. Give me the list.”

Finch hesitated. “How?”

A dry chuckle. “Leave it on the desk. I am not entirely helpless.”

Finch stood up and walked out of the study, feeling a strange sense of unreality. He went to his bedroom to fetch his coat and wallet. He put on his hat, his hands fumbling with the brim. He was a puppet, and the strings were being pulled from the next room. Before he left, he stood in the hallway, listening. He heard the faint crinkle of paper from the study, the sound of his own handwriting being examined by an invisible man. The sheer, maddening impossibility of it all nearly made him laugh, a high, unhinged sound that he choked back into a cough.

The walk to Fleet Street was a new form of torture. London, his city, had become a hostile landscape. Every passerby seemed to stare a moment too long. Every constable on the corner seemed to be watching him, his pockets already heavy with imagined guilt. He clutched the money Griffin had provided, the ‘capital’ from the previous night’s robbery, and the feel of the coins was oily and unclean. He imagined Griffin was following him. It was a paranoid fantasy, but a powerful one. Was that sudden gust of wind just the breeze, or the rush of an unseen body passing him by? Was that flicker of movement in a shop window a reflection, or something more? He found himself looking for signs: a discarded cigarette butt still glowing, a puddle disturbed by a footstep that made no sound. He saw nothing, which only made it worse. The threat was perfect, undetectable.

Jennings and Son was a cavern of dark wood and glass, smelling of sulphur and formaldehyde. Hundreds of labeled bottles lined the shelves, a library of potential miracles and disasters. A small, balding man with spectacles perched on his nose looked up from a ledger as Finch entered.

“Dr. Finch. A pleasure. It has been some time. What can we do for the university today?”

Finch’s prepared speech felt like sawdust in his mouth. “Good morning, Mr. Jennings. I am embarking on a new line of private research. A rather… complex organic synthesis. I have a list of requirements.”

He passed the paper across the counter. Jennings adjusted his spectacles and scanned the list, his thin eyebrows rising slightly.

“My, my. This is quite the cocktail, Doctor. Sulphuric acid, strychnine… This is not for the undergraduate laboratory, I hope.” He gave a small, professional smile, but his eyes were sharp and inquisitive.

“Hardly,” Finch said, forcing a chuckle that sounded like a dry leaf skittering across pavement. “It is a private venture. A study into nerve tissue staining techniques. The strychnine is for a control preparation, of course. To observe its effects at a cellular level.”

It was a plausible lie, a good lie. It was rooted in his own field of medicine, and it sounded impressively technical. He had rehearsed it in his mind a dozen times on the walk over. Yet, he felt the heat rising in his collar, the sweat prickling on his scalp. He felt like an imposter in his own skin.

Jennings nodded, seemingly satisfied. “Of course, of course. Very fascinating. It will take a few moments to gather these. The strychnine, you understand, I must log. Regulations.”

“Perfectly understandable,” Finch said, his voice tight.

As Jennings bustled off into the back room, Finch was left alone in the shop. He stared at the rows of bottles, their labels a catalog of creation and destruction. He was a link in a chain of causation he could not comprehend, purchasing poison for a man who did not exist to the world. A man who could kill, and leave no trace. The shop bell tinkled, and another customer entered, a portly man in a tweed suit. Finch flinched, turning away, trying to make himself as small as possible. He felt marked, tainted. He was no longer a doctor, a man of science and healing. He was the purveyor of a monster’s tools.

Jennings returned with a heavy wooden crate. Each bottle was carefully packed in straw. “Here we are, Doctor. I have made up the invoice. Will you be taking this with you, or shall I have it sent to the university?”

“I will take it,” Finch said quickly, perhaps too quickly. “My new laboratory is at my residence. For convenience.”

“Very good.” Jennings slid the invoice across the counter. “That will be seven pounds, four shillings.”

Finch counted out the money, the unfamiliar coins and notes feeling slick and foreign in his hand. He signed the poisons register for the strychnine, his signature, usually a confident flourish, now a jagged, nervous scrawl. He felt Jennings’s eyes on him as he did it. With a grunt of effort, he lifted the heavy crate.

“Good day to you, Doctor. And the best of luck with your… research.”

“And to you, Mr. Jennings.”

Finch backed out of the shop, turning and nearly stumbling on the pavement. The crate was heavy, a physical burden that mirrored the crushing weight on his soul. He hailed a cab, unable to face the long, paranoid walk back with his toxic cargo. The journey was a blur of anxiety. He sat with the crate on the seat beside him, a box of Pandora’s evils, and stared out at the blur of Londoners going about their lives, blissfully unaware of the phantom that now haunted his own.

When he finally arrived back at his apartment building, he felt a flicker of hope that Griffin might have vanished, that the whole affair had been a fever dream. The hope died the moment he stepped into the hallway. The study door was still ajar. The flat was silent, but it was an occupied silence. He carried the crate into the study and set it down on the floor with a heavy thud.

“You were longer than I anticipated,” the voice said. It came from the desk chair again. He had been waiting.

“There were formalities,” Finch said, his breath coming in ragged gasps from the exertion. “The strychnine had to be signed for.”

“Did he seem suspicious? The chemist?”

“I… I do not think so. I told him it was for nerve tissue research.”

“Clever,” Griffin said, with no warmth in his voice. “Let me see the invoice.”

Finch took the paper from his pocket and placed it on the desk. He watched, mesmerized, as the paper lifted into the air, held by an unseen hand. It tilted one way, then another, as if being read by a connoisseur.

“Seven pounds. An acceptable expense. Unpack the chemicals, Finch. Place them on the table. And then go and fetch the books.”

The commands were coming faster now. The time for acclimatization was over. The work was beginning. For the next hour, Finch was an automaton. He unpacked the bottles, their glass cool and smooth against his trembling fingers. He lined them up on his mahogany reading table, a grotesque parody of a cocktail party. The acrid smell of some of the chemicals already seemed to taint the bookish air of his study. Then, he left again, walking in a daze to the university library. He moved through the hallowed halls of his own institution like a ghost, his colleagues nodding to him, their friendly greetings turning to ash in his ears. He was a traitor in their midst. He used his key to access the restricted stacks, found the books Griffin had demanded, and checked them out, the librarian smiling at him, oblivious.

He returned near dusk. The study was dark, but he did not dare light a lamp without permission. He set the stack of heavy books on the desk.

“Good,” the voice said from the darkness. “You have been efficient. I will grant you that.”

There was a sound of a cork being pried from a bottle, then a soft glugging noise. A sharp, antiseptic smell, the smell of ether, filled the room.

“My first task,” Griffin’s voice explained, sounding almost cheerful, “is to create a bleach. Something to deal with an unfortunate bloodstain. It seems my little excursion last night was not entirely without… complication. A shopkeeper with more bravery than sense. He will recover. But his blood is on my gloves and the cuff of my trousers. A problem of visibility, you see.”

Finch stared into the gloom, his heart a cold, hard knot in his chest. A robbery. A man injured. Bloodstains. He was washing the blood off a phantom’s hands.

“Tomorrow,” Griffin continued, the clinking of glass punctuating his words, “we will begin the real work. The calculations. I will need you to read to me from the physics text. My eyes… well, I have no eyes to speak of at present. You will be my eyes, Finch. Just as you are my hands.”

The room was quiet for a moment, save for the gentle sloshing of liquid. Finch stood frozen, a prisoner in his own home, the darkness a shroud. Then, the voice spoke again, soft, musing, and more terrifying than any command.

“It is a strange feeling, you know. To stand in a room and be less than the air. To be nothing. But soon, very soon, I will be everything. And you, my dear, logical Dr. Finch, you will have had a part to play in the dawning of my new world. Whether you wish it or not.”

The words settled in the silence, each one a stone dropping into the deep, still well of Finch’s terror. He did not move. He barely breathed. The darkness in the study was no longer simply an absence of light; it was a presence, an entity, and it had a voice. It was a shroud woven from fear and the cloying, sweet scent of ether. He was an accomplice. He was a tool. He was nothing.

“You may retire for the night, Finch,” the voice said, its musing tone replaced by one of clinical dismissal. “But do not think of leaving the house. I assure you, it would be a regrettable decision. I am a very light sleeper. And I am quicker than you can imagine.”

A soft, almost imperceptible rustle of cloth nearby made Finch flinch violently, a strangled gasp escaping his lips. He imagined an unseen hand gesturing towards the door.

“Go,” Griffin commanded. “I have work to do. This stain will not do at all. It is an untidy variable in a world that requires precision.”

Finch turned, his movements stiff and jerky like a marionette. He fumbled for the doorknob, his palm slick with sweat. He pulled the door open and stepped into the relative light of the hallway, where a single gas lamp burned low, casting long, dancing shadows. He did not look back. He pulled the door shut, the click of the latch sounding like a vault sealing him out of his own life, or perhaps sealing his doom within it.

He stood in the hallway for a long time, listening. He could hear faint sounds from within the study. The clink of a glass stopper. A soft, rhythmic scrubbing sound. He was cleaning the blood. The blood of a man who had merely tried to protect his property. Finch felt a wave of nausea so profound he had to brace himself against the wall, the cool plaster a shock against his heated skin. What had he done? What had he allowed into his home? He was a man of books, of theories, of quiet contemplation. His life was a carefully constructed edifice of routine and intellectual pursuit. Now, a phantom had walked through its walls and was busy scrubbing blood from its clothes in the heart of it all.

He made his way upstairs to his bedroom, each step a monumental effort. He did not light a lamp. He preferred the darkness now, the familiar shadows of his own furniture a small, pathetic comfort. He undressed slowly, folding his clothes with the force of lifelong habit, a habit that seemed to be from another man’s existence. He lay down in his bed, the sheets cold and unwelcoming. Sleep, he knew, was an impossible luxury.

His house was no longer his. Every creak of the floorboards, every groan of the old structure settling, was now a potential footstep. The whisper of the wind in the eaves was the whisper of the unseen man. Finch lay rigid, his ears straining, listening for the sounds from downstairs. He heard the study door open. He heard soft, bare footsteps on the floorboards of the hall below. Pacing. Back and forth, back and forth. A restless energy emanated from below, a palpable vibration of intellect and simmering rage. Finch squeezed his eyes shut, but the image of the pacing specter was burned onto the inside of his eyelids. Less than the air. Soon to be everything.

Sometime before dawn, exhaustion finally claimed him, pulling him into a shallow, dreamless state that was less sleep and more a temporary cessation of fear. He was woken abruptly. A hand, cold and firm, was on his shoulder.

Finch cried out, scrambling backwards in the bed until he hit the headboard. The room was still dark, the grey light of early morning barely penetrating the thick curtains.

“It is morning, Finch,” Griffin’s voice said, seemingly right beside his ear. It was calm, matter of fact. The hand was gone. “The time for sleeping is over. We have a great deal to accomplish.”

Finch’s heart hammered against his ribs so hard he thought it might break them. To be touched by him. To have him in his bedroom, standing over him as he slept. The violation was absolute. He could smell the faint, clean scent of the bleaching chemicals on the air, a scent that now seemed to cling to Griffin like a perfume.

“Get dressed,” the voice commanded. “Make coffee. Bring a pot to the study, and a cup for yourself. You will need your wits about you today.”

The presence left. Finch did not hear footsteps, only felt a subtle shift in the air, the sense of a body moving away. He sat in his bed for a full minute, trembling, before the ingrained terror of disobeying that voice forced him into motion. He dressed with clumsy fingers, his mind a frantic blank. He went downstairs. The house felt different in the daylight. Sunlight streamed through the windows, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air, but it brought no warmth, no comfort. It only served to highlight the wrongness of it all, making the unseen horror somehow more stark.

The study door was ajar. He pushed it open and peered inside. The room was a disaster. The books he had brought were open on the floor, their pages ruffled. The chemical bottles were rearranged on his reading table, some of them uncorked. A white linen handkerchief and a strip of dark wool, presumably from a trouser cuff, lay on a porcelain plate, both immaculately clean but damp. The acrid smell was stronger now, a chemical fog that stung his nostrils.

He retreated to the kitchen and performed the familiar ritual of making coffee, his hands moving on their own. The simple, domestic task was surreal. He was preparing breakfast for a monster. He poured the coffee into his best silver pot, his hand shaking so much that dark liquid sloshed onto the tray. He carried it back to the study.

“Place it on the desk,” Griffin said from the wingback chair by the cold fireplace. The chair was turned away from the door, so Finch could not see the impression of a body in its cushions. He only knew Griffin was there. “And take your seat at the reading table. The physics text. The one on optics and the refraction of light. Open it to the chapter on refractive indices.”

Finch did as he was told. He sat at his own table, which now felt like a laboratory bench. He poured himself a cup of coffee, the rattling of the porcelain a testament to his shattered nerves. He found the book, its familiar leather cover offering no solace. He opened it to the correct chapter. The page was a blur of equations and dense, academic prose that had once been his entire world.

“Begin reading,” Griffin instructed. “And do not stumble. I need the information, not a dramatic performance.”

So it began. Finch read, his voice a dry monotone. He read of Snell’s law, of Cauchy’s equation, of the relationship between light, pigment, and perception. He read passages he had practically memorized, theories he had taught to students for years. But now, the words were not his. They were being fed into the invisible, insatiable mind in the chair.

Occasionally, Griffin would interrupt. “Stop. Reread that last theorem. Slowly.” Or, “That is flawed. The author fails to account for the cellular level. The refractive index of protoplasm itself must be altered to match that of the air. It is not about pigment, you fool, it is about presence. About density. Read on.”

Finch would obey, his own considerable intellect feeling sluggish and inadequate. Griffin’s mind was a thing of terrifying brilliance. He absorbed the complex information, processed it, and found its flaws in an instant. He was not merely reviewing the science; he was wrestling with it, bending it to his will. Finch was just the mouthpiece. The hours crawled by. Finch’s throat grew raw. His eyes ached from staring at the page. The coffee grew cold in his cup.

Around midday, a new sound began. The squeak of a pen nib on paper. Finch glanced up from his book. A piece of foolscap on a small side table next to the wingback chair was filling with elegant, spidery script. A fountain pen, one of Finch’s own, was moving across the page, seemingly of its own volition. It would write a furious string of equations, then pause, hovering in the air, before darting back to the page. Finch stared, mesmerized and horrified. He was watching a ghost perform calculus.

“Do not gawk, Finch,” the voice snapped, not even looking up from its invisible calculations. “You are losing our place. Continue from the section on light absorption in organic tissue.”

Finch snapped his eyes back to the book, his heart pounding. The sheer strangeness of it was a physical blow. He continued to read, his voice cracking. He felt a growing sense of despair. He was not just helping a criminal; he was helping a genius. A mad, terrifying genius who was using the very laws of physics that Finch so respected to perfect an abomination.

When the grandfather clock in the hall chimed two, the pen finally stopped. It was placed carefully back in its holder.

“That will be sufficient for now,” Griffin said. The voice sounded tired but triumphant. “My theoretical model is nearly complete. The flaw was in the initial stabilization process. I was so concerned with the transition that I neglected the biological repercussions. The body fights it. It tries to become visible again, on a cellular level. It causes… discomfort. And a certain neurological irritability.”

A dry, mirthless chuckle came from the chair. “A profound understatement. Now, Finch, I am hungry. You will prepare a lunch. Cold meat, bread, some cheese. And a bottle of beer. Leave it on the tray outside the door. Do not attempt to watch me. My methods of consumption are not for the squeamish.”

Feeling like a butler in a madhouse, Finch retreated to the kitchen once more. He prepared the food with numb efficiency. The request was so mundane, so human. It made the situation even more grotesque. He sliced the cold roast beef, arranged the cheese on a plate, and found a bottle of ale. He placed it all on a tray and set it outside the closed study door, as instructed. He knocked softly.

“It is done,” he said, his voice a croak.

“Excellent. Go to the sitting room. Read a newspaper. Do not disturb me for one hour.”

Finch did as he was told. He sat in the formal, unused sitting room, the silence of the house pressing in on him. He picked up the morning paper, but the headlines swam before his eyes. He found his gaze drawn to a small article on an inner page. “Shopkeeper Assaulted in Brutal Robbery.” The story described how Mr. Bunting, the owner of the local curiosity shop, had been struck on the head during a late night break-in. The assailant had stolen a small amount of cash and some chemical supplies. The police were baffled, as the shop was locked from the inside and there was no sign of forced entry. Mr. Bunting, now recovering, could only recall a sense of confusion, a voice from nowhere, and then a sudden, violent blow.

Finch let the paper fall into his lap. His hands were shaking again. Bunting. A good man. Finch had bought trinkets from him for years. And Griffin had struck him down without a thought, dismissing him as a man with “more bravery than sense.” The blood he had washed away was Bunting’s blood. The chemicals Finch had purchased had been used to erase the evidence of the assault. His complicity was no longer an abstract terror; it had a name and a face.

When the hour was up, he returned to the study door. The tray was there, but the plate was clean, the bread gone, the beer bottle empty. There was not a crumb out of place. It was as if the food had simply vanished. He took a deep breath and knocked.

“Enter.”

He opened the door. The room was as he had left it, but the atmosphere had changed. The air crackled with a new, feverish energy.

“The experiment must be completed, Finch,” Griffin’s voice said, sharp and decisive. He was pacing again, a restless orbit around the desk. Finch could feel the subtle currents of air as the unseen form moved. “My calculations are sound. I can reverse the process. But it requires a catalyst, a reagent I do not possess. And more importantly, it will be… volatile. The process of becoming visible again will be agonizing. The cellular structure must be forced to absorb light, to reclaim its opacity. It cannot be done piecemeal. It must be total and instantaneous.”

He paused, and Finch felt the invisible gaze fix upon him. He felt a cold dread snake up his spine, a premonition of what was to come.

“I cannot risk the procedure on myself first,” Griffin stated, his voice devoid of any emotion. “The potential for catastrophic failure is too high. An error in the sequence, a miscalculation in the dosage… the results could be unspeakable. I could be solidified halfway. I could be burned from the inside out by the sudden absorption of energy. No, that is not an acceptable risk.”

Finch stood frozen, his mouth dry. He knew what was coming. He knew it with a certainty that was more terrifying than anything he had yet experienced.

“I will need a test subject,” Griffin continued, his voice as calm as a lecturer explaining a simple principle. “Someone to prove the formula. Someone whose biological response can be closely monitored. And you, my dear Dr. Finch, are conveniently located. You are intelligent enough to understand my instructions, and you are here. Your participation will be invaluable.”

The room seemed to shrink, the air growing thick and heavy, impossible to breathe. Finch looked around his study, at his beloved books, his comfortable chair, the artifacts of a life that was now over. He was no longer a collaborator. He was no longer a tool. He was to be the experiment. He was to be the raw material upon which this madman would perfect his monstrous creation, or fail in the most hideous way imaginable. His own body was to become the next laboratory.

“You will fetch the final components for me tomorrow,” Griffin said, his voice now laced with an impatient, predatory excitement. “And then, Dr. Finch… then we shall see if your body is as logical and predictable as your mind. Then we shall make history together.”
