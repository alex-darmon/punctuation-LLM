Enter Cassian and Decius.

DECIUS
I pray you, Cassian, put that humour by.
The market square is no fit place for wit
That bites so sharp; the Corvin faction’s near.

CASSIAN
Let them be near! What, am I a summer’s cloud
To be dispersed by any passing wind?
Thou art so full of caution, Decius,
Thou wouldst not praise the sun for fear its heat
Might one day parch the fields. Thou wouldst take issue
With a rose for having thorns, or with the sea
For being wet. Thy blood runs colder than
A miser’s charity.

DECIUS
And thine so hot,
It boils away thy reason. Be but still.

Enter Lucius and his followers.

CASSIAN
Too late for stillness, friend. See where he comes,
The peacock of the House of Corvin. Mark
How he doth strut, as if the very stones
Were honoured by his tread.

LUCIUS
Good morrow, sirs.
I seek the son of Valerius, Flavius.
Doth he conceal himself amongst his friends?

CASSIAN
Conceal? A Valerius doth not conceal.
He may be occupied with weighty things,
With sums and figures that thy simple mind
Could never comprehend. We trade in silk,
Not empty threats.

LUCIUS
Thou hast a tongue as sharp as any blade,
And half so useful. I have words for him,
Not for his barking dog.

CASSIAN
A dog, say’st thou?
If I be a dog, then art thou the rat
I am sworn to hunt. And I will have thee, sir.
Here is my collar. [_Touches his sword’s hilt._] Here my teeth to bite.

DECIUS
Cassian, enough! We are in public view.
The Prefect’s watch will be upon us soon.
Forbear this talk.

LUCIUS
Let them come. I do not care.
But look, fortune hath saved me the long search.

Enter Flavius.

Here is the man I seek. Flavius, you knave,
Your father’s enterprise doth steal the light
From honest merchants. You are but a thief
Dressed in fine garments.

FLAVIUS
Lucius, that name
I will not own. The reasons that I have
To wish you well do far outweigh your spleen,
And pardon this rough greeting. I do see
You know me not. So fare you well.

LUCIUS
What, dost thou turn away? This shall not serve
To mend the injury thy house hath done.
Turn, coward, and draw thy sword.

FLAVIUS
I do protest I have no quarrel here.
The name of Corvin I do hold as dear
As my own name, for reasons you will know
In some near future. Therefore, be content.

CASSIAN
O weak and shameful peace! O merchant’s truce!
[_Draws his sword._]
Hath honour been so sold and bargained for?
Lucius, you puffed up pigeon, will you fight?

LUCIUS
With thee? With all my heart. Thou art the fool
That stands before the master.

CASSIAN
Come then, sir, your passado. Let us see
If your steel is as sharp as is your tongue.

[_They fight._]

FLAVIUS
Hold, Cassian! Gentle Lucius, put thy sword up.
Decius, help me part them! For shame, friends,
The Prefect hath forbid these brawls in public.
Hold, Lucius! Good Cassian!

[_Flavius steps between them. Under his arm, Lucius wounds Cassian._]

LUCIUS
[_Seeing he has drawn blood._]
Away! Let’s part.

[_Exeunt Lucius and his followers._]

CASSIAN
I am hurt. A pox on both your counting houses!
I am dispatched. Is he fled, and hath no scratch?

DECIUS
What, Cassian, art thou wounded?

CASSIAN
Ay, ay, a prick, a trifle. But ’tis enough.
Go, find a man of ledgers, fetch him quick.
Tell him to settle my accounts.

FLAVIUS
Courage, man; the wound cannot be deep.

CASSIAN
No, ’tis not so wide as a ledger book, nor so deep as a debtor’s prison, but ’twill serve. Ask for me on the morrow, and you shall find me a settled man. I am spiced, I warrant, for this world. A pox on both your fortunes! Zounds, a gull, a braggart, a popinjay, who fights by the rules of fencing masters! Why in God’s name came you between us? I was stabbed beneath your arm.

FLAVIUS
I thought to keep the peace.

CASSIAN
Help me to some bench, Decius, or I shall fall.
A pox on both your houses! They have made
A poor man’s penny of me. I have it,
And soundly too. Your houses!

[_Exeunt Cassian and Decius._]

FLAVIUS
This gentleman, the Prefect’s own good friend,
My truest comrade, hath his mortal wound
For my sake; my good name is ruin’d now
By Lucius’ slander, Lucius, that I hoped
To call my brother. O my sweet Lavinia,
Thy love hath made me soft and womanish
And melted all the temper of my soul.

Re-enter Decius.

DECIUS
O Flavius, Flavius, brave Cassian is dead.
That fiery spirit now hath flown to heaven,
Who all too soon did hold the earth in scorn.

FLAVIUS
This day’s dark business will stain future days;
This is the start of woes that others end.

Re-enter Lucius.

DECIUS
Look, here comes the furious Lucius back again.

FLAVIUS
Again in triumph, and Cassian is slain?
Then gentle mercy, get thee to the sky,
And hot-eyed fury be my captain now!
Now, Lucius, take the ‘thief’ thou gavest me
Back to thy throat, for Cassian’s noble soul
Is but a moment’s journey overhead,
And waits for thine to bear it company.
For thou or I, or both, must follow him.

LUCIUS
Thou wretched boy, that wast his friend below,
Shalt be his partner now in death.

FLAVIUS
This sword shall settle that.

[_They fight. Lucius falls._]

DECIUS
Flavius, away, be gone!
The citizens are roused, and Lucius slain.
Stand not amazed. The Prefect will pronounce
A sentence of pure death if thou art caught.
Hence, fly from here, away!

FLAVIUS
O, I am ruin’s fool!

DECIUS
Why dost thou linger?

[_Exit Flavius._]

Enter Citizens.

FIRST CITIZEN
Where is the man that struck down Lucius?
Cassian’s killer, which way did he run?

DECIUS
There lies the man, slain by young Flavius.

Enter Prefect, with Lord Corvin, Lady Valerius, and others.

PREFECT
Where are the authors of this violent scene?

DECIUS
O noble Prefect, I can tell the tale
Of this sad day’s most miserable fray.
There lies the man, whom Flavius’ hand did slay,
That killed thy kinsman, witty Cassian.

LORD CORVIN
Lucius, my son! O, my posterity!
Prefect, by all the laws of God and man,
As thou art just, for blood of ours we crave
The blood of Valerius. My son, my son!

PREFECT
Decius, who did begin this bloody brawl?

DECIUS
Lucius, here slain, whom Flavius’ hand laid low.
Young Flavius spoke him fair, and bade him think
How thin the cause was, and withal he urged
Your high displeasure. All this he did speak
With sober breath, calm look, and bended knee,
But could not make a truce with the hot rage
Of Lucius, who, to reason wholly deaf,
Did lunge with his sharp point at Cassian’s heart;
Who, being just as hot, turned point to point,
And with a soldier’s pride, with one hand beat
Death’s chilly kiss aside, and with the other
Sent it to Lucius, whose quick skillfulness
Returned it. Flavius, he cries aloud
‘Hold, friends! Friends, part!’ and like a flash of light
His ready arm beat down their fatal swords
And rushed between them; underneath whose arm
A spiteful thrust from Lucius struck the life
From valiant Cassian, and then Lucius fled.
But presently came back to Flavius,
Whose heart had newly welcomed in revenge,
And to it they did go like thunderclaps; for ere
I could draw sword to part them, Lucius fell,
And as he fell did Flavius turn and fly.
This is the truth, or let poor Decius die.

LORD CORVIN
He is a Valerius, and speaks for them.
Affection makes him lie, he is not true.
I’ll wager twenty men fought in this strife
And all those twenty took my poor son’s life.
I beg for justice, which you, Prefect, owe;
Flavius slew Lucius, Flavius must not go
On living.

PREFECT
Flavius slew him, who slew Cassian.
Who now should pay the price for my dear kinsman?

LADY VALERIUS
Not Flavius, Prefect, he was Cassian’s friend.
His crime is but what law itself would end,
The life of Lucius.

PREFECT
And for that same crime,
Immediately we exile him from this time.
My own blood has been spilt in your vile feud,
My kinsman’s life by your crude hands subdued.
But I will tax you with so great a fine
That you will rue you ever crossed that line.

LORD CORVIN
A fine? An exile? Is this justice, then?
A gentle chastisement, a mere rebuke
For spilling out the lifeblood of my son?
You send him on a tour of foreign lands,
Perhaps to see the world, to learn new tongues,
While my son’s tongue is silenced in the dust.
This is no sentence, Prefect, but a prize!
You favor them, the killers of my boy,
Because your blood was mixed with theirs in death.
So Cassian’s ghost buys Flavius a pass
To walk the earth, while Lucius must grow cold.
Is this the law? Then I renounce the law!

PREFECT
Hold your rash tongue, Lord Corvin, lest it bring
Still greater sorrow to your grieving house.
I am not blind to what your heart desires,
A death for death, a body for a body,
To see the scales of justice balanced with
The weight of gore. But think what you demand.
Young Flavius, though his hands are red with blood,
Did act in passion, for a friend just slain,
A friend who was my kinsman, my own flesh.
Shall I send to the block the very man
Who did the thing the law itself would do?
Avenge a murder? Lucius was the first
To draw the blade and let out human life.
His was the crime that set this wheel in spin.
If Flavius dies, then by that logic, Lucius
Deserved his fate. Which is it you would have?
A clean slate, wiped by this unhappy exile,
Or endless cycles of revenge and death
Until our city’s gutters run with blood
Of all our children, and the old men weep
On tombs too numerous for them to count?
I choose the former. Exile is his doom.
And for the fine, it shall be ruinous.
Your feud has cost this city peace and blood.
The price for that will be the Valerius gold.
Their coffers shall be emptied to the state
To pay for watchmen, and to soothe the wounds
That you have carved upon our common flesh.
This is my judgement. It is final now.

LADY VALERIUS
My lord, have mercy. Banishment for him,
Who stood a pillar of his father’s house?
You take the limb, and now you take the root.
Our fortune is the earth in which we grow.
To take our gold is but to kill us slowly,
To leave us barren, withered on the vine.
He is my son, my only son alive,
Let that be grief enough for me to bear.
Do not add penury to my despair.

PREFECT
Your son alive is more than Corvin has.
Be grateful for the mercy you are shown.
The fine is set. Lord Corvin, take your son.
Give him the rites that noble birth demands.
Let this day’s sorrow be a monument,
A stone of warning for the years to come.
Remove these bodies. Cleanse this bloody street.
The sight of them puts murder in the air.

Attendants come forward and bear the bodies of Lucius and Cassian away. Lord Corvin watches them go, his face a mask of iron.

LORD CORVIN
I will obey, my lord, because I must.
The wolf must bow when the great lion roars.
But do not think my heart is satisfied.
A father’s love is deeper than your law,
And his remembrance longer than your reign.
I shall take up my son, but not my peace.
This is not ended. Mark my solemn words.

[_Exit Lord Corvin, followed by his retainers._]

LADY VALERIUS
And what of me? My son, a fugitive!
Where does he hide? How shall he live abroad?
A stranger in strange lands, with empty purse,
Hunted and haunted by this dreadful day.
O, Decius, you who saw it, tell me true,
Was there no other way? No other path?

DECIUS
There was no time for paths, my lady, only
For lightning strokes of passion and of steel.
He did what any man of honor would,
To see his friend cut down by such a blow.
The fault was in the stars, or in the feud
That set these sons of noble houses at
Each other’s throats for trifles light as air.
He is alive. Take comfort in that word.

PREFECT
Comfort is a luxury we have lost.
Go to your house, Lady Valerius.
Bar up your gates and pray this chapter’s closed.
Inform your son, if you have means to do so,
That if he’s found within the city walls
When next the sun does climb into the sky,
His exile turns to sentence of pure death.
Now go. The city has a fever, and
You are the cause. We must all seek the cure.

[_Exit Lady Valerius, weeping, supported by Decius._]

PREFECT
(To his remaining Lords)
A bitter business. See how one hot spark
Can set a forest of old grievances
Ablaze. We have but banked the fire for now,
Not put it out. Keep double watch tonight.
Send patrols to the Corvin residence
And to the Valerius house. We cannot stand
A war within our walls. This city must
Have peace, though I must rule with iron hands
To forge it from their hatred and their grief.

[_Exeunt._]

ACT I, SCENE II

A room in the house of Valerius. The furnishings are rich but the mood is somber. AURELIA, sister to Flavius, paces anxiously.

AURELIA
The air outside is thick with ugly whispers.
I hear them from the servants in the hall,
I see them in the frightened eyes of maids
Who scurry past and dare not meet my gaze.
They speak of fighting, and of blood spilled deep
Upon the stones of the old market square.
They speak the name of Lucius, and my brother,
And Cassian. O, why does mother stay
So long away? Each moment is a year
That lays another wrinkle on my heart.
My mind creates such horrors for the truth,
That truth itself, however grim, would be
A sweet relief.

Enter LADY VALERIUS, her face pale and stained with tears.

AURELIA
O, mother, you are come!
Your face, it is a scroll of tragedy.
Read it to me at once, hide not a word.
Is Flavius well? Is Cassian safe?
Tell me they live, and I can bear the rest.

LADY VALERIUS
The words you ask for cannot be pronounced.
To say ‘they live’ would be to tell a lie
Of half the truth. One lives, the other does not.

AURELIA
Which one? O, do not make me guess the name!
My heart is torn between the two I love.

LADY VALERIUS
Our cousin Cassian, witty, brave, and kind,
Has paid the price for our ancestral hate.
His breath is gone, his laughter is no more.
He fell today, struck down by Lucius.

AURELIA
No! Cassian dead? The sun of our small world
Extinguished? O, the sorrow of it all.
And Lucius, that proud villain, does he walk
Unharmed and smug, while our dear cousin lies
In silent earth? It is a monstrous thing!
And Flavius? Brother? Where was he in this?
Did he not stand and fight for Cassian’s life?

LADY VALERIUS
He did, and more. He fought for Cassian’s honor.
He stood above his body, and with rage
That love and grief had forged into a sword,
He met with Lucius.

AURELIA
And he bested him?
He made him pay? O, tell me that he did!

LADY VALERIUS
He did. The son of Corvin is no more.
Your brother’s sword has sent him to his grave.

AURELIA
Then he is hailed a hero! He is praised
For righting such a wrong! The Prefect must…

LADY VALERIUS
The Prefect, child, is a politician.
He balances the scales not with true weights
Of right and wrong, but with the heavy stones
Of power, and of fear of civil war.
For Cassian’s death, which Lucius did commit,
And Lucius’ death, which Flavius returned,
The sentence is not praise, but banishment.
Your brother is an exile from this day.

AURELIA
An exile! Cast away from home and kin?
Forced out to wander in the wilderness?
This is his thanks for being brave and true?
O, crooked world, where justice has no eyes
And virtue is a crime to be punished!
Where is he now? We must go to him, mother.
We must conceal him, help him, give him aid.

LADY VALERIUS
He fled the scene. I know not where he is.
To hide him here is to invite his death.
The Prefect’s word was clear as ringing steel.
If he is found at dawn, he is to die.
We are undone, Aurelia. We have lost
Our honor, half our blood, and now our hope.
The fine the Prefect levies on our house
Will bleed us dry and leave us pale as ghosts.

A noise is heard from the window. A soft tapping.

AURELIA
What sound is that? The watchman on his rounds?

LADY VALERIUS
No, it is softer. Go, child, bolt the door.
We are besieged by sorrow and by fear.
We trust no one tonight.

The tapping comes again, more urgent. A low voice calls.

FLAVIUS
(From outside)
Mother. Sister. It is I. Let me in.

AURELIA
It is his voice! It is my brother’s soul
Come back to haunt us from his living grave!

LADY VALERIUS
Unbar the window. Quickly, let him in
Before the prowling eyes of night can see.
My son! My son!

Aurelia unlatches a low window, and FLAVIUS, cloaked and grim, climbs into the room. He moves like a hunted animal.

FLAVIUS
Forgive my thief’s approach.
The doors are watched, I am sure of it.
I could not leave, not without one last look
Upon the faces that I hold most dear.

LADY VALERIUS
O, Flavius, foolish, brave, and wretched boy!
To risk your life to come to us tonight.
Your presence is a joy that tortures me,
For it must be so brief.

AURELIA
My dearest brother.
Your hands are cold. Your face is thin with care.
What have they done to you?

FLAVIUS
What have I done?
That is the question that will dog my steps
Through all the lonely roads I am to walk.
I killed a man, Aurelia. Do not think
It is a simple thing, a victor’s prize.
His face will be the shadow of my own
In every polished shield and every stream.
His dying gasp will whisper in the wind.

LADY VALERIUS
He killed our Cassian first! It was a debt
That you collected. He had forfeit life
When his sharp steel found our sweet cousin’s heart.
You must not bear the guilt of justice, son.

FLAVIUS
I saw the life go out of Cassian’s eyes.
A light just snuffed, a story left untold.
And in that moment, reason fled from me.
My soul was hollow, and revenge rushed in
To fill the space where brotherhood had been.
I did not think. My arm was not my own.
It was a part of some great, hateful machine
Built by our fathers, oiled with blood and tears.
And when he fell, when Lucius lay there dead,
The fire in me did not die, but turned
To ice. I felt no triumph, only loss.
A double loss. A void where two souls were.

AURELIA
Then you repent the deed?

FLAVIUS
I repent the world
That made the deed a thing that had to be.
I do not know. My thoughts are a mad storm.
But I must go. The moon is climbing high,
And with her light, my chances shrink and die.
Where shall I go? To what forgotten coast
Will this ill tide of fortune carry me?

LADY VALERIUS
I have some jewels sewn within my gown.
Here, take this purse. It is not much, but gold
Can buy a friend where friendship is for sale.
Go south, toward the coast. Take ship and sail
To any port where our name is unknown.
Rebuild your life. Forget the man you were.

FLAVIUS
Forget? I am the sum of what I’ve done.
I cannot amputate the past, dear mother.
I am a kinsman’s avenger, and a killer.
I am an exile. This is who I am.
But I will live. I swear it on my love
For both of you. I will endure this fate
And one day I will find a path back home.
Aurelia, sister, do not weep for me.
Your tears will make my journey’s bread more salt.

AURELIA
I cannot help it. To be parted thus,
Without a hope of when we’ll meet again.
Write to us, brother. Let us know you live.

FLAVIUS
I will. When it is safe, I’ll find a way.
And now, farewell. The word is like a tolling bell.
Farewell.

He embraces his sister, then his mother, holding her for a long moment.

LADY VALERIUS
Go now, my son. May all the gods that watch
Over the lost and lonely be your guide.
Be safe. Be strong. And live. You must just live.

Flavius moves back to the window, casts one last look at them, and is gone into the night.

AURELIA
He is gone. The house is empty now.
The world is empty.

LADY VALERIUS
No. We are still here.
A house is not just stone, a family
Not only men. We are the roots that hold,
Even when the tallest branch is torn away.
Corvin thinks us defeated, broken, poor.
He does not know the strength a woman finds
When all she loves is threatened by the dark.
We will endure. We will defy them all.
Come, child. We must be lions, not like lambs.
The night is long, but dawn will come at last.

[_Exeunt._]

ACT I, SCENE III

A great hall in the house of Corvin. Black drapes hang on the walls. In the center of the room, on a bier, lies the body of LUCIUS, covered by a sheet. LORD CORVIN stands staring at it. LADY CORVIN enters. Her grief is a sharp, burning thing.

LADY CORVIN
Still standing there? Like you could will his heart
To beat again by staring at the cloth
That covers his cold breast? He is gone, husband.
Our boy is gone, and you do nothing but
Gaze at his absence. Is this all your rage?

LORD CORVIN
My rage is not a thing for show, my dear.
It is not loud, like yours, a summer storm
That cracks the sky and quickly passes on.
Mine is a winter freeze. It builds in silence,
Making the very bedrock crack and split.
I am thinking. Planning.

LADY CORVIN
Planning what?
To pay the fine the Prefect stole from us
Through our dead son? To nod and acquiesce
To this vile mockery of all that’s right?
Flavius, his killer, takes a pleasant trip,
Perhaps to see the isles, and take the sun,
While our son’s sun is set forevermore.
This is the justice that your Prefect gives!

LORD CORVIN
The Prefect’s justice is a coward’s hedge,
A compromise to keep the merchants safe
And let the trade flow smoothly through the streets.
It has no heart, no soul, no honor in it.
And so I will not honor it.

LADY CORVIN
What will you do? Your words are strong, but words
Cannot bring Flavius back to face the axe.
He is already gone, or soon will be.
Fled like the coward that he is.

LORD CORVIN
He is a Valerius. He will run south.
They always do. They love the decadent,
Soft air of the coastal towns. He will seek passage
On some slow tub to a forgotten port.
But the world is not so large, my lady wife,
That a man with gold cannot find another.

LADY CORVIN
You mean to send men after him? To hunt him?

LORD CORVIN
Hunt him? Yes. That is a gentle word
For what I have in mind. I do not want him
Brought back in chains. I do not want a trial.
I want him dead. I want his mother’s heart
To feel the very break that has shattered mine.
I want him lying in a foreign ditch,
His flesh for crows, his name a passing curse
On some poor farmer’s lips who finds his bones.
Exile is not a punishment. It is
A destination. And I will ensure
He reaches his true final destination soon.

LADY CORVIN
Yes. Yes, this is the husband I once knew.
This is the lord of Corvin, not the statue
That stood here grieving. Who will do the deed?
They must be swift. And they must be unseen.
No link to us. The Prefect’s eyes are sharp.

LORD CORVIN
I have two men, brothers, from the northern hills.
They do not value life but for the coin
It brings them. They have served me well before
In delicate affairs. Their blades are sharp,
Their hearts are stones, their loyalty is bought
And paid for. They will find him on the road.
A robber’s work, a tragedy of travel.
So the official story will be told.
No one will ever know. But we will know.
We will know that our Lucius rests in peace,
His killer sent to keep him company
In the eternal dark.

LADY CORVIN
And the others?
His mother and his sister, left behind?
The fine will hurt them, yes, but they still breathe.
They still remain to carry on that name,
That hateful name of Valerius.

LORD CORVIN
One thing at a time. First, we cut the branch.
Then we shall see what happens to the tree.
When winter comes, a tree without its heir
Can be brought down by one determined storm.
Their spirit will be broken with their son.
And what remains, I’ll grind to dust myself.
Go, now. Prepare yourself. We must show the world
A face of sorrow, not of calculation.
We are the victims here. We must play the part,
Until our private justice has been done.

LADY CORVIN
I will. And every tear that I let fall
In public view will be a drop of poison
I save for them in the quiet of my heart.
May they know nothing else but misery.
May their line end in ashes and in shame.

[_Exit Lady Corvin._]

LORD CORVIN
(Alone, pulling back the sheet to look at his son’s face)
Sleep now, my son. Your father is awake.
The debt owed to you will be paid in full,
With interest that the devil himself would praise.
This is not justice. This is family.

He covers the face again and turns away as the stage grows dark.

SCENE II

A squalid tavern room near the city’s southern gate. The only light comes from a single, sputtering oil lamp that casts long, dancing shadows. CASSIAN, a man with the lean, patient look of a wolf, cleans his fingernails with the tip of a long knife. His brother, BRUTUS, a heavy, bull necked man, paces the small space impatiently. A STEWARD, cloaked and hooded, stands by the door.

STEWARD
The directions are simple. He travels south, towards the sea road. He must pass through the Dragon’s Tooth Gorge three days from now. There is no other route for a man in a hurry to leave the province.

BRUTUS
And he will be in a hurry. Exiles always are. They think speed will outrun their reputation. It never does.

CASSIAN
(Without looking up from his knife)
Is he alone?

STEWARD
He has one man with him. An old servant. Little more than a pack mule with memories. He should present no trouble.

BRUTUS
(Stops pacing and grins)
Good. I do not like complications. I like the weight of a coin in my hand and the satisfying sound of a job well done. When is the first payment?

STEWARD
Lord Corvin is not a man who haggles over price or withholds what is promised. Half is here.
(He places a heavy leather pouch on the table. It lands with a solid, metallic thud.)
The rest will be delivered to your usual contact upon confirmation of the deed.

Cassian stops his cleaning. He picks up the pouch, weighs it in his hand, and then looks at the Steward for the first time. His eyes are cold and grey.

CASSIAN
Confirmation. What sort of confirmation does the lord require? A sworn word? A token?

STEWARD
A token would be best. The boy wears a silver ring, his family crest. A wolf’s head. Bring that back. And bring back the news that no one will ever find the body. The gorge is deep. The river below it is swift and cold.

BRUTUS
A fall from a great height. Such a clumsy boy. A tragic accident on a treacherous road. We are experts in accidents.

STEWARD
Lord Corvin cares little for the story you invent. He cares only for the ending. Be certain it is the final one. There can be no mistakes. His grief is a fire that will consume anyone who fails him. Do you understand?

CASSIAN
We understand the weight of gold. And the weight of failure. We have never failed to balance our accounts. Tell your lord his son’s ghost can begin to rest. We will handle the boy.

STEWARD
Three days. The Dragon’s Tooth. Do not be late.
(The Steward pulls his hood lower and slips out of the room, closing the door softly behind him.)

Brutus immediately moves to the table and tries to snatch the pouch. Cassian’s hand darts out, pinning Brutus’s wrist to the wood.

BRUTUS
Brother! Let go. I only wish to see the color of it.

CASSIAN
You see with your hands, Brutus. And you think with your gut. We will see the color when the work is done. Not before. This is a lord’s payment, not a purse snatched in an alley. It comes with expectations.

BRUTUS
Expectations. Kill a boy and his dog. It is hardly the most difficult task we have faced. I could do it in my sleep.

CASSIAN
That is what worries me. You sleep too soundly. This is not some merchant on a dark street. This is the son of a noble house. And the man paying us is the most powerful lord in this province. If this goes wrong, there is no hole deep enough for us to hide in.

BRUTUS
It will not go wrong. I will break him. You can fetch the ring.

CASSIAN
(Releases his brother’s wrist)
No. We will do it together. As we always do. I will be the rock he trips on. You will be the fall that kills him. Now, get some rest. We ride before dawn.

Brutus grunts, rubbing his wrist. He picks up a clay jug of wine from the floor and takes a long drink.

BRUTUS
I still say it is a pity. All this fuss for one boy killing another. In the hills where we grew up, that was just called a Tuesday.

CASSIAN
That is why we left the hills, brother. Because here, their Tuesdays pay in gold.

Cassian wipes his knife clean and sheathes it. He blows out the lamp, plunging the room into absolute darkness.

SCENE III

The main hall of the Valerius estate. The room is noticeably barer than it should be. The space on a wall where a large tapestry once hung is now a pale rectangle. Niches that held silver statues are empty. LIVIA VALERIUS, Marcus’s mother, sits in a high backed chair, staring into a cold, empty hearth. She is wrapped in a dark shawl, a statue of grief. Her daughter, HELENA, a young woman with a sharp, intelligent face, stands by the window, looking out into the rain slicked courtyard.

HELENA
The last cart has gone. The fine is paid. The Prefect’s men were… thorough. They took the silver Apollo. And the tapestries from the western wing.

LIVIA
(Her voice is a dry whisper)
Let them take it. What is silver and thread to me now? They have already taken my son. The rest is just stone and dust.

HELENA
He is not taken, Mother. He is exiled. He lives. He breathes. He will find a new life.

LIVIA
A new life? What life is that? A wanderer, a fugitive, with the brand of killer upon him. Every man’s hand turned against him. Every night spent looking over his shoulder. That is not a life, Helena. It is a slow, walking death. I would rather he had fallen beside Lucius Corvin on that field. At least then I could bury him. I could mourn him properly.

HELENA
Do not say that. Do not even think it. While he lives, there is hope. We must be strong for him.

LIVIA
Strong? Look at me. This house is a tomb. Your father’s legacy, sold off to pay for a moment of boyish rage. And for what? So that Lord Corvin can sit in his grand hall and count his gold and his righteousness, while my Marcus sleeps in ditches and begs for bread.

HELENA
He will not beg. We gave him all we could. And Cato is with him. He will watch over him. Marcus is smart. He is a survivor.

LIVIA
He is a boy! He was always too quick to anger, too proud. Just like his father. I warned him. I told him the Corvin boy had a viper’s tongue. I told him to stay away. But he would not listen. Pride. It is the Valerius curse. It has cost us everything.

Helena turns from the window, her expression hardening.

HELENA
No. Not everything. They have not taken this house. They have not taken our name. And they have not taken us. You are Livia Valerius. I am your daughter. We are not broken. We are bent. We will endure this.

LIVIA
You sound so certain. You did not see the look in Lady Corvin’s eyes at the judgment. It was not grief I saw there. It was triumph. A cold, absolute victory. She will not rest until we are utterly destroyed. This is only the beginning.

HELENA
Then let her try. Let them all try. They think because the men of this house are gone, that we are defenseless. They are wrong. A wolf is still a wolf, even when the pack is scattered. Father taught me how to manage the estates. He taught me numbers and letters. Things they do not think a woman needs to know. I will use them. I will rebuild what we have lost. Brick by brick. Coin by coin.

LIVIA
You cannot fight a man like Lord Corvin. He is a mountain. We are but pebbles at his feet.

HELENA
A mountain can be worn down by a patient stream. Or it can be shattered by a single crack in its foundation. Everyone has a weakness, Mother. Even a lord. We will survive. And one day, Marcus will return. I swear it. I swear it on our name.

Livia looks at her daughter, a flicker of something other than despair in her eyes for the first time. She reaches out a trembling hand.

LIVIA
My child. You have your father’s fire. Be careful it does not consume you as it did him.

HELENA
(Taking her mother’s hand)
It will not consume me. It will keep us warm until the sun decides to shine on this house again. Now come. Let me help you to your chambers. We must rest. We have much work to do tomorrow.

Helena helps her mother to her feet, and they slowly exit the vast, empty room, two lonely figures against the encroaching twilight.

SCENE IV

A clearing in a forest, two days’ journey south of the city. A small fire crackles, pushing back the deep darkness of the woods. MARCUS VALERIUS sits staring into the flames. He is younger than his reputation suggests, his face smudged with dirt and etched with worry. CATO, his family’s old servant, a man with a face like a weathered map, stirs a small pot hanging over the fire.

CATO
You must eat, my lord. You have had nothing but water and regret since we left the city gates. One will not sustain you, and the other will poison you.

MARCUS
I am not hungry. Every time I close my eyes, I see him. Lucius. I see him falling. I see the surprise in his eyes. He was… he was my friend, once. Before our fathers’ quarrel became our own.

CATO
Friendship is a fragile thing in these times, my lord. It breaks easier than a clay pot. What happened was a tragedy born of pride. His and yours. But it is done. You cannot carry the dead with you on this road. They are too heavy a burden.

MARCUS
It is not his ghost I carry. It is the thought of my mother. And my sister. I left them to face the vultures alone. To sell our family’s history to pay for my crime. They will hate me. They must.

CATO
Hate you? Never. They love you. Every hard choice they make, every humiliation they endure, they do it so that you may live. Your life is their hope. Do not dishonor their sacrifice by wasting away in sorrow. You must live. And live well. That is the only payment you can offer them now.

MARCUS
Live well? As what? A mercenary? A beggar? My name is worthless now. Worse than worthless. It is a danger to anyone who hears it.

CATO
A name is what you make it. Here, beyond the reach of the Prefect and the province, you are not Marcus Valerius, killer of Lucius Corvin. You are just a young man with a strong arm and a quick mind. You can be a farmer. A smith. A sailor. You can be anything you choose to be. The world is larger than one city and its petty hatreds.

Marcus picks up a stick and prods the fire, sending a shower of sparks into the night air.

MARCUS
Did you see Lord Corvin’s face? When the sentence was read? It was like a mask carved from ice. He accepted the Prefect’s judgment, but his eyes… his eyes passed a different sentence. A final one. I do not think this is over, Cato. I do not think exile is enough for him.

CATO
The law is the law. Even a great lord must abide by it.

MARCUS
He makes his own laws. My father always said so. He said Lord Corvin believes justice is a weapon to be wielded, not a scale to be balanced. I feel a chill, Cato, that has nothing to do with the night air. I feel as if I am being hunted.

CATO
(Places a hand on the hilt of the short sword at his belt)
Then we will be watchful. I may be old, but my eyes are still sharp. And this old dog still has a few teeth left. No one will harm you while I draw breath. Now, eat. We have a long walk tomorrow. The gorge is steep, and you will need your strength.

Cato hands Marcus a wooden bowl filled with a thin stew. Marcus takes it, his expression grim. He looks up at the vast, starless sky.

MARCUS
The gorge. Yes. I long for the sea. I feel if I can just reach the sea, I can wash this all away. The blood. The guilt. The fear. I can sail away and never look back.

CATO
The sea is a good place to forget. And a good place to begin again. Eat, my lord Marcus. Tomorrow we walk towards the sea.

Marcus nods and begins to eat, his movements slow and mechanical. The fire burns lower, and the immense, silent forest seems to press in around them.

SCENE V

The office of the PREFECT QUINTUS. It is an orderly, functional room, filled with scrolls and maps. QUINTUS, a man in his fifties with a weary but intelligent face, stands before a large map of the province. His AIDE, a young, earnest man named Decius, stands beside him.

QUINTUS
He took the south road. Towards Tarentum. It is the predictable choice. He wants a port. He wants to disappear.

DECIUS
Shall I send a patrol to monitor his progress, sir? To ensure he honors the terms of his exile?

QUINTUS
No. The boy is not my concern. The terms are met. He has left the city’s jurisdiction. Let him go. It is the man who remains behind who interests me. Lord Corvin.

DECIUS
My lord? He accepted your judgment. The fine has been paid by the Valerius family. The matter is concluded.

QUINTUS
Concluded? Decius, you are young. You believe that when a document is stamped and filed, the story ends. But with men like Corvin, the official story is often just the cover of a much darker book. I have known him for twenty years. I have never known him to be satisfied with a legal judgment when a personal slight is involved. Especially one paid in blood.

DECIUS
But he gave no sign. He was stoic. Composed.

QUINTUS
Exactly. That is what troubles me. A man whose only son has been cut down should be raging. He should be demanding a head. He should be appealing my decision, using his influence, making my life a living hell. This quiet, cold acceptance… it is not in his nature. It is the calm surface of a deep and dangerous water.

DECIUS
What do you suspect he will do?

QUINTUS
What he always does. He will seek his own justice. The kind that does not require witnesses or a judge. The boy, Marcus Valerius, is a dead man walking. Corvin will not let him reach the sea. He will have him killed on the road and make it look like the work of bandits. It is his signature. Clean, deniable, and absolute.

DECIUS
Can we act on such a suspicion? We have no proof.

QUINTUS
No. We cannot. And I cannot spare the men to be the boy’s bodyguard across the entire province. My duty is to this city, not to one exiled killer. But I will not have a lord, any lord, thinking he can conduct private wars under my watch. It is a disease that rots a city from within.

DECIUS
So what is to be done?

QUINTUS
We watch. We listen. I want a man near Corvin’s estate. Not close enough to be noticed, just close enough to report who comes and goes. I am particularly interested in any visitors from the northern hills. He favors a certain type of tool for this kind of work. And I want you to send a quiet message to the magistrate in Tarentum. Inform him that should a young nobleman, formerly of this city, meet with a tragic accident on the road, he should investigate the matter with extreme prejudice and send his findings directly to me.

DECIUS
Yes, Prefect.

QUINTUS
Corvin thinks he is clever. He thinks his grief gives him a license to act as a god, dispensing life and death. But even gods can leave footprints. And it is my job to find them. Now go. I want to know the moment Corvin’s dogs leave their kennel.

Decius bows and exits. Quintus turns back to the map, tracing the southern road with his finger.

QUINTUS
Run, little wolf. Run as fast as you can. A storm is coming for you. And I wonder if you will be swift enough to outrun it.

He sighs, a deep, tired sound. He is a man holding back the tide with his bare hands. The stage fades to black.

ACT II

SCENE I

The study of LORD CORVIN. The room is dark, furnished with heavy, imposing pieces of wood and leather. The only light comes from a single oil lamp on a large desk, casting long, dancing shadows. Bookshelves line the walls, but they are neat, untouched. A large, stern bust of a young man, CORVIN’S deceased son, sits on a marble pedestal in the corner, almost a shrine. CORVIN stands with his back to the door, looking out a tall, narrow window at the darkening hills. He is a man carved from granite, still and silent. After a long moment, a servant, LUCIUS, enters. He is older, with a nervous energy that contrasts sharply with Corvin’s stillness. He hesitates at the door.

LUCIUS
My lord. You have not eaten.

CORVIN
(Without turning)
I have no appetite.

LUCIUS
Forgive me, my lord. But to starve the body is to starve the spirit. It will not aid your grief.

CORVIN
My grief needs no aid. It is self sufficient. It is all I have left of him. Leave the tray.

Lucius quietly places a tray with bread and a cup of wine on a small table. He does not leave.

LUCIUS
There is… another matter, my lord. The men you were expecting. They have arrived. They are waiting in the stables, as instructed.

Corvin turns slowly from the window. His face is a mask of control, but his eyes burn with a cold fire.

CORVIN
And were they discreet?

LUCIUS
As shadows, my lord. They came over the northern ridge after sundown. No one in the city would have seen them.

CORVIN
Good. Their leader. What is his name?

LUCIUS
He calls himself Drusus. A hard man. He has the look of a wolf that has chewed off its own leg to escape a trap. He does not speak much.

CORVIN
Men who talk are not worth what they are paid. I do not pay for conversation. Did you give him the description?

LUCIUS
Everything you provided. The route the boy is taking. The clothes he wears. The name of the servant with him. I also gave him the first payment. He seemed… satisfied.

CORVIN
He will be more satisfied when the work is done.

Corvin walks to his desk and picks up a small, exquisitely carved wooden horse, turning it over and over in his hands. It was clearly a child’s toy.

LUCIUS
My lord… if I may be so bold. Is this truly the only path? The Prefect has passed sentence. The boy is exiled. He will never trouble this city or your house again. To do this… it invites scrutiny. It invites risk. Quintus is not a fool.

Corvin places the horse down with deliberate care. He looks at Lucius, and for a moment, the mask cracks. A flicker of raw pain is visible.

CORVIN
Quintus is a bureaucrat. He is a clerk who wears a sword. He thinks justice is a word written on a scroll. He thinks a life for a life can be balanced by sending one boy to a different city. My son is not in a different city, Lucius. He is in the cold earth. He is dust. And the one who put him there breathes the air, feels the sun on his face, and dreams of a future.

LUCIUS
But vengeance…

CORVIN
Do not use that word. Vengeance is a hot, messy thing. It is for lesser men. This is not vengeance. This is a correction. An error has been made in the order of things. A life was taken and the scales were not balanced. I am simply restoring the balance. The law failed to do it, so I must.

LUCIUS
And the Prefect? If he suspects?

CORVIN
Let him suspect. Quintus is a dog who is proud of his leash. He will only go as far as his precious laws allow him. He will sniff and he will circle, but he needs proof. Drusus and his men will leave no proof. Bandits are a regrettable feature of our provincial roads. The magistrate in Tarentum will write a brief report, Quintus will file it, and the world will continue to turn. The only difference will be that Marcus Valerius will have reached his final destination somewhat earlier than planned.

He turns to the bust of his son. The shadows play across its marble face, giving it a sorrowful expression.

CORVIN
They say a father should not outlive his son. They are wrong. A father must outlive his son, if only to make sure his accounts are settled. See to it that Drusus and his men have what they need. Wine, food, fresh horses. They are to leave an hour before dawn. I want it done before the boy reaches the foothills. I want it done quickly.

LUCIUS
Yes, my lord.

Lucius bows and backs out of the room, leaving Corvin alone with the shadows and the ghost of his son. Corvin walks to the bust and places a hand on its cold cheek.

CORVIN
Sleep now, my son. It is almost over. Justice is coming.

The lamp flickers, and the stage fades.

SCENE II

A squalid common room of a roadside inn. The air is thick with the smell of stale beer and woodsmoke. Rain streaks down a single grimy window. At a rough hewn table in the corner, MARCUS VALERIUS picks at a piece of hard bread. He is young, with the ghost of aristocratic arrogance still clinging to him, but it is frayed by fear and exhaustion. His fine clothes are stained with mud. Opposite him, GAIUS, a man in his fifties with the weathered face of a loyal family servant and former soldier, sharpens a short sword with a whetstone. The sound is a rhythmic, metallic scrape that grates on the nerves.

MARCUS
Must you do that? The sound is setting my teeth on edge.

GAIUS
A dull blade is a heavy blade, young master. And a heavy blade is a slow blade. Out here, slow is the same as dead.

MARCUS
We are not going to be attacked. We are two days from the city. No one has followed us.

GAIUS
You do not know that. You see the road ahead. I watch the road behind. Lord Corvin is not a man to forget. Or to forgive.

MARCUS
The Prefect exiled me. It is the law. Corvin is a lord, not a king. He must abide by it.

Gaius stops sharpening the sword and looks at Marcus. His expression is one of pity.

GAIUS
The law, master Marcus, is a high wall around a city. It protects those inside. We are not inside anymore. Out here, there is only the law of the wolf and the deer. And we are looking very much like deer right now.

MARCUS
I am not a deer. I faced his son. I did not run. It was a fair fight. He challenged me.

GAIUS
And now he is dead and you are here. Fairness does not come into it. A father has lost his only heir. That is a debt he will feel must be paid. He will not care how the debt was incurred.

Marcus pushes his plate away, his appetite gone.

MARCUS
What are we to do, then? Sleep in ditches? Never stop moving? We cannot live like that. The money my mother gave us will not last a month.

GAIUS
We will be careful. We will stay off the main roads where we can. We will reach Tarentum. Once there, in a new city, under a new magistrate, we will be safer. We can send a message to your uncle in Gaul. He will help. But until then, we must be vigilant.

A large, slovenly INNKEEPER approaches their table, wiping his hands on a filthy apron.

INNKEEPER
Will there be anything else for you? Or are you done?

GAIUS
We will take the room for the night.

The Innkeeper eyes Marcus’s travel-stained but well-made tunic.

INNKEEPER
That will be three silver pieces. In advance. And another for the horse.

GAIUS
Three? That is robbery. The room is little more than a closet with a leaking roof.

INNKEEPER
It is my price. Take it or sleep in the rain with the frogs. Makes no difference to me.

Gaius looks at Marcus, who gives a slight, defeated nod. Gaius carefully counts out the coins from a small pouch and slaps them onto the table. The Innkeeper scoops them up greedily.

INNKEEPER
The room is at the top of the stairs. And see that you cause no trouble. I do not want the watch asking questions at my door.

He lumbers away.

MARCUS
He knows who I am.

GAIUS
He knows you are a nobleman in disgrace. That is a kind of carrion that attracts flies. He does not care why. He only smells your misfortune and wants to profit from it.

MARCUS
This is to be my life now? Scorn from innkeepers and fear in every shadow? I should have stayed. I should have faced their judgment.

GAIUS
And you would be in a cell now, waiting for an executioner, not in an inn, waiting for dawn. There is a great difference, young master. One of them is called hope. Now, try to eat. You will need your strength.

Gaius returns to his whetstone. The scraping sound fills the room again. Marcus picks up the bread, but he does not eat. He stares out the window at the rain, at the dark road that stretches on forever.

MARCUS
I do not feel like a wolf. I feel like the bait in a trap.

GAIUS
Then we must be clever enough to steal the bait and not spring the trap. Rest. I will keep watch.

The stage fades, the scraping sound of the whetstone the last thing to be heard.

SCENE III

Prefect Quintus’s office. It is late. The map of the province is still on the table. Quintus sits behind his desk, reading a scroll by lamplight. He looks tired, but his eyes are alert. The door opens and DECIUS enters. He is cloaked, and rain glistens on his shoulders.

DECIUS
Prefect.

QUINTUS
(Without looking up)
Report.

DECIUS
Your suspicions were well founded. Visitors arrived at Corvin’s estate an hour after sunset.

QUINTUS
Visitors? Or employees?

DECIUS
The latter, I would say. Three of them. They did not use the main gate. They came on foot over the northern hills, where the estate wall is lowest. They were dressed as hunters, but the man I had watching said they moved like soldiers. Or worse.

QUINTUS
Did he get a good look at them?

DECIUS
At their leader. A man with a scarred face and cold eyes. He matched the description of a mercenary named Drusus. He is known in the northern territories. A man who undertakes sensitive work for wealthy patrons. He specializes in making things look like accidents.

Quintus finally puts down his scroll and looks at Decius. A grim, hard line has settled around his mouth.

QUINTUS
His signature. Just as I said. Corvin does not even bother to be creative. He merely dips into his purse and buys the same tools he has always used. It is arrogance.

DECIUS
They met with Corvin’s steward, Lucius. They were taken to the stables, not the main house. They left again an hour before dawn, this time on horseback, heading south. They are on the road.

QUINTUS
They will move faster than the boy. They will get ahead of him and wait for him in a quiet place. The Oakhaven Pass, most likely. It is narrow and dense with trees. Perfect for an ambush.

DECIUS
What are your orders, Prefect? We could send a patrol. Intercept them on the road. We do not have proof of intent, but we could detain them on a weapons charge, delay them.

QUINTUS
No. A patrol would be my hand tipping his. Corvin would simply recall them and find another way. A slower poison, perhaps. And he would know that I am watching him. He would become more careful, more subtle. I do not want him to be subtle. I want him to be caught.

DECIUS
But the boy, Marcus Valerius. He will be killed.

Quintus stands and walks to the map, placing a finger on the Oakhaven Pass.

QUINTUS
That is a distinct possibility. His life is not my primary concern. The rule of law in this city is. Corvin has challenged it. He has decided his private grief outweighs public order. He is setting himself up as a king in my jurisdiction. That cannot be allowed to stand. If I let this pass, every petty lord with a grievance will start hiring assassins to settle his disputes. The roads will become private battlefields.

DECIUS
So we do nothing? We let the boy die to prove a point?

QUINTUS
I did not say we will do nothing. I said we will not send a patrol. You do not stop a snake by shouting at it. You wait for it to strike, and then you pin its head to the ground. Is Cassian back from his assignment in the west?

DECIUS
He returned this afternoon. He is quartered with the scouts.

QUINTUS
Send for him. Tell him to come here immediately. And tell him to come by the garden entrance. I do not want him seen.

DECIUS
Cassian? Prefect, he is not a soldier. He is a… shadow.

QUINTUS
That is precisely what I require. Drusus is Corvin’s tool. Cassian will be mine. Corvin wants to make it look like the work of bandits. He wants it to be deniable. The key to unraveling a deniable act is a witness no one knows is there. Drusus will be looking for patrols and travelers. He will not be looking for a single man in the trees, a man who knows how to move without being seen, a man whose only task is to watch and remember.

DECIUS
You want a witness to the boy’s murder.

QUINTUS
I want a witness to an act of conspiracy and murder ordered by a nobleman of this city. With that testimony, I can bring Corvin down. Not with suspicion. Not with rumor. But with the cold, hard weight of the law he so disdains. Go. Bring me Cassian.

Decius hesitates for a second, a troubled look on his face. He then nods sharply.

DECIUS
Yes, Prefect.

He exits. Quintus remains at the map, his finger still pressing down on the spot marking the Oakhaven Pass.

QUINTUS
You set your pieces on the board, Corvin. Now I will set mine. Let us see whose trap is stronger.

The stage fades to black.

SCENE IV

A narrow road through a dense forest. The Oakhaven Pass. Morning light struggles to pierce the thick canopy of leaves, dappling the ground in shifting patterns of light and shadow. The air is still and heavy with the scent of damp earth. MARCUS and GAIUS walk beside their lone horse. Gaius has his hand resting on the hilt of his sword. His eyes dart back and forth, scanning the trees.

MARCUS
Why are we walking? The horse is not even tired. We are making no time at all.

GAIUS
This place… I do not like it. It is too quiet. No birds. No squirrels. The forest is holding its breath.

MARCUS
You are letting your fears get the better of you, old man. It is just a forest.

GAIUS
I have been a soldier for thirty years, master. Fear is a friend. It is a signal. And my friend is screaming at me right now. The road narrows up ahead. A perfect place to…

He is cut off as an arrow thuds into a tree trunk just inches from his head. Before they can react, three men step out onto the road ahead of them. The leader is DRUSUS, his scarred face devoid of expression. The other two are brutal looking men armed with short swords and axes. From behind, a fourth man appears, cutting off their retreat. They are trapped.

DRUSUS
Marcus Valerius.

His voice is flat, a statement of fact, not a question.

MARCUS
Who are you? What do you want?

DRUSUS
We are merchants. And we have come to collect a debt owed to Lord Corvin.

GAIUS
(Drawing his sword)
Get behind me, master.

Drusus gives a small, almost imperceptible nod. The two men in front of him advance slowly. The man behind does the same.

GAIUS
(To Marcus, in a low voice)
When I move, you run. Do not look back. Run into the woods and do not stop. Do you understand?

MARCUS
I will not leave you.

GAIUS
You will do as I say! Your life is the one that matters. Mine is already spent. Now!

With a roar, Gaius charges forward, not at the two men, but straight at Drusus. It is a brave, suicidal move, meant to create a distraction. The two thugs are surprised and hesitate. Drusus, however, is not. He sidesteps Gaius’s wild swing with practiced ease and brings the pommel of his sword down hard on the back of Gaius’s neck. The old servant crumples to the ground, unconscious or dead.

MARCUS
Gaius!

He draws his own sword, the one used in the duel. It feels impossibly heavy in his hand. He is trembling, not with the controlled adrenaline of a formal duel, but with raw terror.

DRUSUS
Your servant was a fool. But a loyal one. Do not make his sacrifice meaningless. Throw down your sword. It will be quicker.

The four men begin to circle Marcus, their weapons held ready. Marcus’s eyes dart between them. There is no escape. This is not a duel. It is an execution.

MARCUS
Corvin sent you.

DRUSUS
Lord Corvin is a grieving father. We are simply concerned travelers who came across a terrible bandit attack. A tragedy.

One of the thugs lunges at Marcus. Driven by panic, Marcus parries the blow clumsily, his blade scraping against the axe head. He stumbles back. This is not the clean, elegant swordplay of the training yard. It is a frantic, desperate struggle for one more second of life. He knows he cannot win. But the thought of Gaius’s sacrifice ignites something in him. A spark of defiance. He will not die begging.

He lets out a yell, a sound somewhere between a battle cry and a scream of despair, and rushes the nearest man. He ducks under a wild sword swing and thrusts his own blade forward. The point catches the man in the thigh. The thug cries out in pain and surprise.

It is a small victory, but it costs Marcus dearly. As he is focused on his target, Drusus steps in from the side, his movements fluid and deadly. He does not go for a killing blow. He kicks the back of Marcus’s knee, buckling his leg. As Marcus falls, Drusus slams a mailed fist into the side of his head.

The world explodes in a flash of white, then darkness. Marcus collapses on the forest floor, his sword clattering on the stones beside him.

DRUSUS
(To the man Marcus wounded)
Get up. It is a scratch. Bind it.
(To the others)
Finish him. Make it look like a robbery. Take the purse and the horse.

One of the thugs raises his axe over the unmoving form of Marcus Valerius. He grins. The axe begins its descent.

From deep within the forest, unseen by anyone, a single figure, CASSIAN, watches from the cover of a thicket. He makes no move to intervene. He simply watches, his face as impassive as stone. He is a ghost, a memory, a witness. And that is all he is meant to be.

The stage goes to black.

ACT I
SCENE II

LIGHTS UP.

The scene is frozen for a heartbeat. The THUG with the axe, his face a mask of cruel joy, the weapon at the peak of its arc. The forest is silent, holding its breath.

A sharp, whistling sound, followed by a wet thud.

The Thug’s triumphant grin vanishes, replaced by a look of profound surprise. He looks down. An arrow, fletched with dark grey feathers, protrudes from his throat. He makes a gurgling sound. The axe falls from his nerveless fingers, clattering uselessly on the stone beside Marcus’s head. The Thug pitches forward, dead before he hits the ground.

Drusus and the other two men spin around, weapons raised, searching the shadows.

DRUSUS
Ambush! To the trees!

The second THUG, the one with the sword, lets out a roar and charges blindly towards the thicket from which the arrow came. He crashes through the undergrowth. There is a rustle of leaves, a swift, almost silent movement, and then the sound of a choked cry. The Thug stumbles back out of the thicket, his sword arm hanging at a bizarre angle. He has a knife buried deep in his collarbone. He collapses.

CASSIAN steps out of the shadows. He is not a large man, but he moves with a chilling economy of motion. He holds a short, curved blade in one hand and a bow in the other. His face is calm, his eyes missing nothing.

DRUSUS
(To the wounded thug)
Stay back. Keep watch on the boy.

The wounded THUG, his leg crudely bound, limps backward, keeping his axe between himself and the newcomer, his eyes wide with fear.

Drusus takes a step forward, his sword held in a classic duelist's stance, but his feet are planted with the solid certainty of a brawler. He assesses Cassian.

DRUSUS
A brave rabbit to leap into the wolf’s den. Who pays you?

CASSIAN
I am not here for coin.

DRUSUS
Then you are a fool. Only fools and rich men act without payment. You do not look rich.

CASSIAN
And you look like a man who has outlived his luck.

Drusus does not reply with words. He lunges. The attack is blindingly fast, a thrust aimed straight for Cassian’s heart. Cassian does not parry. He pivots, letting the blade slide past his ribs, and brings his bow up in a hard, upward swing, cracking it against Drusus’s wrist. Drusus grunts in pain, his grip faltering for an instant. It is all the opening Cassian needs. He drops the bow and flows forward, his knife a blur.

Drusus is a professional. He recovers, backpedaling, his own sword creating a wall of steel. The blades meet with a sharp clang, not the ringing song of a duel, but the percussive, ugly sound of a fight to the death. They break apart, circling each other.

DRUSUS
You fight like a gutter rat from the capital. No honor. No form.

CASSIAN
Honor is a luxury for men who do not have to clean their own kills.

The wounded THUG, seeing Drusus occupied, decides to be a hero. He raises his axe and hobbles towards Cassian’s exposed back.

CASSIAN
(Without looking back)
A word of advice.

He sidesteps another of Drusus’s lunges, the movement fluid and precise.

CASSIAN
When a dog is wounded, it should lie still.

He spins, his leg lashing out in a low kick that connects squarely with the Thug’s injured thigh. The man screams and goes down, clutching his leg. Cassian does not even pause. He turns back to Drusus, who has used the brief distraction to charge.

Drusus leads with a powerful slash. Cassian ducks under it, so close his hair is nearly parted by the blade. As he comes up, he slams the pommel of his dagger into Drusus’s chin. The man’s head snaps back, teeth clicking together. Cassian follows through, his blade tracing a quick, shallow line across Drusus’s sword arm. Drusus snarls, more in frustration than pain, and shoves Cassian back with his armored shoulder.

DRUSUS
Enough of this.

He whistles sharply. A signal. He is not planning to win this fight. He is planning to survive it. He feints high, then kicks a spray of dirt and leaves into Cassian’s face.

Cassian blinks, momentarily blinded. Drusus does not press the attack. He turns and runs, grabbing his wounded man by the collar and hauling him into the forest.

DRUSUS
(His voice echoing back)
Corvin will hear of this! You have made a powerful enemy, ghost!

Silence descends. Cassian stands perfectly still for a moment, listening to the sounds of their retreat. He does not give chase. He slowly lowers his knife, his chest rising and falling in the first visible sign of exertion. He retrieves his bow.

He walks over to the Thug he kicked, who is now trying to crawl away. Cassian places a foot on his back, stopping him. The man freezes, trembling.

CASSIAN
Tell me something.

The THUG whimpers.

CASSIAN
Who else knows you were here?

THUG
No one. It was just us. Lord Corvin’s orders. Secret. Please. I have a family.

CASSIAN
They all do.

He moves with swift, dispassionate finality. The Thug gives a single, sharp sigh.

The clearing is now still, littered with the dead. Cassian stands among them, a spectre in the dappled light. He walks to the body of the archer he killed first and pulls his arrow free with a practiced tug, wiping the blood on the dead man’s tunic.

He then turns his attention to the living. He kneels beside Gaius first. He places two fingers on the old man’s neck, feeling for a pulse. He finds one. It is faint, thready, but it is there. For the first time, a flicker of something unreadable crosses Cassian’s face. It is not pity, not relief. It is something closer to calculation.

He moves to Marcus. The young nobleman is beginning to stir, groaning. A dark bruise is already forming on the side of his head where Drusus struck him. Cassian watches him, his expression impassive again.

Marcus’s eyes flutter open. The world swims into a nauseating blur of green leaves and grey sky. His head throbs with a blinding, relentless rhythm.

MARCUS
Gaius…

His voice is a weak croak. He pushes himself up onto one elbow, his body screaming in protest. His eyes slowly focus on the scene around him. The dead men. The blood. And the stranger standing over him, holding a bow and a bloody knife.

MARCUS
Who…

He scrambles backward, his hand fumbling for his sword. It is not there.

CASSIAN
It is over. They are gone.

MARCUS
Gone? Who are you? Did you… did you do this?

Cassian gestures with his knife to the bodies.

CASSIAN
They were going to kill you. I persuaded them otherwise.

Marcus’s eyes dart around, finally landing on the still form of his servant. Panic cuts through the pain in his head.

MARCUS
Gaius!

He crawls over to the old man’s side, his movements clumsy and frantic. He puts a trembling hand on Gaius’s chest.

MARCUS
Is he… is he dead?

CASSIAN
He breathes. For now. The blow to his neck was meant to kill. The man was skilled, but perhaps too confident. Or your servant has a thicker skull than he looks.

Marcus looks from Gaius’s pale face to Cassian’s hard one. Suspicion and a dawning, terrifying understanding war within him.

MARCUS
You were watching. You were here the whole time.

CASSIAN
The forest has many eyes.

MARCUS
Why? Why did you wait? Gaius… he could have…

His voice breaks.

CASSIAN
I act when the moment is right. Not before. Four armed men are not a threat to be taken lightly. Waiting thinned their ranks for me.

MARCUS
Thinned their ranks? He is a man! My friend! Not a number in your equation!

CASSIAN
Sentiment is a luxury. One you can no longer afford. Get up.

Marcus glares at him, a fire of indignation burning through his fear and pain.

MARCUS
I am not going anywhere with you.

CASSIAN
You have two choices. You can stay here and wait for their friends to return. And they will return. Or you can come with me and have a chance of seeing sunrise. The choice is yours. But make it quickly. Your friend is bleeding inside his skull. The longer he lies here, the more certain his death becomes.

The cold, brutal logic of Cassian’s words extinguishes Marcus’s anger, leaving only a grim residue of despair. He looks down at Gaius, then at the dead men, then back at the stranger.

MARCUS
Where would we go?

CASSIAN
Away.

He offers no more. He walks over to Marcus and crouches down, his gaze clinical.

CASSIAN
Hold still.

Before Marcus can protest, Cassian takes a strip of cloth torn from a dead man’s tunic and begins to clean the gash on Marcus’s head. His touch is rough, efficient, entirely without sympathy.

MARCUS
(Wincing)
Corvin sent them. He said so.

CASSIAN
Of course he did. You killed his only son. Did you expect a fruit basket?

MARCUS
It was a duel. It was honorable.

CASSIAN
His son is dead. Your honor means nothing to him now. All he has left is vengeance. And he is a man of considerable resources. The men he sent today were a message. The next ones will be an army.

Marcus’s world, a world of rules, honor, and noble obligation, feels like a distant dream. This was the reality. Blood, dirt, and a cold-eyed stranger telling him truths he did not want to hear.

MARCUS
What do you want from me? Why help me? No one does anything for nothing.

Cassian finishes tying the crude bandage around Marcus's head. He pulls the knot tight, making Marcus hiss in pain.

CASSIAN
Let us just say that Lord Corvin and I have… conflicting interests. Your continued existence is a great inconvenience to him. Therefore, it is a great convenience to me.

He stands up, his gaze sweeping the clearing.

CASSIAN
We need to move. Now. We cannot take the road.

MARCUS
What about Gaius? We cannot just leave him. And I cannot carry him. Not far.

CASSIAN
We will not leave him.

Cassian walks to Marcus’s horse, which stands nervously at the edge of the clearing, nostrils flared at the scent of blood. He soothes the animal with a low murmur and a steady hand, then begins unbuckling the saddle.

MARCUS
What are you doing?

CASSIAN
Making a sled. We will need your cloak. And his.

He works quickly, his hands sure and practiced. He strips the branches from two long, sturdy saplings, lashing them together with leather straps from the horse’s tack. He lays the two cloaks across the frame, creating a crude but functional travois. It is the work of a man who has done this before.

MARCUS
You… you have seen this kind of thing before. This butchery.

CASSIAN
(Without looking up from his work)
I have seen worse.

He says it with such flat finality that Marcus has no doubt it is true. Together, they gently lift Gaius onto the makeshift sled. The old man is limp, a dead weight. His breathing is shallow.

CASSIAN
The horse will pull him. You will lead the horse. And you will keep up. Do you understand?

Marcus nods, his throat too tight to speak.

CASSIAN
Take your sword.

He nods towards the blade lying on the ground. Marcus retrieves it. The familiar weight of the hilt is no comfort. It feels alien, an artifact from another life.

CASSIAN
Forget everything you learned in the training yard. Forget stances and parries. When you fight to live, there is only one rule.

MARCUS
What is it?

CASSIAN
The other man falls. That is all.

He takes a last look around the clearing, his eyes lingering for a moment on the dead. He is memorizing their faces, their equipment. He turns and melts into the trees.

CASSIAN
Come. Darkness is our only friend tonight.

Marcus takes the reins of his horse. The animal resists for a moment, then, with a gentle tug, follows. The travois scrapes softly over the leaves and dirt, carrying the dying servant away from the site of his master’s execution. Marcus follows Cassian’s shadowy form deeper and deeper into a forest that no longer looks like a peaceful stretch of nature, but a vast and threatening maze.

The sun begins to set, casting long, bloody-looking shadows through the trees. The journey is a nightmare. Marcus’s head pounds, every step an agony. Thorns and branches tear at his fine clothes. His soft leather boots, made for riding and walking on city cobblestones, are no match for the unforgiving terrain. Several times he stumbles, his legs threatening to give out.

The first time he falls, Cassian stops and looks back, his form a dark silhouette against the fading light.

CASSIAN
Get up.

MARCUS
I need… just a moment.

CASSIAN
The wolves will give you a moment. To tear out your throat. Lord Corvin’s men will give you a moment. To put a bolt in your back. I will not. Get up.

Anger, shame, and sheer exhaustion drive Marcus back to his feet. He grips the reins tighter and forces himself onward. He does not know how long they walk. Hours bleed into one another. The forest becomes a suffocating presence, the night absolute. The only sounds are their own ragged breaths, the horse’s nervous snorts, and the relentless, scraping sound of the travois dragging behind them.

Finally, when Marcus is certain he cannot take another step, that he will simply lie down and let the forest swallow him whole, Cassian stops. They are at the base of a rocky escarpment, a wall of stone rising up into the darkness. Hidden by a curtain of ancient ivy is a dark opening. A cave.

CASSIAN
We rest here.

He unhitches the horse and ties it to a low-hanging branch where it can graze on what little scrub is available. Then, with an ease that belies the hours of travel, he lifts Gaius in his arms and carries him into the cave. Marcus follows, stumbling into the cool, damp dark.

Cassian lays Gaius down gently on the smooth stone floor. He builds a small, smokeless fire with practiced speed, using dry wood he must have stored there. The small flames push back the oppressive darkness, revealing a space that is larger than it appeared from the outside. It is dry, and deep within, there are signs of habitation. Bedrolls made of furs, a small stack of supplies, a waterskin. This is Cassian’s place.

Cassian immediately turns his attention back to Gaius, examining the old man in the flickering firelight.

CASSIAN
He has a fever. The swelling will be getting worse. There is little I can do for a wound like this.

MARCUS
But he will live?

Cassian meets his gaze. His eyes hold no comfort, only truth.

CASSIAN
Hope is a poor physician, boy. All we can do is make him comfortable. The rest is up to the gods. Or his own stubbornness.

He soaks a cloth in water from the skin and places it on Gaius’s forehead. Marcus watches, a hollow feeling of utter helplessness consuming him. He killed a man in a duel and felt the strange, terrifying thrill of it. Now he could do nothing to save the man who had served him his entire life.

CASSIAN
Eat.

He tosses a piece of hard, dry bread and a strip of salted meat to Marcus. It is tasteless, but Marcus eats ravenously. He did not realize how hungry he was.

MARCUS
Why are you doing all this? “Conflicting interests” is not a good enough reason to risk your life for a stranger. To take on a man like Corvin.

Cassian is silent for a long time, staring into the fire. The flames dance in his dark eyes.

CASSIAN
Fifteen years ago, Lord Corvin sat as a magistrate in a trial. A land dispute. A small farmer was accused of stealing from Corvin’s neighbor, a wealthy landowner with eyes on the farmer’s well-watered fields. The farmer was innocent. He had witnesses. He had proof.

MARCUS
What happened?

CASSIAN
Corvin was paid. The proof was dismissed as forgery. The witnesses were threatened into silence. The farmer was found guilty. He was stripped of his land, his name, his dignity. He was flogged and exiled. His wife died of shame and sickness a year later.

A log in the fire cracks, sending a shower of sparks into the air.

CASSIAN
His son was sent to the salt mines as an indentured criminal. He was twelve years old.

Marcus looks at the hard, weathered face of the man across the fire from him. He is no longer just a stranger, a mysterious savior. He is a story. A ghost, as Drusus had called him.

MARCUS
That was you. You were the son.

Cassian does not confirm or deny it. He simply continues to stare into the flames, his expression as unyielding as the stone walls of the cave around them.

CASSIAN
Lord Corvin took everything from that family. He built his power on the ruins of other people’s lives. He believes he is untouchable. He believes consequences are for lesser men. Killing his son was the first consequence he has faced in a very long time. It has made him… vulnerable. Unbalanced. And a man who is unbalanced makes mistakes.

He looks at Marcus, his eyes sharp and intense.

CASSIAN
You are not the reason I am here, Marcus Valerius. You are merely the tool. You are the stone that I will use to bring the whole rotten structure of his life crashing down.

The stage goes to black.
