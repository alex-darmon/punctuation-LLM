Enter Lucian and Gareth.

GARETH
I pray you, Lucian, put that humour by.
The Duke’s own court is no fit place for barbs.

LUCIAN
Why, man, my wit is like a courtier’s shoe; it is made to tread on tender toes. Thou art too soft for this world, Gareth. Thou wouldst apologize to a stone for stumbling on it. Thou wouldst start a quarrel with a cloud for raining, having no other cause but that thy best cloak is of a wat’ry hue. What man but such a man would find fault with his own reflection for looking so grimly back at him? Thy heart is so full of peace it hath no room for a pulse, and yet for all thy quiet, thou art drawn into my scuffles as a moth is to my candle. Didst thou not have words with a cook for seasoning the venison too high? with another for wearing a feather of the same shade as thine own? And yet thou wilt school me on discretion!

GARETH
And I were half so keen to jest as thou art, any man might purchase the lease of my tongue for a copper groat and a stale crust.

LUCIAN
The lease! O base!

Enter Lord Valerius and his retainers.

GARETH
By my soul, here comes Valerius and his pack.

LUCIAN
By my empty purse, I fear them not.

VALERIUS
Keep pace, I’ll have a word with one of them.
Good morrow, gentlemen. I seek Cassian.

LUCIAN
He is not here. But you may have a word with me, my lord. Or two, if your wit can furnish them. Perchance you might couple it with a sharp look?

VALERIUS
You shall find my temper sharp enough, sir, should you test its edge.

LUCIAN
Could you not find a reason for your temper, without my testing? Or is it as baseless as a courtier’s promise?

VALERIUS
Lucian, thou keepest company with Cassian.

LUCIAN
Company? What, dost thou make us merchants, to trade in fellowship? And thou make merchants of us, look to find our accounts are full of scorn. Here is my ledger; here’s that will reckon with you. Zounds, company!

GARETH
We stand here in the common eye of the court.
Withdraw into some gallery or chamber,
And speak your grievances with calmer tongues,
Or else take leave; for all the world looks on.

LUCIAN
Men’s eyes were made to stare, so let them stare.
I will not shift my ground for any man.

Enter Cassian.

VALERIUS
Well, let peace be your shield, sir, here comes my quarrel.

LUCIAN
But I’ll be flayed, sir, if he serves your cause. Marry, walk him to the field, he’ll be your master; your lordship in that sense may call him quarrel.

VALERIUS
Cassian, the honour that I owe my house
Affords no gentler name for thee than this:
Thou art a traitor.

CASSIAN
Valerius, the debt I owe your house
Doth far outweigh the anger in thy words.
A traitor am I none;
Therefore good day; I see you know me not.

VALERIUS
Boy, this shall not excuse the slights and scorns
That thou hast laid on me, therefore turn and draw.

CASSIAN
I do protest I never slighted thee,
But hold thee in a bond thou canst not guess
Until thou know’st the reason for my bond.
And so, good Valerius, whose name I prize
As dearly as mine own, be satisfied.

LUCIAN
O foul and patient yielding! O base calm!
[_Draws._]
This pretty courtesy pays all the score.
Valerius, you master of the fence, will you advance?

VALERIUS
What wouldst thou have of me?

LUCIAN
Good Master of Mean Looks, nothing but a measure of your steel; that I mean to be acquainted with, and if you treat me rudely, send you limping for the rest of your days. Will you pluck your blade out of his scabbard by the hilt? Make haste, lest mine be at your throat ere it be out.

VALERIUS
[_Drawing._]
I am for you.

CASSIAN
Gentle Lucian, put thy sword up.

LUCIAN
Come, sir, your passado.
[_They fight._]

CASSIAN
Draw, Gareth; beat down their weapons.
Gentlemen, for shame, forbear this broil,
Valerius, Lucian, the Duke expressly hath
Forbid such conflict in the palace walls.
Hold, Valerius! Good Lucian!
[_Exeunt Valerius with his Retainers._]

LUCIAN
I am struck. A blight on both your banners. I am sped.
Is he gone, and hath no scratch?

GARETH
What, art thou hurt?

LUCIAN
Ay, ay, a nick, a trifle. Marry, ’tis sufficient.
Where is my boy? Go, varlet, fetch a physician.
[_Exit Boy._]

CASSIAN
Courage, man; the wound cannot be deep.

LUCIAN
No, ’tis not so wide as a fool’s grin, nor so deep as a politician’s purse, but ’tis enough, ’twill serve. Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find my wit is spent. I am seasoned, I warrant, for the next world. A blight on both your banners. Zounds, a peacock, a popinjay, a snake, to prick a man to silence. A formal, mannered coward, that fights by the rules of dancing! Why the devil came you between us? I was hurt beneath your arm.

CASSIAN
I thought all for the best.

LUCIAN
Help me to a chair, Gareth,
Or I shall fall. A blight on both your banners.
They have made a poor jest of me. I have it,
And soundly too. Your banners!
[_Exeunt Lucian and Gareth._]

CASSIAN
This gentleman, the Duke’s own favoured nephew,
My truest friend, hath got his mortal wound
In my defence; my honour smirch’d and stain’d
By Valerius’ slander. Valerius, that an hour
Hath been my brother. O, my dear Elara,
Thy love hath made me soft and womanish
And in my spirit quench’d a soldier’s fire.

Re-enter Gareth.

GARETH
O Cassian, Cassian, witty Lucian’s dead,
That fiery soul hath taken wing to heaven,
Which all too soon did hold the earth in scorn.

CASSIAN
This day’s dark business on more days depends;
This but begins the woe another ends.

Re-enter Valerius.

GARETH
Here comes the proud Valerius back again.

CASSIAN
Again in triumph, and my Lucian slain?
Away to heavens, you calculating thoughts,
And burning rage be now my only guide!
Now, Valerius, take the ‘traitor’ back again
That late thou gav’st me, for my Lucian’s soul
Is but a moment’s flight above our heads,
Waiting for thine to bear it company.
Either thou or I, or both, must follow him.

VALERIUS
Thou wretched boy, that didst consort him here,
Shalt with him hence.

CASSIAN
This blade shall settle that.
[_They fight; Valerius falls._]

GARETH
Cassian, away, be gone!
The guard is up, and Lord Valerius slain.
Stand not amaz’d. The Duke will doom thee death
If thou art taken. Hence, be gone, away!

CASSIAN
O, I am fortune’s fool!

GARETH
Why dost thou stay?
[_Exit Cassian._]

Enter Guards.

FIRST GUARD
Which way ran he that kill’d Valerius?
Cassian, that traitor, which way did he fly?

GARETH
There lies that Valerius.

FIRST GUARD
Up, sir, go with me.
I charge thee in the Duke’s name to obey.

Enter Duke, attended; Lady Aris, and others.

DUKE
Where are the vile beginners of this fray?

GARETH
O noble Duke, I can explain it all,
The sorry sequence of this fatal brawl.
There lies the man, slain by young Cassian’s hand,
That slew your kinsman, clever Lucian.

LADY ARIS
Valerius, my son! O my dear child!
O Duke! O husband! See the blood so wild
Run from my pride! Duke, as you are just,
For blood of ours, let their blood turn to dust!
O son, my son!

DUKE
Gareth, who began this bloody fray?

GARETH
Valerius, here slain, whom Cassian did slay;
Cassian, that spoke him fair, bid him reflect
How grave the quarrel was, and did expect
Your high displeasure. All this offered
With patient voice, calm mien, and bended head,
Could not appease the choler and the pride
Of Lord Valerius, who cast peace aside,
And with a fencer’s trick, with point of steel
Did wound your kinsman, bold Lucian’s breast,
Who, just as hot, met fury with a jest
And martial skill. Then Cassian cries aloud,
‘Hold, friends! Friends, part!’ and rushing through the crowd,
His arm beats down their points; but from beneath
That very arm, a spiteful thrust brought death
To stout Lucian, and then Valerius fled.
But presently came back, with haughty tread,
To Cassian, who had but new conceived revenge,
And to’t they went like lightning; for, ere I
Could draw my sword to part them, he did die,
The Lord Valerius, and Cassian did fly.
This is the truth, or let your Gareth die.

LADY ARIS
He is a friend to Cassian and his cause.
His love for him makes him defy the laws
Of honest witness. Nay, some twenty men
Did fight in this dark plot, and even then,
Those twenty swords could only take one life.
I beg for justice to conclude this strife;
Cassian slew my son, Cassian must not live.

DUKE
Cassian slew him, but he slew my kin.
Who now the price of Lucian’s blood shall win?

MONTAGUE
Not Cassian, Duke, he was Lucian’s friend;
His crime concludes but what the law should end,
The life of Valerius.

DUKE
And for that offence
Immediately we do banish him from hence.
I have a stake in your factions’ bitter game;
My blood is spilt to ornament your name.
But I’ll amerce you with so great a cost
That you shall weep for all that you have lost.

I will not hear another word of this.
My judgment stands. The sun must not go down
Upon our city’s rage. See to the dead;
Let them be borne with honour to their homes,
A grim reminder of this day’s distress.
And you, the heads of these two warring lines,
Montague and Aris, you shall come to me
At my own palace ere the vesper bell,
To know my further pleasure in this case.
For now, disperse. Clear these blood-spattered stones.
Let peace, though wounded, have some place to breathe.

LADY ARIS
Banishment? A holiday from justice!
A gentle journey for a murderer’s health!
Duke, this is not a sentence, but a gift.
He should be quartered, let his traitor’s heart
Be fed to dogs upon this very street!

DUKE
Have you no sense of what you ask, my lady?
Your son, Valerius, struck the first foul blow
That spilt my own dear blood. Were I to be
As you are, drunk with wrath, then all your house
Would pay the price. But I am prince and judge,
Not executioner to your desires.
His banishment is mercy, but a mercy
That carries in its core a blade of steel.
He is cut off from all he knows and loves.
Go to your home. Your grief is loud, I know,
But so is law, and it must be obeyed.
Attend me, lords.

[_Exeunt Duke, Montague, Guards, and Attendants._]

LADY ARIS
He is cut off? He breathes, he sees the sun,
He walks upon the earth that my son’s blood
Now sanctifies with murder. O, my lord,
My husband, where are you? Where is my kin?
Shall we stand by and watch this mockery?

[_Enter Lord Aris, an older, colder man than his wife._]

LORD ARIS
I am here, madam. I have heard it all.
The Duke is politic. He fears to lean
Too far to one side, lest the other rise
And shake his seat of power. It is wise,
As statecraft goes.

LADY ARIS
Wise? Call you this wisdom?
To let the serpent that has stung my child
Go sliding into exile, free to live?
This is not wisdom, husband, this is fear.
A coward’s compromise.

LORD ARIS
Then let him fear.
We need not be so careful in our grief.
The Duke has passed his sentence. Let it be.
The world is wide, and banishment is long.
But life, for one so hunted, can be short.
Come, wife. We’ll mourn Valerius at home,
And in our sorrow, we shall find a way
To make the Duke’s soft sentence absolute.
Gareth, you stay. A word with you, my friend.

[_Exeunt Lady Aris and her attendants, bearing the body of Valerius._]

GARETH
My lord, I am no friend to you or yours.
I stand with Cassian and with the truth.

LORD ARIS
The truth is what the victor writes, young man.
And in this city, we are not yet beaten.
You spoke a pretty tale there, for the Duke.
You painted Cassian as a blameless saint
Forced into combat. It was deftly done.
But I know you. You were his second self,
His shadow in the sun, his shield in strife.
Where has he fled?

GARETH
That, I will never tell.
He is beyond your reach, and so he’ll stay.

LORD ARIS
Beyond my reach? Is there a place on earth
So far a father’s vengeance cannot stretch?
You are a fool to think your loyalty
Can build a wall around him. Tell me now,
And I may speak for you. Your part in this
Is not so small as you would have them think.
You drew your sword. You were a part of it.
The Duke, in haste, has overlooked your crime.
I shall not be so careless.

GARETH
Threaten me.
It is the only language that you know.
First with your swords, and now with poisoned words.
Cassian is gone. That is all you need know.
Find him yourself, if your dark heart is set
Upon more bloodshed. I am done with you.

[_Exit Gareth._]

LORD ARIS
So be it. The stubborn friend protects the fool.
But every secret has a price, and soon,
Someone will sell me his. The world is full
Of men whose purses are more light than love.
Cassian, though banished, you are not yet safe.
Your exile is a prison, and its walls
Are all the world. I am the warden of it.

[_Exit Lord Aris._]

SCENE II. A Friar’s humble cell.

[_Enter Cassian, his clothes disordered, his face a mask of despair._]

CASSIAN
Banishment! O Friar, call it death!
For that is what it is. A slower knife,
A gentler poison, but the end’s the same.
To be cast out from all that gives life breath,
To wander strange lands with a stranger’s tongue,
To see no face I know, to hear no voice
That calls my name in friendship or in love.
This is not life. It is a painted tomb,
And I, a ghost, am cursed to walk within it.

[_Enter Friar Lawrence, a man of quiet wisdom and gentle eyes._]

FRIAR LAWRENCE
Hold, my good son. Thy words are wild with grief.
Thou art alive. The Duke, in his great mercy,
Hath looked upon thy deed and seen the cause,
And turned aside the axe of law for thee.
The world is not this city. Other skies
Are just as blue, and other suns as warm.
Banishment is not death. Be thankful for it.

CASSIAN
Thankful? For what? For this aching void?
For this sharp severance from my very soul?
You do not understand. You are a man
Of God, and find your country in your faith.
My world is here, within these city walls.
My friends, my honour, all my history.
And Elara… O Friar, she is here!
How can I live when half my heart remains
A prisoner here, while I am forced to fly?
To be in exile is to be forgot.
The world will spin, the seasons turn, and she…
She will be pressed to wed some other lord,
Some man who is not banished, not disgraced.
And I will be a whisper, then a breath,
And then be nothing. That is worse than death.

FRIAR LAWRENCE
Now you blaspheme against both love and hope.
If her affection is as true as yours,
Then distance is a test, not an abyss.
Her heart will travel with you. And the Duke,
His anger will not burn for evermore.
Time is a healer of all wounds, my son,
Even a prince’s pride. When this dark storm
Has passed, and all the tears are dry, your friends,
Lord Montague and others, may yet plead
Your case, and win a pardon for your fault.
You killed Valerius, who killed the Duke’s
Own kinsman. It is tangled, but not lost.
Be patient. Go, live for a while abroad.
And I shall be your eyes and ears at home,
And send you word of all that passes here.
When fortune smiles, I’ll call you back again
To claim your life, your honour, and your love.

CASSIAN
A pardon? Is there such a hope? A light
So distant in this darkness? Tell me, Friar,
Do you speak this from wisdom, or from pity?

FRIAR LAWRENCE
I speak it from my knowledge of the world.
Men’s passions are a tide; they ebb and flow.
The Duke is just, but he is also shrewd.
He banished you to quell the house of Aris.
When they are calm, and your own house more strong,
The scales of justice may be balanced new.
But you must help your fate. You must be gone
Before the morning watch. If you are found
Within the city walls when dawn arrives,
The sentence turns to death. That is the law.

CASSIAN
Death, banishment. They are the self-same thing.
But I will go. I’ll grasp this sliver’s hope
You offer me. But first, I must see her.
I must see Elara. Just one last time.
To look into her eyes, to hold her hand,
To say farewell. Without that, I lack strength
To take the first step on that lonely road.
Can you arrange it, Father? Can you help?

FRIAR LAWRENCE
It is a risk. The city watch is doubled.
The house of Montague is surely watched
By spies from Aris’s camp. It is most perilous.
And yet, a spirit starved of hope may die
More surely than a body starved of bread.
Let me consider. Stay you here, concealed.
I will send word. Be patient, and be still.
Your rashness brought you to this sorry pass.
Let wisdom be the guide to lead you from it.

[_Exit Cassian into an inner chamber. The Friar sits, deep in thought._]

SCENE III. A room in Montague’s house.

[_Elara paces the floor. Lyra, her old nurse, watches her with concern._]

ELARA
He did not send for me. The Duke is there,
And all the lords, and yet no word arrives.
O Lyra, this suspense is like a rack
That stretches out my soul upon its frame.
Each moment is an age of dreadful thought.
I see a scaffold, hear a heavy bell.
I see his face, so noble and so brave,
Look one last time upon an unkind world.
Why does my father not return?

LYRA
My lady,
You must have patience. These are matters of state.
They take their time. Lord Montague, your father,
Will do all that he can for Cassian.
He loves him as a son. You know he does.

ELARA
He does, but is his love a match for law?
Is it a shield against the rage of Aris?
Lady Aris is a she-wolf robbed of her whelp.
She will cry out for blood, and only blood
Will ever quench her thirst. Hark! A footstep.
It is my father. Go, Lyra, quickly see!

[_Lyra goes to the door. Enter Lord Montague, his face grim and weary._]

ELARA
Father! What news? O, tell me, is he safe?
Does Cassian live? Speak, though the word be death!

MONTAGUE
He lives.

ELARA
Thanks be to all the saints in heaven!
He lives! The Duke was merciful. O, joy!
What is the sentence, then? A fine? A year
Of penance in some cloister? What decree
Has tempered justice with such gentle grace?

MONTAGUE
The Duke has banished him.

ELARA
What did you say?
My ears have played me false. I heard a word
That sounded like… like banishment.

MONTAGUE
My child,
It is the truth. Young Cassian is banished.
At dawn, if he is found within the walls,
He dies.

[_Elara sways, and Lyra rushes to support her._]

LYRA
My lady! Dearest child! My lord,
This news is too severe. Her heart is frail.

ELARA
No, I am well. Leave me. Banished.
It is a word that has no sense or form.
It means… he is gone? Forever?

MONTAGUE
Elara,
It was the best that we could hope to gain.
Lady Aris shrieked for nothing less than death.
The Duke himself has lost his kinsman Lucian.
That Cassian lives at all is miracle.
His life is saved.

ELARA
His life? What life is that?
A life without a home, without a friend?
A life without a name, a life without…
Without a future? You call that a life?
It is a ship without a port or star,
Adrift upon a dark and endless sea.
And I am left upon the shore to watch
Until it sinks below the world’s far edge.
O, father, you have brought me home a shroud
And told me it is silk for my wedding day.

MONTAGUE
Daughter, you must be strong. This is a blow
To all our house. The feud will not sleep now.
The house of Aris will not be content.
We must be vigilant. Your safety, too,
Is now my deepest care. Lord Aris has
A cold and plotting mind. He will seek hurt
In any place he can.

ELARA
My safety?
What is my safety when my heart is gone?
He is alone out there. Hunted, perhaps.
With every setting sun, I’ll think of him
In some rude shelter, with no friendly face.
With every storm, I’ll think him cold and lost.
Did you not plead for him? Did you not tell
The Duke of all his worth, his noble soul?

MONTAGUE
I spoke until my throat was hoarse with it.
But blood cries louder than a courtier’s plea.
The Duke is firm. The sentence cannot change.
We must endure it. Now, go to your room.
Rest, if you can. The night will be a long one.

[_Exit Montague._]

ELARA
Endure it? He asks me to endure the fire
That melts the very substance of my life.
O, Lyra, what am I to do? To live
Knowing he wanders, exiled and alone?
My heart is banished with him. All of me
That felt and hoped and dreamed has gone with him.
I am a hollow shell left on the sand.

LYRA
My child, my lamb. Do not give way to this.
While there is life, there is a chance for hope.
He is not dead. The Friar, good old man,
He has a subtle and a resourceful mind.
And he loves Cassian. Let me go to him.
Perhaps there is some comfort to be found,
Some message to be sent, some final word.

ELARA
A final word. Yes. That, at least. That much
The cruellest fate must grant me. Take this ring.
[_She slips a ring from her finger._]
Give it to him. Tell him this circle means
My love for him is endless, like its form.
Tell him that though our bodies may be parted,
My soul will seek his out in every prayer.
And ask the Friar, in his holy grace,
If I might see him. O, but for a moment!
To say goodbye. Go now, good Lyra, fly!
My future hangs upon the speed of your return.

[_Exit Lyra. Elara collapses in a chair, weeping._]

SCENE IV. A lavish chamber in the house of Aris.

[_Lord Aris stands by a window, looking out into the night. Lady Aris sits, staring into the fire, her face hard as stone._]

LADY ARIS
The sun has set. The murderer still breathes.
He is preparing for his pleasant trip,
Packing his bags with stolen, happy years.
And my Valerius lies upon a slab,
His journey over. Cold. So cold and still.
Husband, you are too calm. Your blood runs slow.
Does not the image of our slaughtered son
Set fire to your veins?

LORD ARIS
My fire burns
Within, my lady, where it can forge steel.
A public rage is but a show of smoke.
It warns the enemy. My grief is cold,
And all the more deadly for the ice it wears.

LADY ARIS
What steel? What ice? I see but talk and sighs.
The Duke has cheated us. And you consent.
You let this Cassian slip right through our hands
To live a long and comfortable life
In some warm, foreign land.

LORD ARIS
Comfortable?
Do you think so? An exile’s life is hard.
He has no friends, no money, and no name.
He will be watched. And he will be afraid.
Every shadow will hold a threat for him.
Every corner will hide an enemy.

LADY ARIS
I want him dead! I do not care for threats.
I want his heart to stop, his breath to cease.
I want the same cold silence for his mother
That now consumes my own maternal soul.
Since law refuses me, I will find means
Beyond the law.

LORD ARIS
And that is what I think.
But we must be discreet. The Duke’s command,
Though weak, is still the law. If we are caught
Defying it, our whole house could be fined,
Or worse, our titles stripped, our lands forfeit.
We must not be connected to his end.

LADY ARIS
Then how? How can we reach him far away
And leave no trace of our own handiwork?

LORD ARIS
There are men in Mantua, and in Padua too,
Who are not rich, but are most skilled with drugs.
A little powder in a cup of wine,
A subtle poison that mimics a fever,
And our dear Cassian’s travels will be done.
He will be buried in some foreign soil,
A lonely death, with no one there to weep.
A note will reach the Duke that the poor boy,
So filled with grief and shame, succumbed to illness.
Who would suspect?

LADY ARIS
A poison. Yes.
It is a coward’s tool. I wished for steel.
I wished to see him bleed as my son bled.
But if it brings his death, then I am pleased.
How soon can this be done?

LORD ARIS
I have a man,
A merchant who departs for Mantua
Within the week. I will give him a purse
Of gold, and careful, secret instructions.
He will find the right apothecary there.
We only have to learn where Cassian goes.
And for that, I have set a watch upon
The Friar’s cell, and on the house of Montague.
He will not leave this city without some
Attempt to see his friends, or that pale girl,
Lord Montague’s sad daughter. When he moves,
My spies will follow, and his path is known.
Then our quiet message can be sent ahead.

LADY ARIS
Good. This is good. At last, a worthy plan.
My mind has some small ease. Now I can grieve
And know that vengeance is not just a dream,
But a design, a map that leads to death.
O, my Valerius, your ghost will rest.
Your mother swears it on your sacred blood.

[_They stand in silence, two figures of cold, determined hate._]

SCENE II.

The Friar’s cell. A simple room with a wooden table, a few books, and a crucifix on the wall. A single candle burns, casting long, dancing shadows. FRIAR LAURENCE is pacing the floor. There is a frantic knocking at the door.

FRIAR LAURENCE
Who comes with such a storm to beat my door?
The night is late, and honest men are sleeping.
Speak, lest I think you some unholy thing
That walks abroad when darkness rules the world.

VOICE (O.S.)
Father! It is I, Cassian! Let me in!
For pity’s sake, unbar the door to me,
A wretched soul who finds all other doors
Now shut against him.

FRIAR LAURENCE
(Unbarring the door)
Cassian, my son.
Your voice is wild, your knuckles bruised and raw.
Come in, come in from the accusing night.
The Duke’s decree has reached me. I have heard.
You are to live. Thank heaven for its grace.

(CASSIAN stumbles into the room. His clothes are disheveled, his face a mask of anguish.)

CASSIAN
You call this living? Father, you mistake
The word’s true sense. This is a breathing death.
This is a hell reserved for those who loved
Too much on earth. To banish me from here
Is to tear out my eyes and bid me see,
To cut my tongue and ask that I should sing.
Verona holds my life. It holds my soul.
It holds the very air I need to breathe,
For Helena is here. And I am not.

FRIAR LAURENCE
Be patient, son. This is a gentler doom
Than what the law demanded. Be you glad.
The Duke has turned his wrath to clemency.
Valerius is dead, but you may live.
This is not justice, Cassian, this is grace.

CASSIAN
Grace? You call this grace? O, you are a priest,
And see the world through scripture’s patient lens.
But I am flesh and blood, and my hot heart
Cannot be cooled by holy rhetoric.
Had he pronounced my death, it were a kindness.
One sharp, swift pain, and then an endless peace.
But exile, Father, exile is a blade
That does not kill, but tortures every hour.
Each dawn I wake in some strange, hateful town,
I will remember dawn in fair Verona.
Each face I see will be a mocking ghost
For it is not her face. Each word I hear,
A noise, a clatter, for it is not her voice.
Do you not see? The world beyond these walls
Is but a desert, empty, vast, and cruel.
And I am sent to wander in it, mad
With thirst for one I never more can see.

FRIAR LAURENCE
Your words are wild, born of your sudden grief.
Sit down, my son. Let reason be your guide.
Mantua is not so far. The world is wide,
And full of hope for those who know to look.
You are yet young. Your life is not yet done.

CASSIAN
My life was done the moment that he spoke
The word of banishment. My name is dead,
My honor is a ghost, my fortune lost.
And Helena… O father, say her name
And feel the sorrow that it brings to me.
How will she live, hearing that I am gone?
Her father’s house will be a prison to her.
Her tears will be her only company.
She thinks me false, perhaps. She thinks I fled
Like some base coward, leaving her to weep.

FRIAR LAURENCE
She knows you are no coward. I have sent
A message to her, through her trusted nurse,
That you are safe, and hiding in my cell,
And that I beg a moment of her time
To come and bid a final, fond farewell.

CASSIAN
(Leaping to his feet)
You did this? You have asked her here? To this place?
O, blessed man! O, comfort in despair!
When will she come? Does she already know?
To see her one last time, to feel her hand,
To look into her eyes and there to store
A memory to light my endless dark…
It is a gift I did not dare to ask.

FRIAR LAURENCE
I hope she comes, and soon. The watch is set,
And you must be away before the sun
Paints traitors on the eastern hills for all
The world to see. Now listen to my plan.
This is no end. It is a harsh delay.
You will to Mantua, and live there for a time,
Discreet and private. Keep your spirits up.
I will remain here, and will choose my moment
To speak unto your friends, and to the Duke.
When tempers cool, and this sad tragedy
Is but a memory, we will announce
Your secret marriage. Then we will implore
The Duke for pardon on our bended knees.
With all your friends to speak upon your side,
I do not doubt he will recall you home,
With twice the honor that you had before.

CASSIAN
A plan? There is a plan? O, say it twice,
That hope’s faint music may convince my soul
It is not dreaming. A secret marriage,
You say? But we are not yet wed.

FRIAR LAURENCE
A flaw
Easily mended. I have the power,
And you the will. If she agrees to it,
A vow before you go, a sacred bond,
Will make you man and wife in heaven’s eyes.
It will give her the strength to wait for you,
And you a reason to endure your pain.

(There is a soft, hesitant knock at the door.)

Hush now. I think she comes. Remember, son,
No wild despair. Be calm and rational.
You must give her your strength, not she give hers
To your unmanly tears.

(The Friar opens the door. HELENA stands there, veiled and cloaked, accompanied by her old NURSE.)

NURSE
Good father, I have brought her as you asked.
The house is sleeping. Not a soul did stir.
But we must be swift. Her father’s temper,
Should he awake and find her chamber empty,
Is like a tempest.

FRIAR LAURENCE
Thank you, my good woman.
Wait here, outside the door. Your watchfulness
Is its own virtue.
(To Helena)
Come in, my child. Do not be afraid.
There is a friend here who would speak with you.

(HELENA enters, her face pale in the candlelight. She sees Cassian, and a small cry escapes her lips. They rush into each other’s arms.)

HELENA
Cassian! They said… I heard you were to die.
My heart has been a cold and heavy stone
Within my breast these past two dreadful days.

CASSIAN
And now it beats again to hold you close.
Helena, my love, my only life.
To see you is to see the sun again
After a century of bitter night.
But it is true. I am condemned. Not dead,
But banished. I must leave Verona’s walls
Before the morning comes, or I will die.

HELENA
Then I will go with you. Banish me too.
I care not where we go. A desert hut
With you is paradise. A palace here,
Without your face to look upon each day,
Is but the blackest dungeon. Let me come.
I will not be a burden. I will work,
And serve you, and my love will make us rich.

CASSIAN
My sweet, brave heart. If only it could be.
But how could I, a penniless, shamed man,
Drag you, a noble lady, through the mire
Of my disgrace? To make you live in want,
An exile’s wife, with no good name to shield you?
No, I must bear this punishment alone.
You must stay here, my hidden, precious jewel,
And wait for me.

FRIAR LAURENCE
The boy speaks wisely, child.
To go with him would be to seal his fate,
And ruin your own name beyond repair.
But I have offered him a path to hope.
A plan that, with God’s grace and patient time,
May bring him home to you a pardoned man.

HELENA
What plan? What hope? O, father, speak of it.
For any glimmer in this awful dark
Is worth a kingdom.

FRIAR LAURENCE
I propose you two
Exchange your vows this very night, right here.
Become as one in holy matrimony.
Then Cassian will to Mantua, and wait,
While I work here to mend the broken peace.
Your marriage, when revealed at the right time,
Will be a powerful and moving plea
To join two houses and to end the feud
That cost Valerius his life, and you
Your present happiness. The Duke has a heart.
He will not keep a husband from his wife
Forever.

HELENA
(Looking at Cassian)
A wife? To be your wife?
Even if you are a hundred leagues away,
To know that I am yours, and you are mine,
By law of heaven? Yes. O, Cassian, yes.
My heart has been your own since first we met.
Let my vows speak it to the listening saints.

CASSIAN
And mine to you. Then let this grim farewell
Be but the preface to our joyful tale.
Father, we are ready. Do not delay.
Let this small, holy candle be the sun
That shines upon our wedding.

(The Friar takes his stole and a small book. Cassian and Helena kneel before him, their hands clasped. Outside, hidden in the deeper shadows of the cloister wall, two men watch the faint light in the Friar’s window.)

LURCO
Did you see that? A woman, by my oath.
And cloaked as if she were a midnight thief.

VETUS
I saw her. That was Montague’s one chick,
The pale one, Helena. And with her nurse.
So the young cockerel comes here not for prayer,
But for a hen. It makes a kind of sense.
His life is forfeit if he’s found at dawn.
A man might wish for one last taste of wine
Before the long fast.

LURCO
Quiet. You speak too loud.
Lord Aris does not pay us for our thoughts
On wine and women. He pays for the facts.
The boy is in the cell. The girl is there.
This means he has not fled the city yet.
We wait and watch. When he departs this place,
One of us follows him until we know
Which road he takes. The North road to Milan?
Or South to Mantua? That single word
Is worth a hundred ducats to our lord.

VETUS
Mantua, I’d wager. It is closer.
And the Gonzaga court is ever slack
In hunting fugitives from other states.
He will think himself safe there. Poor, foolish boy.
He does not know that distance is no cure
For a long purse and an even longer hate.
Did you see Lady Aris’s eyes today?
As cold and hard as funeral marble.
She wants his blood. And she will have it, too.

LURCO
She will. And we will have her husband’s gold.
So keep your tongue from wagging, and your eyes
Upon that door. The less we say, the less
We can be made to say, should things go wrong.
Our job is but to see, and to report.
Now watch.

(Inside the cell, the ceremony is concluding.)

FRIAR LAURENCE
… and what God has thus joined together,
Let no man put asunder. You are now
Man and wife. You may not kiss the bride,
For time is short and grief is at the door.
But take this blessing.
(He makes the sign of the cross over them)
Now, Cassian, you must go.
The eastern sky will not long wear its black.
Take this small purse. It is not much, but will
Secure you lodging for a week or two.
And send me word, through a trusted source I know,
Once you are settled. I will write to you
Of all that passes here.

CASSIAN
My wife. The word is music.
(He turns to Helena, taking her hands)
Helena, my wife. I must depart.
But I leave not my heart, for that stays here
In your sweet keeping. Wear this ring for me.

(He gives her a simple gold ring.)

It was my mother’s. Let it be a sign
That I am ever with you.

HELENA
And take this.
(She removes a locket from her neck)
It holds a lock of my own hair. Let it lie
Against your heart, and whisper to you there
That I am constant, faithful, and your own,
Until we meet again, or death shall part us.

NURSE
(Knocking softly)
Sir, Madam, you must part. I hear the sound
Of the first cart that rumbles to the market.
The city starts to wake.

CASSIAN
Then I must sleep.
Farewell. O, that one word contains all pain.
My love. My wife.

HELENA
My husband. Go. Be safe.
Write to me. Live for me.

(They share one last, desperate embrace. Cassian pulls his cloak around his face and slips out of the cell door, the Friar showing him a path through the back of the monastery gardens.)

FRIAR LAURENCE
God go with you, son. Be wise and be patient.

HELENA
(Sobbing quietly)
He is gone. The light is gone.

FRIAR LAURENCE
The sun will rise again, my child. For you,
And him. Have faith. Now you must go, as well,
Before your absence from your father’s house
Is noted. Go in peace. Be strong. Remember
You are a wife now, and must have the courage
A wife must own.

(Helena, supported by the Nurse, leaves the cell. The Friar is left alone. He sinks onto a stool, the picture of exhaustion and worry.)

FRIAR LAURENCE
A heavy work this is. To patch up peace
With secret threads that may be snapped so soon.
Protect them, Lord. Protect their tender love
From the harsh winds of this world’s bitter hate.

SCENE III.

A street outside the monastery. LURCO and VETUS emerge from the shadows.

VETUS
He took the garden path. It leads directly
To the south gate. I told you. It is Mantua.
I will follow at a distance, just to be sure
He does not double back. You take the news
To Lord Aris. Tell him his fox has flown
South to his new earth.

LURCO
And tell him too
That we expect the rest of our reward
Before the sun is fully in the sky.
His hate can have its breakfast, once our purses
Have had their own. Be careful. Do not be seen.

VETUS
I am a shadow, friend. A ghost with ears.
The boy will know no more that I am there
Than does a mouse that the quiet owl above
Is watching him.

(Vetus slips away down the dark street. Lurco pulls his own cloak tight and heads in the opposite direction.)

SCENE IV.

A chamber in the house of Montague. LORD MONTAGUE and LADY MONTAGUE are standing over HELENA, who is seated, her face tear-stained.

LORD MONTAGUE
I say you will not weep! What cause have you
For all these tears? Your family is safe.
Your name is not disgraced. This Cassian,
This hot-blood brawler of the public streets,
Is gone, and good riddance. He has brought shame
And death into this city. That your name
Was ever linked with his, however briefly,
Is stain enough. You should be glad of it.

HELENA
He is no brawler, father. He is a man
Of honor and of gentle heart. Valerius
Did mock and challenge him. What could he do?
The code of honor forced his hand to draw.

LORD MONTAGUE
The code of honor? Do not speak to me
Of honor, girl. I know its shape and weight.
Honor is prudence. Honor is restraint.
And honor, most of all, is loyalty
To one’s own house and one’s own noble class.
This Cassian is a traitor to his own.
And you, with all this endless, girlish grief,
Are fast becoming traitor unto yours.

LADY MONTAGUE
My lord, be gentle. She is young. Her heart
Is tender, and she thought she was in love.
Time will attend to it. Her sorrow now
Is but a summer shower. It will pass.

LORD MONTAGUE
It will pass sooner if I make it pass.
I have been patient with this fantasy.
I have indulged these sighs and stolen looks.
But now the game is done. A man is dead.
A family in this city cries for blood.
We must show them, and all Verona too,
Where our allegiance lies. It is not with
A banished killer. Therefore, you will cease
This lamentation. You will dry your eyes,
And you will ready your most cheerful face.
For I have news for you. Important news.

HELENA
What news, my lord? No news can be of worth
To me, unless it be that Cassian
Is pardoned and may come back home again.

LORD MONTAGUE
(His voice rising in anger)
Still on that name! Have you no sense of shame?
You will forget that name. You will erase it
From your soft mind. For you are to be wed.
And not to some disgraced and penniless rogue,
But to a man of fortune and high birth.
Count Parisio has asked me for your hand.

HELENA
(Standing in horror)
Count Parisio? That man? I do not know him.
I have but seen him once at some state ball.
He is a stranger to me.

LORD MONTAGUE
He is the Duke’s own kinsman! He is rich!
His name is spotless and his future bright.
He is no stranger to your prospects, girl.
This is an honor I did not expect.
His suit will raise our house and heal the breach
This sad affair has made in our good name.
I have accepted. The marriage will be
On Thursday next.

HELENA
On Thursday? No! It cannot be.
It is too sudden. Father, I beseech you.
I am not well. My heart is full of grief.
How can I be a bride when all my soul
Is dressed in mourning?

LORD MONTAGUE
This is not a plea.
It is a statement of a settled fact.
Your wedding day is set. You will be ready.
You will go to the church upon my arm,
And you will smile, and you will speak your vows,
And you will be a proud and happy bride,
Or you will be my daughter nevermore.
Do you now understand the choice you have?

HELENA
I will not wed him. I would rather wed
The grave itself, and have my bridal bed
Be in the family crypt. My hand will not
Be given to a man my heart abhors.
My vows are promised to another’s name.

LORD MONTAGUE
(Advancing on her)
You dare defy me? In my own house?
You graceless, disobedient, wretched girl!
My fingers itch to strike that pale, proud face.
Get to your room. And do not leave it, save
To go to church. If you are not there
On Thursday morning, ready to obey,
Then by my soul, I will cast you out myself
Into the streets, to beg or starve or die.
We have no place for daughters who are not
Our property to give as we see fit.
Now go!

(Helena flees the room in tears. Lady Montague tries to intervene.)

LADY MONTAGUE
My lord, you are too harsh with her.
She is our only child. To force her thus…

LORD MONTAGUE
I am not harsh, I am her father. She
Is but a foolish girl who does not know
What is for her own good. She will thank me
In later years. This marriage will proceed.
I will not have my family’s reputation
Ruined by a sentimental child. It is decided.

SCENE V.

The study in the house of Aris. LORD ARIS stands by a table, looking at a map. LADY ARIS is seated nearby, her hands idle in her lap. LURCO is admitted.

LORD ARIS
Well? Speak. What is your news? I have been waiting.

LURCO
My lord, the man you watch is on the move.
He left the city just before the dawn,
By way of the south gate. My partner Vetus
Follows him now, to make his course secure.
He takes the road to Mantua.

LORD ARIS
(A cold smile touches his lips)
To Mantua. Good. That is as I wished.
The journey is not long. Two days, perhaps.
He will be tired when he arrives, and thirsty.
Here is your payment.
(He tosses a heavy purse to Lurco)
You have done well. Now go. And speak of this
To no one, ever. If a word of this
Should ever reach another person’s ear,
Your life is forfeit. Do you understand?

LURCO
My lord, my silence is as deep and black
As any well. You have my solemn word.

(Lurco bows and exits.)

LADY ARIS
So it is done. The path is known to us.
The arrow knows its target.

LORD ARIS
It is begun.
He calls for his steward.
Ho, bring me Silas, my chief factor here.
The merchant who prepares to leave today.
(To his wife)
Our man of business, Silas, is discreet.
He owes his fortune to my patronage.
He will ask nothing, and he will obey.

(SILAS, a well dressed merchant, enters and bows.)

SILAS
My lord, you sent for me? My mules are packed.
My guards are ready. I await your leave
To start my journey.

LORD ARIS
Silas, you are bound
For Mantua, is that not so?

SILAS
It is, my lord.
To meet with agents for the finest silks
That come from Genoa.

LORD ARIS
A worthy trade.
I have a small commission for you there.
A private matter. I have here a purse,
(He produces a very heavy purse of gold)
And a sealed letter. You will take them both,
And when you are in Mantua, you will find
An apothecary, by the name of Cordo,
In the small lane behind the ducal square.
His shop is marked by a green serpent’s sign.
Give him this letter and this purse of gold.
He will know what it means. You need not wait
For any answer. Just deliver them,
And then go on about your usual trade.

SILAS
(Taking the items)
My lord, it will be done as you command.
This Cordo, I will find him. You may trust me.

LORD ARIS
I know I may. A safe journey to you, Silas.
May all your trading prosper.

(Silas bows again and exits.)

LADY ARIS
The wheels are turning now. The trap is set.
My mind can almost see it. A dark room
In some cheap inn. A cup of sour wine
Made suddenly more potent. Then a fever,
A quiet groan, and then… an end to him.

LORD ARIS
It will be noted as a common sickness.
The grief of exile, a malignant air.
No one will question it. The Duke will hear
And nod his head, and think his judgment proved,
That banishment itself was punishment enough.
He will not know our justice has its own
Long arm, to reach where his could not.

LADY ARIS
O, Valerius. My son, my dearest son.
Your killer walks his final, weary miles.
He thinks he walks toward a newer life.
He does not know he walks directly to
The cold embrace of his own waiting grave.
Your mother sees to it. Your ghost will rest at last.
At last, my heart will know a moment’s peace.

(A long silence hangs in the air. LORD ARIS watches his wife. Her eyes are distant, fixed on a vision only she can see. He steps toward her, placing a hand gently on her arm.)

LORD ARIS
The words are spoken. Now the silent wait,
Which is the harder part of any plan.
The deed is distant, but the worry near.
Come, sit with me. Let not this poison work
Upon our spirits, when it is designed
For him alone.

LADY ARIS
(Pulling her arm away, not with anger, but with a cold distraction)
I feel no poison, husband. I feel… clean.
As if a film of grime that coated me
Since that dark day has now been washed away.
I breathe again. The air is not so foul
With his continued life. The waiting? Yes.
The waiting is a trial. But I will bear it.
I would wait years to know this day would come.

(ELARA, a young serving woman, enters quietly.)

ELARA
My lady, pardon me. The seamstress waits.
She has the velvet that you wished to see
For the new gown. She asks if you are free
To look upon the cloth.

LADY ARIS
(Turning on her with a sudden fire)
A gown? You speak of gowns? You think my mind
Has room for silks and velvets at this hour?
Tell her to go. Tell her to burn the cloth.
I have no need of it. I have no need
For anything but silence. Leave me now.

ELARA
(Flinching back, terrified)
Yes, my lady. At once. Forgive me.

(Elara curtsies low and hurries from the room.)

LORD ARIS
That was not wise, my love. A servant’s tongue
Wags more on mysteries than certainties.
Your grief is known, but such a sudden rage
Will plant a seed of wonder in her mind.
We must be as we were. Two parents bowed
By sorrow, yes. But not two hunters poised
Above the snare.

LADY ARIS
Forgive me. You are right. My nerves are strung
As taut as any bowstring. Every sound
Seems like a threat, or else an idle fool’s
Distraction from the purpose of my soul.
I will compose myself. I will be stone
Until we have the news.

LORD ARIS
And when it comes?

LADY ARIS
When he is dead, and word of it has crossed
The mountains and the plains to find our door?
Then I will wear the brightest silk of all.
And they will call it unseemly grief.
They will not know it is a victor’s robe.

LORD ARIS
Come. Let us walk within the gallery.
The painted faces of our ancestors
Will lend us their impassive strength. We wait.
And heaven help the man who stands between
This waiting and its end.

(They exit. The stage is empty for a moment. The scene changes.)

(SCENE: A dusty road somewhere between the Dukedom and Mantua. The light is fading. CLAUDIO and LEO, his friend and companion, sit on a fallen log by the roadside.)

LEO
The sun is low. We should have pushed ahead.
That village, two miles back, had a clean inn.
This dusk will bring a chill into the air.

CLAUDIO
I could not, Leo. I could not go on.
My legs are lead, but it is not the road
That wears me down. My soul is tired. It walks
This path in chains. Each step I take away
From all I knew, the heavier they seem.

LEO
You must not think on that. What’s done is done.
The Duke was merciful. He gave you life.
Exile is not a death. It is a birth
In some new land. Mantua is fair, they say.
A place of art and learning. We can build
A new life there. You have your skill with sums,
Your knowledge of the law. You will not starve.

CLAUDIO
He gave me life. And I took one from him.
From Lord Aris. His son. Valerius.
I see his face when I attempt to sleep.
He was my friend, you know. Before the rage,
Before the wine and insult lit the flame
That reason could not quench. I see his eyes,
So full of shock. Not anger. Just surprise
That I, his friend, could hold a blade so true.

LEO
It was a duel. It was not meant to kill.
His lunge was wild. You parried. It was chance,
A slip of steel, a stumble on the grass.
The council heard the facts. They knew it was
No planned attack. Hence, banishment, not death.
You must forgive yourself.

CLAUDIO
Forgive? How can I?
Does the hand that drops the priceless vase
And sees it shatter into dust forgive
Itself for clumsiness? No. It remains
An empty hand, that aches with what it lost.
I wrecked a family. I broke their hearts.
And for what gain? A moment’s foolish pride.
Lady Aris… I saw her at the trial.
Her face was not a human face at all.
It was a mask of winter. Cold and hard.
She did not weep. She watched me, and I felt
My blood run cold. Her silence was a curse
More terrible than any shout or tear.

LEO
She is a mother grieving for her son.
What else could you expect? But she is there,
And you are here. The miles will be a balm.
New sights, new sounds, new people. Time will blunt
The sharpest edge of this. Come, stand with me.
Let us walk on. The moon will light our way.
Mantua awaits. A place to build again
What you have wrecked. A place to find your peace.

CLAUDIO
(He rises slowly, heavily)
Peace. I would settle for a dreamless night.
Just one. Where I do not see his falling form,
Or her unblinking eyes. You are a friend,
Leo, to follow me into this dark.
I do not merit such a loyalty.

LEO
Then live a life that does. Come now. The road.

(They shoulder their packs and walk off into the gathering gloom. The scene changes.)

(SCENE: An apothecary’s shop in Mantua. The room is dark, cluttered. Shelves are filled with dusty glass jars containing herbs, powders, and coiled things suspended in liquid. A stuffed owl with glass eyes stares from a high perch. CORDO, a gaunt, ancient man with thin white hair, is carefully grinding something in a mortar. There is a knock at the door. Cordo does not look up.)

CORDO
The shop is closed. Come back when morning breaks.
The sick should learn some patience with the night.
It is their constant, true companion.

(The door opens anyway, and SILAS enters. He looks around nervously.)

SILAS
Forgive my entrance. I was told to come.
Are you the man they call Cordo the wise?

CORDO
(Still not looking up, his pestle moving in a slow, steady circle)
Some call me wise. Some call me something else.
It makes no matter to the herbs I grind.
They do not care for names. What do you want?
I have no cure for fear, if that’s your plague.
It is the only one I cannot sell.

SILAS
I am not sick. I come from far away.
A journey of some weeks. I bear a letter,
And this, for you.
(He places the sealed letter and the heavy purse on the counter.)
My master, Lord Aris, sends them to you.

(At the name, CORDO stops grinding. He slowly raises his head. His eyes are pale and piercing.)

CORDO
Aris. I know the name. A noble house.
I knew his father, many years ago.
A man who understood that some weeds must
Be pulled up by the root, lest they should choke
The garden’s prize.
(He picks up the letter, examining the wax seal.)
The serpent crest. Unbroken. Good.
And this?
(He lifts the purse, judging its weight in his palm.)
A healthy purse. The patient must be loved.
Or hated. Gold weighs just the same for both.

SILAS
My lord just said to give these things to you.
He said you’d know their meaning. I was told
I need not wait for any answer. My work
Is now complete.

CORDO
(Cracking the seal with a long, yellowed fingernail. He unrolls the parchment and reads it, his thin lips barely moving. His face shows no expression. After a moment, he rolls it back up.)
Yes. The diagnosis is quite clear.
A lingering disease. A wasting sickness.
One that has proven resistant to all
The common cures. It requires a more…
Specific remedy. A single dose.

(He holds the parchment over a candle flame. It blackens, curls, and turns to ash, which he lets fall into a brass bowl.)

SILAS
I do not need the details of your work.
I am a merchant, not a man of… this.
I will take my leave.

CORDO
Of course. You have done well. You are the hand
That passes coin across the counter. Clean.
Go on about your trade. Sell silks and spice.
Forget this alley and this serpent’s sign.
And tell your lord the cure will be prepared.
It will be swift. And it will seem quite natural.
I take some pride in that. My art is not
To kill, you understand. It is to help
Nature along its predetermined path.
To hasten the inevitable end.

SILAS
(Bowing hastily)
Good night to you.

(Silas exits quickly. Cordo watches him go, then calmly picks up the heavy purse and drops it into a lockbox. He returns to his mortar and pestle, and the rhythmic, grinding sound fills the silence of the shop once more.)

(The scene changes.)

(SCENE: The gardens of the Aris estate. A few weeks have passed. LADY ARIS is staring at a rose bush, though not really seeing it. HELENA, her niece, a young woman in her late teens, approaches.)

HELENA
Aunt, there you are. I looked for you inside.
The house feels… quiet. Heavy. Like the air
Before a storm. Are you unwell?

LADY ARIS
(Turning, her expression softening slightly at the sight of her niece)
Helena. No, child. I am well enough.
Just thinking. This was his favorite place.
Valerius. He loved these white roses.
He said they were like honest, open souls.
Not like the red ones, hiding passion’s thorns.

HELENA
I remember. He brought me one, that day
He taught me how to hold a dueling sword.
My hand was clumsy, and he laughed so hard.
He said I fought as if I held a fish.
I miss his laugh. I miss it more than words.

LADY ARIS
We all do, child. We all do.

HELENA
Is it enough? This punishment? This exile?
That man, Claudio, he walks beneath the sun.
He breathes the air. He eats and drinks and sleeps.
And my dear cousin lies beneath the earth.
It does not seem a balance. It feels wrong.
I hear the whispers in the court. They say
The Duke was lenient. That our family’s grief
Was not well served by such a gentle stroke.

LADY ARIS
(Her voice taking on a hard edge)
The Duke’s law is the Duke’s. It is not ours.
Do not concern your mind with such affairs.
Justice finds its own path, child. Be assured.
It can be like a river underground.
Unseen, unheard, but flowing to the sea
All the same.

HELENA
A river underground? What do you mean?
You speak in riddles, Aunt. Your eyes today…
They are not full of sorrow. They are full
Of fire. It frightens me a little.
Lord Aris too. He paces in his study,
Staring at maps and calendars, as if
He waits for some great shipment from abroad.
There is a tension in this house. A wire
Pulled tight. I fear that if I touch it, it
Will make a sound both terrible and strange.

LADY ARIS
You have a poet’s heart, Helena. Good.
Then use it to remember your lost cousin
In verse and song. But leave the prose of law
And punishment to those with harder hands.
Now, tell me of your day. Did you see friends?
Did you receive that book you waited for?
Let us speak of the living for a while.

HELENA
(Sensing the wall that has been put up)
Yes, Aunt. Of course. The book did come today.
It is a history of ancient wars.
But I find I have little taste for it.
The stories all seem far too real of late.

(Lady Aris looks away from her, back towards the roses, her face a mask of impenetrable resolve. Helena watches her, a troubled look in her eyes. The scene changes.)

(SCENE: A cheap, dreary room in an inn in Mantua. CLAUDIO lies on a narrow bed, pale and sweating. LEO is bathing his forehead with a damp cloth.)

LEO
Just rest. The fever from the road has caught you.
It happens. Bad water, or the evening chill.
A day or two of quiet in this room
And you will be yourself again.

CLAUDIO
(His voice is weak)
I feel so cold, Leo. A chill so deep
No blanket seems to reach it. My own bones
Are made of ice. And I am thirsty. So thirsty.

LEO
I will call for wine. It will warm your blood.
The innkeeper says his best is from the hills.
It will be rough, but strong.
(He goes to the door and calls out)
Ho, innkeeper! A flask of your best red wine!
And be quick about it.

(He returns to the bedside. Claudio is shivering.)

CLAUDIO
It is her curse. The one she never spoke.
Her silence. It has followed me. It found me here.
This is not road-fever, Leo. This is…
An ending.

LEO
Do not speak like that. It is despair that makes
The body weak. You are a strong man, Claudio.
You will fight this. You will see the sun again.
We have not come this far for you to fail.
Think of the new life. The one we spoke of.
Free of the past.

CLAUDIO
(A faint, bitter smile)
The past is not a coat that you can shed.
It is your skin. You wear it till you die.

(The INNKEEPER enters, a slovenly man carrying a dusty flask and two cups.)

INNKEEPER
Your wine. As ordered. Cost you three small coins.

LEO
(Taking it and handing him the money)
Here. Is it clean? My friend is ill.

INNKEEPER
As clean as any. Grapes, and feet to crush them.
What more is there to wine? If he is sick,
Perhaps some water would be better.

LEO
No. He needs the strength. Go.

(The Innkeeper shrugs and exits. Leo pours a cup of the dark red wine and helps Claudio sit up.)

LEO
Here. Drink this. Slowly now.

(Claudio takes the cup in his trembling hands and drinks.)

CLAUDIO
It has a bitter taste. A strange, sharp bite
Beneath the fruit.

LEO
It is cheap wine in a cheap inn, my friend.
We are not at the Duke’s table now.
Drink it down. Let it burn away the chill.

(Claudio drinks more, then sinks back onto the pillows, coughing.)

CLAUDIO
The burning… it is in my gut. A fire.
Leo… I cannot feel my hands.
Valerius… Tell him… no… Tell his mother…
I am sorry.

LEO
Claudio? What is it? Your eyes…
Claudio! Speak to me!

(But Claudio’s eyes are glazing over. A shudder runs through his body, and then he is still. Leo stares in horror, the cup falling from his own hand and spilling the dark wine across the floorboards.)

LEO
No. No, it cannot be. Claudio!

(He shakes his friend, but there is no response. He puts a hand to his chest, then his neck, searching for a pulse. He finds nothing. He stumbles back from the bed, his face a picture of disbelief and dawning terror. The scene changes.)

(SCENE: The great hall of the Aris estate. A month later. LORD and LADY ARIS are seated. The strain of waiting is visible on both of them. Lord Aris drums his fingers on the arm of his chair. Lady Aris is unnaturally still.)

LORD ARIS
Still no word. Silas should have returned by now.
His business in Mantua would be long since done.
Unless… unless he met with some mischance.
Or was discovered.

LADY ARIS
He will not be discovered. He is prudent.
And Cordo is a ghost. He leaves no tracks.
The delay is nothing. A slow journey,
A muddy road, a lame horse. That is all.
Patience, my lord. Our arrow, once released,
Flies to its mark. We cannot call it back,
Nor can we speed its flight. We only wait
To hear it strike.

LORD ARIS
This waiting gnaws at me. At night I dream
The plan has gone astray. The Duke has learned
Of our design. I see his guardsmen at
The door. I wake in a cold sweat.

LADY ARIS
And I sleep soundly. For the first time since
Our son was taken. I dream of a stone
Being rolled away from the black mouth of a tomb.
I feel the rightness of it. Do not fear.
Our cause is just. Our vengeance will be served.

(HELENA enters the hall. She looks from one to the other.)

HELENA
Good morning, Aunt. Uncle. You are both
Awake so early. Is there any news?
You seem… expectant.

LORD ARIS
News? No, child. What news would there be?
We are merely… enjoying the morning quiet.

HELENA
I see. It is just that you have both had
That same look for a month. As if you listen
For a footstep that has not yet arrived.

(Before anyone can answer, a SERVANT enters.)

SERVANT
My lord, a messenger has just arrived.
He bears a letter. He says it comes
From Mantua.

(Lord Aris and Lady Aris exchange a single, charged look. Lord Aris schools his features into a mask of neutrality.)

LORD ARIS
From Mantua? I have no business there.
Send him in.

(The SERVANT bows and exits, returning a moment later with a formal MESSENGER, who bows low.)

MESSENGER
My Lord Aris. I bring a formal notice
From the Mantuan authorities. A matter
Of some delicacy.

(He produces a letter with a black seal. Lord Aris takes it. His hand is perfectly steady as he breaks the seal and unrolls the parchment. He reads. Lady Aris watches his face, not the letter. Helena watches them both.)

LORD ARIS
(After a long moment, he looks up, his face impassive)
I see. You may go. You will be given food
And rest before your journey back.

MESSENGER
My lord is gracious.

(The Messenger bows and exits with the servant.)

HELENA
Uncle? What is it? The seal was black.
That means a death. Who do we know in Mantua?

LORD ARIS
(He folds the letter carefully)
It is a formal notice. It concerns the man,
Claudio. The man who killed Valerius.
It seems his exile has been cut quite short.

LADY ARIS
(Her voice is a low, steady whisper)
He is dead?

LORD ARIS
He is. According to this document,
He was taken by a sudden, wasting fever
Not long after arriving in the city.
He died in an inn, attended by his friend.
The city physicians pronounced it a
Malignant humor, brought on by the grief
Of his disgrace and the rigors of his travel.
They offer their condolences. How very
Civil of them.

(A deep silence fills the hall. Lady Aris closes her eyes for a moment. A single tear escapes and runs down her cheek, but her expression is one of profound, terrible relief.)

LADY ARIS
The debt is paid. Valerius, my son.
Your spirit may find rest. The world is clean again.

LORD ARIS
(He moves to the fireplace)
This letter must be burned. No trace must remain.
The matter is now closed. It is an act of God.
A sad but fitting end. That is the tale
We will repeat, should anyone inquire.

(He drops the letter into the flames. They watch it burn.)

HELENA
(Her voice is small, full of a new, dawning horror as she looks at their cold, satisfied faces)
A fever? So swift a fever, to take a man
So young and strong? How very… unfortunate.
