Enter CASSIAN and LEANDER.

LEANDER
Nay, do but hear me. Thy choler is a cloud that rains on thee alone. To fret at such a slight is to give the man a victory he did not win.

CASSIAN
A victory? He looked upon me, Leander, with an eye that called me peasant. His lip did curl so, I saw my own name written there in scorn. Thou wouldst have me swallow such an insult? I would sooner swallow poison.

LEANDER
And so thou dost, for what is anger but a poison of the mind? Why, thou art a man to pick a fight with thine own shadow for aping thee too closely. Thou wouldst challenge the south wind for blowing thy hat from thy head. Thy heart is a tinderbox, and the whole world a flint and steel. Hast thou not heard? The Lady Oriana is to be seen this day. A sight of her would cool thy fever.

CASSIAN
She is the fever. To think she must endure that family’s company.

Enter SIR VALERIUS and ORIANA.

LEANDER
Speak of the devil, and he shall appear to collect his due.

SIR VALERIUS
Well met, young sirs. I had not thought to find such company in this honest square. One of you, I think, hath business with me.

CASSIAN
And if I have, I choose my own time and place to press it.

SIR VALERIUS
What, a coward’s heart wrapped in a courtier’s tongue? I say you have a thing that is mine, and I will have it back.

CASSIAN
Sir, you are old, and the years have perhaps made your memory a sieve. I have nothing of yours but my contempt, which you are welcome to.

SIR VALERIUS
You have my name’s honour, which you drag through the mire by standing near this lady. She was promised to my house, a bond sealed and sworn. You, sir, are a thief of oaths.

ORIANA
Father, I pray you! My lord Cassian, do not heed him.

CASSIAN
He calls me thief? Now by my soul, the lie shall be crammed back down his throat with steel! Draw, you old winter branch, and see if I cannot prune you!

LEANDER
Cassian, hold! For the love of God, this is a public place! The Prince’s edict is fresh in every ear. Let us withdraw and speak of this in private.

SIR VALERIUS
No, let the sun bear witness. Let all men see how a peacock may be plucked of his fine feathers.

CASSIAN
I’ll pluck your heart out first!
[They draw their swords.]

ORIANA
Help, someone! They will kill each other!

LEANDER
Gentlemen, put up your blades! This is but madness!
[Leander steps between them to beat their weapons down.]

CASSIAN
Stand aside, Leander! This is not thy quarrel!

SIR VALERIUS
Out of my way, meddler!
[Sir Valerius lunges at Cassian. Leander catches the thrust meant for his friend. He stumbles back.]

LEANDER
I am hit. A pox on both your houses and the pride that fires them. I am sped. Cassian... a foolishness... a great foolishness.
[He falls.]

ORIANA
O heavens, Leander!

CASSIAN
Leander! O, what has thou done, you dog? You have slain a man whose life was worth ten of your own withered line! His blood cries out for vengeance, and it shall not cry in vain!

SIR VALERIUS
It was his own folly brought him to it.

CASSIAN
And this shall bring thee to thy grave!
[They fight fiercely. Cassian disarms Sir Valerius and runs him through.]

SIR VALERIUS
Unholy boy... thy rage... will be thy ruin...
[He dies.]

CASSIAN
[Stands, aghast, looking from Leander to Valerius.]
What is this? Two men lie dead? No, one is my friend... and the other... O, bloody, bloody day! The sun itself doth hide its face in shame. I have become the very butcher that my rage condemned.

ORIANA
[Weeping over Leander.]
He is gone, Cassian. Gentle Leander is gone.

CASSIAN
And I am lost. Fortune hath made a monster of me. Flee, Oriana, go! The watch will be here soon. My life is forfeit. My very soul is forfeit for this day’s work.
[He kneels by Leander’s body as the sound of running feet and shouts grows nearer.]

[The sound of a watchman’s horn is heard in the distance. The shouts grow more distinct.]

ORIANA
Flee where, my lord? To what dark corner of the world can you run where this deed will not follow? Your shadow will be stained with blood, your sleep a congress of dead men’s ghosts.

CASSIAN
Let them haunt me! Better my own damnation than to see you swept up in this ruin. My name is now a curse, my presence poison. Go, sweet Oriana, before the law’s cold iron clasps us both.

ORIANA
No! I will not leave you. What you have done was born of madness, a fever of the blood. But to abandon you to the wolves of justice, that would be a cold and calculated sin. I will stay. I will speak for you.

CASSIAN
Speak? What can you say? That I am a murderer? That my rage, like a blind dog, has savaged both my friend and foe? The truth is a blade that points only at my heart.

[Enter CAPTAIN FLAVIO of the City Watch, with four armed WATCHMEN carrying torches that cast flickering, monstrous shadows.]

CAPTAIN FLAVIO
In the Prince’s name, hold! What devil’s work is this? Two noblemen slain in the city square! Lay down your sword, my lord. The peace is broken, and blood cries out for judgment.

CASSIAN
[Lets his sword clatter to the stones. He does not even look at the Captain, his eyes fixed on Leander’s still form.]
I am unarmed. My only weapon now is grief, and it is turned against myself.

ORIANA
Good Captain, I beseech you, hear me. This was no foul murder plotted in the dark. It was a clash of honour, a sudden, tragic fire. Sir Valerius, my father, gave the challenge.

CAPTAIN FLAVIO
Lady, your father lies there with a hole in his breast. The law does not ask who spoke the first rash word, only who dealt the last fatal blow. My lord Cassian, you are my prisoner.

CASSIAN
I am a prisoner already, Captain. Chained to this moment for all eternity. Lead on.

WATCHMAN ONE
What of the lady, Captain?

CAPTAIN FLAVIO
She is a witness. And the daughter of the slain. Treat her with all respect, but she must come with us to the palace. The Duke will hear this matter before the sun is an hour older.

ORIANA
[Moving to Cassian’s side as the Watchmen bind his hands.]
My lord, be strong. There is a truth that these men’s eyes cannot see. I will make the Duke see it.

CASSIAN
[His voice is a hollow whisper.]
Do not defend me, Oriana. Defend your own good name from being linked with mine. This is the only service you can do me now. Forget you ever saw me.

ORIANA
Forget you? I would sooner teach the sea to forget the moon. My heart is bound to yours, for triumph or for ruin.

CAPTAIN FLAVIO
Enough. The Duke awaits. Take up the bodies. This square has been a stage for tragedy; let us clear it now for the sad epilogue of law.

[The WATCHMEN take up the bodies of Leander and Sir Valerius. Cassian is led away in chains. Oriana follows, her head held high, though her face is a mask of sorrow. The stage is left empty and silent.]

[SCENE II. A Hall of Judgment in the Duke’s Palace. DUKE LORENZO sits upon a raised chair. He is a man of sixty, severe and weary. CAPTAIN FLAVIO stands to one side. Cassian stands in chains before the Duke, guarded. Oriana stands apart, pale but composed.]

DUKE LORENZO
Lord Cassian. It is a bitter office to see a man of your high birth and once bright promise stand before me in this wretched state. My edict was proclaimed through every street and square. No man, on pain of death, should draw his sword in private quarrel. Did you think this law a petty thing? A scarecrow for the common folk, while nobles such as you might brawl and kill like rutting stags?

CASSIAN
Your grace, I thought of nothing but the insult to my name. A rage fell on me, a red mist, and in it, reason drowned. I do not seek to pardon my own fury. It is a beast that I have fed too long, and now it has devoured all I hold dear.

DUKE LORENZO
It has devoured more than that. It has devoured Sir Valerius, a councillor to this state. And worse, far worse, it has slain Leander, a man whose gentle temper was a balm to this hot-headed city. His only crime was to love peace more than pride.

CASSIAN
And for that crime, I stand condemned. Not by your law, your grace, but by my own soul. The death of Sir Valerius was the act of a heated enemy. But Leander... Leander was my friend. His blood is on my hands, a stain that will not wash away. For that, I ask no mercy. I deserve none.

DUKE LORENZO
Your confession does you some small credit, though it cannot bring the dead to life. We have heard the Captain’s report. Now, we must hear the witness. Lady Oriana. Step forward.

[Oriana moves to the centre of the hall. She does not look at Cassian.]

DUKE LORENZO
Daughter of the slain. A terrible duty falls to you. Yet you must speak, and speak the truth. Tell us what you saw. Let your truth be the scale in which this man’s life is weighed.

ORIANA
Your grace, I am my father’s daughter, and I mourn him. But I am also a servant of the truth, and I will not betray it, even for a murdered sire. My father sought this quarrel. He came to the square with anger in his heart and insults on his tongue. He called Lord Cassian a coward and a thief.

DUKE LORENZO
And did Lord Cassian not answer insult with insult, and threat with threat?

ORIANA
He did, your grace. The pride of men is a tinderbox, and my father struck the spark. They drew their swords, two lions in their rage. It was then that Leander, noble Leander, stepped between them. He sought only to make peace, to remind them of your law, to save them from themselves.

[Her voice breaks for a moment, and she pauses to compose herself.]

ORIANA
My father, blinded by his fury, lunged for Cassian. Leander, in his love for his friend, took the blow. He caught the point that was not meant for him. He fell, a sacrifice to a pointless feud.

DUKE LORENZO
So Leander’s death was at your father’s hand?

ORIANA
It was his steel, your grace. But it was their shared rage that drove the point home. When Cassian saw his friend fall, a madness seized him. What he did next was not a duelist’s work, but a man avenging the kindest soul he knew. He fought not for himself, but for the ghost of Leander. He was a storm my father’s wind did raise.

DUKE LORENZO
[Strokes his beard, deep in thought.]
A storm you say. A storm that leaves two good men dead and the Prince’s law in tatters. You speak with a lawyer’s skill, my lady, turning murder into a tragedy of errors. But the fact remains. Two men are dead. The law is broken. Justice must be served, or my authority is but a puff of wind. Lord Cassian, your life is forfeit by the letter of my law.

CASSIAN
I am ready to pay the price.

DUKE LORENZO
Yet… the lady’s testimony gives me pause. You did not strike the first true blow. Your friend was slain by your opponent’s steel, however accidental. The rage that followed, while unlawful, has a human root. To kill you would be simple, but justice is not always simple. It must be a remedy, not merely a revenge. Therefore, I will not have your head.

[A flicker of hope in Oriana’s eyes.]

DUKE LORENZO
Death is too quick a release for such a crime. You have shattered the peace of my city. You have robbed it of two of its sons. Your punishment, then, shall be a living death. You are hereby banished from this city and all its territories. You will leave before the sun sets today. If you are ever found again within these walls, on any pretext, at any time, you shall be taken and executed on the spot, without trial or appeal. This is my judgment.

CASSIAN
Banishment? You call that mercy? To live, yet be from Oriana torn? To walk the earth, knowing she is here, breathing this air, and I cannot? This is a torment worse than any death. It is the rack, the fire, the wheel, all in a single word.

DUKE LORENZO
Then you shall learn what you have thrown away. You prized your fleeting honour above the steady comforts of this life. Now you shall have a lifetime to contemplate your honour in some foreign ditch, while others enjoy the life you squandered. Captain, take him to the city gates. Give him his horse, his sword, and a purse of fifty crowns. Then see him gone. The sentence is pronounced.

CASSIAN
[Looks at Oriana, a world of despair in his eyes.]
Oriana.

ORIANA
[Takes a step toward him, her hand outstretched.]
Cassian.

DUKE LORENZO
No. There will be no farewells. The bond between you is broken, severed by your own bloody hand. He is a dead man to this city. Let him depart like a ghost at cockcrow.

[The GUARDS take hold of Cassian and pull him toward the exit.]

CASSIAN
A ghost I am then. A ghost who will haunt the lonely roads and dream of what is lost. Farewell, your grace. And may you never know the hell you have created here today. Farewell, my love. My only love.

[He is dragged from the hall. Oriana watches him go, her face a stark, white stone. She stands alone before the Duke.]

DUKE LORENZO
Lady Oriana. You are now my ward. Your father’s house and lands fall under my protection until a suitable marriage can be arranged to secure them. You will be treated with the honour due your station. Return to your home. Mourn your father. And forget this banished man. The city must have peace.

[The Duke rises and exits, leaving Oriana standing alone in the vast, empty hall. She slowly sinks to her knees, not in a faint, but as if the weight of her grief is too great to bear standing.]

[SCENE III. A Dungeon Cell. It is dark and damp. CASSIAN is alone, his chains removed. He paces the small space like a caged animal.]

CASSIAN
Banished. The word is like a bell that tolls my own damnation. To be alive and not to live. To breathe, and yet be breathless. O sun, why do you trouble to climb the sky and light a world I can no longer call my own? And you, Leander, my gentle friend, your ghost doth haunt this cell more than these stones. You stand in every shadow, your kind eyes filled with pity and reproach. It was my pride that killed you. My own hot, foolish pride. I saw the world as a great stage for my own valour, and did not see the friends who held the scenery up. Now the stage is broke, the lights are out, and I am left to stumble in the dark.

[The cell door creaks open. A plain, loyal-looking SERVANT enters, carrying a small bundle. This is PEDRO.]

PEDRO
My lord Cassian.

CASSIAN
Pedro? How did you gain this place? Do you come to watch the monster in his cage before they drive him out into the wild?

PEDRO
I come to serve you, my lord, as I have always done. I bribed the gaoler with what little coin I had. He gives us but a moment. I have brought your riding cloak, and a flask of wine, and bread. The road you travel will be a cold companion.

CASSIAN
[Takes the flask and drinks deeply.]
You are a good man, Pedro. A better friend than I deserve. What do they say of me in the city? Do the gossips and the merchants curse my name?

PEDRO
Some do, my lord. They call you the Bloody Lord. But others speak of the provocation. They say Sir Valerius was ever a man of sour and jealous temper. And all men, everywhere, mourn Leander. His name is spoken like a prayer for peace.

CASSIAN
As it should be. And the lady Oriana? Have you heard word of her? Does she keep her rooms, drowned in her sorrow?

PEDRO
She does, my lord. They say she sees no one. The Duke has placed his own guard upon her house. She is a prisoner in all but name, a golden bird in a ducal cage.

CASSIAN
[Takes a ring from his finger.]
A cage. We are both prisoners then, she of his law and I of his sentence. Pedro, you must do one last thing for me. This is the last and dearest service you can render. Find a way to see her. Bribe a maid, climb a wall, I care not how. Give her this ring. Tell her my heart is locked within its band. Tell her that though my body is banished, my soul remains with her, a silent watcher in her halls. Tell her… tell her to live, and be happy, and forget the wretch who brought such ruin on her house.

PEDRO
To live and forget you, my lord? That is a cruel message to deliver.

CASSIAN
It is a necessary one. Her life must not be shackled to my dead hope. She must be free. Now go, Pedro, before the guards return. And take my thanks. You are the last piece of my old life, and I thank you for your loyalty.

PEDRO
God keep you, my lord, on whatever path you tread.

[Pedro takes the ring and exits. The door clangs shut. Cassian is alone again. He sinks onto a stone bench and buries his face in his hands.]

[SCENE IV. A Chamber in Oriana’s House. The room is richly appointed but feels stifling. ORIANA sits by a window, looking out. Her lady-in-waiting, BEATRICE, a sharp-eyed woman of thirty, is sewing nearby.]

BEATRICE
You have not eaten all day, my lady. You will waste away to nothing but a shadow. Grief is a glutton, but you must not feed it with your own health.

ORIANA
I have no appetite, Beatrice. The world has turned to ash in my mouth. My father is dead. Leander is dead. And Cassian… Cassian is worse than dead. He is a name I am forbidden to speak, a memory I am commanded to erase.

BEATRICE
The Duke is a hard man, but perhaps a wise one. A clean break is less painful than a wound that never heals.

ORIANA
You speak of it as if it were a childish fancy. A ribbon I must discard. Beatrice, I love him. That is not a thing to be commanded or erased. It is the very root of me. To pull it out would be to kill the plant entire.

BEATRICE
But my lady, he killed your father. How can your heart hold both these things at once? How can it mourn the dead and love the hand that slew him? It is a civil war within a single breast.

ORIANA
I do not know how. I only know it does. My father’s death was a horror. I see it when I close my eyes. But I also see the goad he used, the relentless pride that drove him to that fight. My love has been a poison in two veins, his and Cassian’s. It has brought forth only death. Perhaps the Duke is right. Perhaps I am a curse upon the men who look at me.

BEATRICE
Now you speak madness. You are a victim in this, not the cause. You are the prize they fought for, and in their foolishness, they smashed the trophy. But you are not smashed, my lady. You are still whole.

ORIANA
Am I? I feel like a city that has been sacked. The walls are standing, but the treasury is looted and the people slain. What is left of me to be whole?

[A serving maid enters quietly.]

SERVING MAID
My lady, a man is here. He says his name is Pedro, and he was Lord Cassian’s servant. He begs a moment of your time. He says he brings a token.

ORIANA
[Rising to her feet, her lethargy gone.]
Pedro? From Cassian? Let him in at once! At once!

BEATRICE
My lady, the Duke’s command…

ORIANA
The Duke may command my house, but not my heart. Let him in, I say!

[The maid scurries out and returns with PEDRO. He bows low.]

PEDRO
My lady Oriana. Forgive this intrusion. My master, before he left the city’s bounds, gave me a charge I could not fail to keep.

ORIANA
Speak, man. What news? Is he safe? Where has he gone?

PEDRO
He rode north, my lady. Toward the mountains. His face was set like flint. He gave me this for you.

[He holds out the ring. Oriana takes it as if it were a holy relic. She slips it on her finger.]

PEDRO
He bid me tell you that his heart is locked within its band. He said his soul remains with you. And he… he bid me tell you to live, and be happy, and forget him.

ORIANA
[A short, sharp laugh, devoid of humour.]
Forget him. Did he truly think I could? The fool. The noble, idiotic fool. To ask the flower to forget the sun.

BEATRICE
It is a kind farewell, my lady. He seeks to set you free.

ORIANA
He seeks to build my prison walls higher. To have me live a quiet life of ease while he wanders in the wilderness? To marry some dull lord the Duke chooses for me, and grow old and fat with contentment while his heart is breaking on a lonely road? No. That is not freedom. That is the most bitter bondage of all.

[She begins to pace the room, a new fire in her eyes.]

ORIANA
Beatrice, the Duke believes this is the end. He has passed his sentence, closed his book, and thinks the story’s over. But he has written only the first chapter.

BEATRICE
What do you mean, my lady? What can you do? You are a woman, and his ward.

ORIANA
I am my father’s daughter. I have his lands, his name, and if I do not have his temper, I have his will. A Duke’s decree is not the word of God. It is the breath of a man, and it can be changed. By persuasion, or by pressure, or by stealth. This banishment shall be a brittle cage. I’ll find the key, or break the bars myself.

PEDRO
My lady?

ORIANA
Pedro. Your master thinks himself a ghost. But ghosts can find their way home. You will not return to your own life just yet. You will work for me now. You will be my eyes and ears. I must know where your master goes, how he fares. And you, Beatrice, will help me. We will play the part of grieving daughter and loyal servant. But in the quiet of these rooms, we will plot a new act for this play. One the Duke has not foreseen.

BEATRICE
It is dangerous, my lady. Treasonously so.

ORIANA
And what is life without a little danger, Beatrice? It is a long, slow walk to the grave. I have had enough of death. I choose to fight for life. For his, and for my own. Now leave me. I must think.

[Beatrice and Pedro bow and exit, leaving Oriana alone. She stands in the center of the room, looking at the ring on her finger. The last light of the setting sun streams through the window, catching the gold of the ring and illuminating her face, now filled not with grief, but with fierce and dangerous resolve.]

ACT II

SCENE I

Oriana’s chambers. A week has passed. Night. The room is lit by a dozen candles, casting long, dancing shadows. A large map is spread across a low table, weighted at the corners with books. Oriana, dressed in a simple, dark gown, kneels before it, tracing a route with her finger. Beatrice stands near the window, a nervous sentinel. Pedro stands by the door, his posture stiff with attention.

ORIANA
The northern road is the most direct. He would have taken it to make for the coast. If he seeks passage on a ship, he will go to Porto Vellis.

PEDRO
He would, my lady. But the Duke’s men will watch the port. They will expect it. Lorenzo is no fool. He would know they would look for him there. He will avoid the ports. He will go east, over the Dragon’s Tooth mountains.

ORIANA
The mountains? In this season? The passes will be choked with snow. It is a death sentence.

PEDRO
It is a sentence of freedom, my lady. No one would follow him there. He values his life, but he values his liberty more. He would risk the cold to escape the Duke’s long shadow. He spoke of it once, of wanting to see the Sunken Kingdoms of the east. He will go that way. I am sure of it.

ORIANA
[She moves her finger across the map, over the jagged lines representing the mountains.]
Then you must go that way too. But not behind him. You will go ahead. Use the southern pass, it is longer but safer. You will arrive at the town of Silver Creek before he does. You will secure lodging, food. You will be a friendly face in a wilderness of strangers.

BEATRICE
My lady, this is madness. To send Pedro into the mountains alone… and for what? To watch? To wait? What can one man do?

ORIANA
One man can carry a purse of gold. One man can deliver a message. One man can be a friend when all others have been stripped away. Lorenzo travels with nothing but the clothes on his back and the Duke’s curse on his name. We will give him a lifeline, Beatrice. A thin, silken thread he can follow back home.

PEDRO
I will need a good horse. And a letter of passage. The Duke’s patrols question everyone on the roads.

ORIANA
I have thought of that.
[She rises and goes to a heavy wooden chest. She lifts the lid, revealing several bags of coin and a rolled parchment tied with a ribbon.]
This is gold from my mother’s inheritance. It is mine, to do with as I please. It will buy you the best horse in the city and passage through any gate. The parchment is a commission. It states that you travel on my authority to inspect my family’s estates in the north. It bears my father’s seal. No one will question it.

[Pedro takes the bags and the parchment, his eyes wide.]

PEDRO
My lady… this is a fortune.

ORIANA
It is nothing. It is dross. Spend it freely. Your only duty is to find him. Do not let him see you at first. Just watch. Learn his habits, his path. Send word back to me through the merchant caravans that pass through Silver Creek. You know the code we discussed?

PEDRO
The vintage is poor this year. The crop is failing. That means he is unwell, or in danger. The harvest is bountiful. That means he is safe and making progress.

ORIANA
Good. Beatrice will be your point of contact here. She will receive the messages.

BEATRICE
I am a lady’s maid, not a spymaster. My hands tremble just hearing this. If we are caught…

ORIANA
[She turns to Beatrice, her voice softening but losing none of its intensity.]
Then we are caught. But I would rather be caught trying to mend a great wrong than live a long life accepting it. Do you think I do not feel fear, Beatrice? It is a cold knot in my stomach every moment I am awake. But I will not be ruled by it. Lorenzo is a good man. A noble man. He was cast out for the crime of loving me. I will not stand by and watch that injustice curdle into a tragedy. Will you?

BEATRICE
[After a long pause, she shakes her head, her expression firming.]
No, my lady. I will not.

ORIANA
Then we are a fellowship of three against a duchy. The odds are not in our favour. That will make our victory all the sweeter. Pedro, you leave at dawn. Go to the stables now. Choose your horse. Say you are running an errand for me. No one will think twice.

PEDRO
Yes, my lady. I will not fail you. And I will not fail him.
[He bows deeply, first to Oriana, then a respectful nod to Beatrice, and exits.]

BEATRICE
Godspeed go with him. He will need it. And so will we. The Duke sent word. He wishes to see you tomorrow.

ORIANA
[She turns from the map, a cold calm settling on her face.]
Does he. Then the next act begins sooner than I thought. He will expect to see a wilting flower, drenched in the dew of her own tears.

BEATRICE
And what will he see?

ORIANA
He will see what he expects. I will be the perfect picture of feminine grief. Pliant, sorrowful, obedient. I will play my part so well he will believe he wrote the lines himself. But the stage is mine now, not his. He just does not know it yet.

SCENE II

The Duke’s study. A grand, severe room lined with shelves of leather bound books. A fire burns low in a vast stone fireplace. The DUKE stands before it, warming his hands. He is a man in his fifties, grey at the temples, with an air of absolute authority. CAPTAIN VALERIO, a hard faced man in the uniform of the Duke’s Guard, stands at attention in the center of the room.

DUKE
You are certain he is gone? No lingering in the villages outside the city walls? No last minute appeals to foolish friends?

VALERIO
He is gone, Your Grace. My men watched him until he was a speck on the northern road. He did not look back. He carries only a small pack. He is truly banished. The city is clean of him.

DUKE
Good. A fever must be purged completely, Captain. Lorenzo was a fever in the mind of my ward. His ambition was a sickness in my court. I am not a cruel man. I could have had his head. Banishment is a mercy.

VALERIO
A mercy, Your Grace.

DUKE
See that the ports are watched. For a month. If he is foolish enough to show his face, arrest him. He has disobeyed a direct edict. That would be treason. And treason has a simple cure.

VALERIO
It will be done. And what of the Lady Oriana? She has not left her rooms. Her servants say she takes no food.

DUKE
She grieves. It is the way of women. Their hearts are ships without rudders, tossed about by the storms of emotion. She will weep for a time, and then she will forget. It is my duty to provide her with a new harbour. A safe one.

VALERIO
Your Grace?

DUKE
I have been in correspondence with the Count of Alva. His son, Felipe, is a man of good standing. Strong. Loyal. Utterly devoid of poetry. He will make a suitable husband. He will anchor her, give her children, and turn her mind from this youthful folly to the duties of a wife and mother.

VALERIO
An excellent match, Your Grace. The alliance would strengthen your northern border.

DUKE
Indeed. Politics, as in all things, must be served. Her father would have approved. He knew the necessity of a strong match over a fanciful one. I will speak with her tomorrow. I will give her the news. It will give her a new purpose, something to distract her from her sorrow.

VALERIO
She may resist. She had a… strong attachment to the young man.

DUKE
She will posture. She will weep. She will protest her undying love. And then she will do as she is told. She is a good girl, at heart. She is merely lost. I am her guardian, Valerio. It is my charge to find her path for her. I show her this path not as a tyrant, but as a loving father. She will see that, in time. Now go. Double the watch on the palace gates for a few weeks. Grief can make people unpredictable. I want no more romantic nonsense.

VALERIO
Yes, Your Grace.
[Valerio bows and exits. The Duke turns and picks up a small, painted portrait from his desk. It is of a young, smiling Oriana.]

DUKE
Oh, my child. You think this is the end of your happiness. But I am saving you from the one thing more dangerous than a serpent’s venom. A poet’s heart. You will thank me one day.

SCENE III

A dark corner of the palace gardens, near the stables. The air is cold. The moon is a sliver behind fast moving clouds. Pedro, now dressed in sturdy travelling clothes with a heavy cloak, is checking the saddle on a strong black horse. Beatrice emerges from the shadows, clutching a small, cloth wrapped bundle.

BEATRICE
I thought I would miss you.

PEDRO
The horse needed a final check. The night is long, and the road longer.

BEATRICE
[She holds out the bundle.]
Here. Bread and cheese. And a flask of wine. It is not much, but the kitchens were closed.

PEDRO
[He takes it, a rare smile touching his lips.]
It is more than enough. Thank you, Beatrice.

BEATRICE
Be careful, Pedro. The world outside these walls is not a kind place. Especially for a man alone.

PEDRO
I have my master’s luck. It has always been bad, so it can only get better.

BEATRICE
Do not joke. I am afraid. For you, for him. For my lady. We are playing with fire. The Duke is not a man to be trifled with.

PEDRO
The Lady Oriana is not a woman to be trifled with either. I have served Lorenzo since we were boys. I have never seen him so… broken, as he was when he gave me that ring. He believes he has lost everything. But our lady is trying to build him a bridge back from despair. I will be the first stone in that bridge. I am not afraid of the Duke. I am only afraid of failing them.

BEATRICE
You are a good man, Pedro.

PEDRO
I am a loyal one. It is all a servant has. Tell her… tell her my journey has begun. And that the first star of the evening is bright. It will be our sign. The mission has started well.

BEATRICE
I will tell her. May your horse be swift and your path safe.

[A sudden noise. The clang of a gate latch nearby. Both of them freeze, melting back into the deeper shadows behind a stone pillar. Two guards walk past, their halberds resting on their shoulders, their voices low and grumbling.]

FIRST GUARD
...cold enough to freeze the devil himself tonight. What I would give for a warm fire and a mug of ale.

SECOND GUARD
You will get a cold stone wall and a long watch, and you will be grateful for it. The Captain was in a foul mood today. Wants the patrols doubled.

FIRST GUARD
Why? The city is quiet as a tomb.

SECOND GUARD
Something about the banished boy. The Duke fears he might have friends. Best be on our toes.

[The guards continue on their way, their footsteps fading into the night. Pedro and Beatrice wait a long moment, hearts pounding, before stepping out again.]

PEDRO
You see? The Duke’s web is already tightening. I must go. Now.

BEATRICE
They mentioned the Captain. I saw him leaving the Duke’s study earlier. They are planning something.

PEDRO
Then you must be our ears. Listen at every door. Watch every face. My part is on the road. Yours is just as vital, here in the lion’s den.

BEATRICE
I will.
[He mounts the horse in one fluid motion. He looks down at her, his face serious in the moonlight.]

PEDRO
Do not lose hope, Beatrice.

BEATRICE
I will not. Go.
[With a final nod, Pedro turns the horse and, moving at a silent walk, guides it out of the gardens and onto a dark, unused path that leads toward a small postern gate. Beatrice watches until he is swallowed by the darkness, clutching her shawl tightly around her.]

SCENE IV

Oriana’s chambers. The following day. The heavy curtains are drawn, plunging the room into a gloomy twilight. Oriana is seated in a high backed chair, wearing a severe black gown. She looks pale and listless. There is a knock at the door.

BEATRICE
(from outside)
My lady. His Grace the Duke is here to see you.

ORIANA
[Her voice is quiet, weary.]
Let him enter.

[The door opens. The DUKE enters, his expression one of paternal concern. Beatrice slips in behind him and stands silently near the door. The Duke approaches Oriana.]

DUKE
My dear child. You cannot stay in the dark forever. Grief is a houseguest, not a permanent resident. You must show it the door eventually.

ORIANA
You speak as if grief is a choice. My heart is not a room I can simply tidy when I wish. You have taken its finest furnishing. The room is now bare.

DUKE
Such poetry. It was always his influence. A pretty turn of phrase cannot mend a broken fence or govern a province, Oriana. Life requires prose. Solid, dependable, and clear. I have come to speak to you of your future.

ORIANA
I have no future. Only a past. A past that ended two days ago on the northern road.

DUKE
Nonsense. Your life is just beginning. You are the daughter of a great man, the inheritor of great lands and a great name. You have duties. Responsibilities. To your people, and to your station.

ORIANA
What duty could you possibly ask of me now? Shall I sit and do my needlework? Shall I read cautionary tales? What is there for me?

DUKE
A marriage.
[Oriana looks up at him. Her expression is perfectly blank, but her hands clench in her lap.]
It is time you had a husband. A strong man to guide and protect you. I have spent many months considering the matter, and I believe I have found the perfect match. Felipe, the son of the Count of Alva.

ORIANA
[A small, bitter laugh escapes her.]
Alva’s son. I have met him. He has the soul of a tax collector and the wit of a plough horse.

DUKE
He has the largest estates on our northern border. He is a renowned soldier and a dutiful son. His heart is not filled with foolish songs and idle dreams. He will be a good, steady husband. He will not lead you down a path to ruin and disgrace. The arrangement is most advantageous.

ORIANA
An arrangement. You speak of my life as if it were a trade agreement. So many bushels of my happiness in exchange for a stronger border.

DUKE
I speak of securing your place in the world. I am your guardian. It is my right and my responsibility. This is not a request, Oriana. It is a decision. You will marry him.

ORIANA
[She rises slowly, her eyes, though seemingly full of sorrow, have a glint of steel deep within them. She moves to the window and pulls back a curtain, letting a sliver of grey light into the room.]
And when is this… transaction to take place? Am I to be measured for a wedding gown tomorrow?

DUKE
Do not be dramatic. I am not without compassion. You are in mourning. For the memory of a foolish boy, but in mourning nonetheless. You shall have a season. Three months. Grieve for what you believe you have lost. When the spring comes, you will put aside these black clothes and your blacker moods. You will meet with Felipe, and you will consent. The wedding will be in the summer.

ORIANA
Three months. To bury my heart and prepare for a life sentence. You are most generous, Your Grace.

DUKE
I am most patient. But my patience has its limits. This is for your own good, Oriana. One day, when you are holding your firstborn son, you will understand. You will look back on this as a fever dream. Now, I will leave you to your thoughts. I expect to see you at court in a month’s time. Your period of deep mourning will be over then.

[He turns and walks to the door. He pauses, his hand on the latch.]

DUKE
Try to eat something, child. There is no victory in starving yourself.
[He exits. Beatrice rushes to Oriana’s side.]

BEATRICE
My lady! A marriage! What can we do? Three months is no time at all!

ORIANA
[She turns from the window, and the mask of grief is gone. Her face is alive with fierce calculation. A small, dangerous smile plays on her lips.]
Three months. Beatrice, he thinks he has given me a deadline. A cage that will slowly close. The fool. He has given me a gift.

BEATRICE
A gift? My lady, it is a death knell!

ORIANA
No. It is a weapon. It is time. Three months is a lifetime. In three months, an army can march across a continent. A king can be overthrown. A banished man can find his way home. The Duke thinks he is closing his book. But Pedro is on the road. We have gold. We have a plan. And now, we have a clock. And it is ticking in our favour, not his. Summer is coming, Beatrice. And there will be a wedding. But the groom will not be Felipe of Alva.

BEATRICE
A weapon? My lady, your hands are empty! We are two women in a locked room. His guards are at the door.

ORIANA
The most dangerous weapons are not forged in steel, Beatrice. They are forged up here.
[She taps her temple with a slender finger.]
He sees a grieving girl. He expects tears and sighs and a slow, wilting acceptance. We will give him that. We will give his court a perfect portrait of tragic beauty. But behind the curtain, we will sharpen our knives.

BEATRICE
But what is the plan? Pedro is half a world away, with a price on his head.

ORIANA
Half a world is just a journey. A price is just a number. First, we must get word to him. Not our usual channels. They will be watched now. The Duke is patient, but he is not a fool. He will be watching for a sign of rebellion. We must be cleverer.

BEATRICE
How? Every letter is opened. Every messenger is questioned.

ORIANA
[A small, secret smile returns to her lips.]
Go to the library. On the topmost shelf, behind the dreary collection of ‘Histories of Taxation’, there is a small, leather-bound volume. ‘Sonnets of a Forgotten Poet’. The cover is plain, dark green. Do you know the one I mean?

BEATRICE
I think so. It is always covered in dust. No one has touched it in years.

ORIANA
Exactly. Inside the back cover, the leather is loose. Pry it open. You will find a small, folded piece of paper and a vial of colorless ink. Bring them to me. And Beatrice…

BEATRICE
Yes, my lady?

ORIANA
Walk. Do not run. Look bored, as if you are merely fetching me something to help me sleep. Your fear is a beacon. Douse it. For me.

BEATRICE
[She takes a deep, steadying breath, her loyalty overcoming her terror.]
Yes, my lady. I will. For you.

[Beatrice curtsies and exits, leaving Oriana alone. Oriana walks to the large, ornate mirror that hangs on the wall. She stares at her reflection. She practices a look of profound sadness, letting her shoulders slump, her lip tremble. Then, in an instant, her face hardens again, her eyes flashing with resolve. She is an actress preparing for the role of a lifetime. The door opens and Beatrice returns, clutching the small book. She quickly closes the door behind her.]

BEATRICE
I have it. No one saw me. The librarian was asleep at his desk.

[Oriana takes the book and moves to her writing table. She works quickly, extracting the paper and the vial as she described. She flattens the paper. It appears to be a simple piece of sheet music.]

ORIANA
The ink is made from lemon juice. It will be invisible until it is held to a flame. The music itself is the key. Every third note, if you know the scale of the old kingdom, spells out a letter. A slow and tedious code, but unbreakable.

BEATRICE
What will you write?

ORIANA
[She dips a fine quill into the invisible ink and begins to write between the lines of music.]
I will write: ‘The cage has a date. Summer. The hawk must fly before the swallows nest.’ He will understand. I will tell him to make for the monastery at Greyfell. The abbot there still remembers my father’s kindness. He will find sanctuary. And from there, he will find our allies.

BEATRICE
Allies? My lady, who would dare stand against the Duke?

ORIANA
Not everyone loves a tyrant. The northern barons chafe under his taxes. The merchants resent his control of the ports. They are all just waiting. Waiting for a leader. For a symbol. My father’s true heir was not the Duke, it was the man I chose. The man the people loved. Pedro. The Duke knows this. That is why he was banished, not executed. A martyr is more dangerous than an exile.

[She finishes writing, her brow furrowed in concentration. She blows on the page gently.]

ORIANA
Now, how to send it? This cannot go by royal courier.

BEATRICE
Old Elspeth. The washerwoman. Her son drives a cart to the villages near the mountains twice a week. He passes not five miles from Greyfell. He is a simple boy, they will not suspect him.

ORIANA
Excellent, Beatrice. You see? You have a mind for this as well. Give Elspeth’s son a gold coin. Tell him it is for his mother’s sick sister. Tell him to deliver this ‘song’ to the abbot, a gift for their choir. And tell him that if anyone asks, it is a sad little tune I wrote myself, to pass the time.

BEATRICE
I will do it at once.

[Beatrice carefully folds the sheet music and conceals it in her sleeve. She moves towards the door.]

ORIANA
Beatrice.

BEATRICE
[She turns back.]
My lady?

ORIANA
This is the first move in a great game. The board is the entire duchy. The pieces are men and women, gold and steel. And the prize is everything. Do not be afraid.

BEATRICE
I am not afraid for myself. I am afraid for you.

ORIANA
Then you are a fool. You should be afraid for him.

[Oriana gestures vaguely in the direction of the Duke’s chambers. Her smile is gone, replaced by an expression of cold, implacable fury. Beatrice nods and exits. The scene fades to black.]

[SCENE II]

[A month has passed. The setting is a grand ballroom in the ducal palace. It is filled with lords and ladies in fine attire. Music plays softly. The mood is one of polite, restrained celebration. Oriana stands near a set of balcony doors, dressed in a gown of deep violet, a concession to the end of her formal mourning period. She holds a glass of wine she has not touched. Her expression is one of distant, elegant sorrow. She is playing her part perfectly. Lord Valois, a foppish and obsequious courtier, approaches her.]

VALOIS
My Lady Oriana. A vision of melancholy beauty. Your presence graces the court once more. His Grace is most pleased.

ORIANA
Lord Valois. You are too kind. I am merely doing my duty.

VALOIS
Nonsense. It is a joy to see you. We have all been so terribly concerned. To lose a young love… tragic. But time, as they say, is the great healer. And a new love, a more… suitable love, is the finest medicine of all, is it not? I hear Prince Felipe is a man of remarkable substance.

ORIANA
I have heard he is a man. That is all I know.

VALOIS
Oh, much more than that! A fine soldier, I am told. And so very wealthy. The mines in Alva are legendary. Your children will want for nothing.

ORIANA
[Her voice is quiet, but carries a sharp edge.]
It is a comfort to know my unborn children’s financial security is so well considered. Tell me, Lord Valois, is there any news from the coast? I hear the fishermen have had a poor season.

VALOIS
[Slightly taken aback by the change of subject.]
The coast? Oh, I shouldn't think so. The Duke’s trade policies have filled the treasury. All is well. You should not trouble your pretty head with such matters. You should be thinking of summer gowns and wedding flowers.

[The Duke approaches, his presence silencing the nearby chatter. Valois bows deeply.]

DUKE
Oriana. I am glad you came. You see? The world does not end. It merely turns.

ORIANA
Your Grace.

DUKE
Lord Valois, leave us. I wish to speak with my niece.

VALOIS
Of course, Your Grace. At once.

[Valois scurries away into the crowd.]

DUKE
He means well, but he has the subtlety of a blacksmith. You look pale. Have you been eating?

ORIANA
I have little appetite for celebration. You commanded my presence, so I am present. Is that not enough?

DUKE
It is a start. I received a letter from Felipe’s father this morning. They are most eager. They wish to send a portraitist, to capture your beauty for their court.

ORIANA
So they may see the prize before it is delivered? Very practical.

DUKE
[He sighs, a flicker of irritation crossing his face.]
It is a gesture of courtesy, Oriana. A tradition. He wishes to have your likeness. He also writes of Felipe. The boy is composing poetry for you.

ORIANA
[A bitter laugh escapes her before she can stifle it.]
Poetry? How wonderful. Perhaps he can write an ode to our trade alliance. Or a sonnet about border security.

DUKE
Enough. Your grief has curdled into venom. It does not become you. You will sit for the painter. You will receive the poems. You will act the part of a grateful and obedient bride to be. This is not just about you. This is about the stability of this entire realm. Pedro’s followers are still whispering in the taverns. There are whispers of unrest in the north. This marriage will silence them. It will show strength. Unity.

ORIANA
It will show that you have sold your only kin for a treaty.

DUKE
It will show that I have saved her from her own foolish heart. I am doing what your father would have done.

ORIANA
[Her voice drops to a fierce whisper.]
Never say that. Never compare yourself to my father. He led with love. You rule with fear.

[Before the Duke can respond, a new figure approaches. He is older, with a stern, intelligent face and the robes of a senior advisor. This is Lord Cassian.]

CASSIAN
Your Grace. Lady Oriana. I hope I do not intrude.

DUKE
[Turning, his anger masked by courtly smoothness.]
Lord Cassian. What is it?

CASSIAN
A messenger has arrived from the southern territories. He carries an urgent dispatch.

DUKE
Urgent? What is the matter? Has there been a border skirmish?

CASSIAN
I do not know the details, Your Grace. The seal is for your eyes only.

DUKE
[He glances back at Oriana.]
We will finish this conversation later. Try to look as if you are enjoying yourself. Smile. It is an order.

[The Duke turns and strides away with Lord Cassian, leaving Oriana alone again. Beatrice materializes at her side, her face pale with worry.]

BEATRICE
My lady, what did he say? You looked so… angry.

ORIANA
He is bringing a painter. To capture my face for my future husband. He wants me to smile.

[Oriana turns her head slightly, looking out over the crowded ballroom. Her eyes are not on the dancers, but seem to be looking through the walls, far beyond the palace.]

ORIANA
Let him paint me. Let Felipe see the face of the woman whose life he is so eager to purchase. Let them all see it. Let them hang it on their walls and toast to their good fortune. They are capturing a shadow. A ghost. By the time the paint is dry, the woman in the portrait will be gone.

BEATRICE
What do you mean?

ORIANA
[She finally takes a sip of her wine, her eyes glittering in the candlelight.]
The message was sent a month ago, Beatrice. An army can march a long way in a month. Allies can be gathered. Plans can be laid. Every day that he sits here, comfortable in his power, is a day we are using against him. The dispatch Cassian brought… I wonder.

BEATRICE
What do you wonder, my lady?

ORIANA
I wonder if the world outside these walls is as quiet as the Duke believes it to be. I wonder if the first tremor has been felt.

[She scans the crowd, her gaze sharp and calculating. She is no longer a grieving girl in a ballroom. She is a general, surveying the battlefield. The scene fades to black.]

[SCENE III]

[Two weeks later. The Duke’s council chamber. It is a severe, wood panelled room, dominated by a large table covered in maps and documents. The Duke stands at the head of the table. Lord Cassian and Lord Valois are present, along with a grizzled, uniformed man, General Marius.]

DUKE
Read the report again, Marius. Every word.

MARIUS
[He holds a piece of parchment.]
Your Grace. As I said. Our garrison at the Blackwater Pass was overrun three nights ago. It was not a large force, perhaps two hundred men. But they were disciplined. They moved at night, struck hard, and vanished before a proper counterattack could be mounted. They took all the weapons and supplies. Our commander, Captain Rolen, was killed.

DUKE
Killed? Or executed?

MARIUS
His body was left hanging from the gatehouse. With a white sash tied to his arm. The sash of the old royal guard. The one loyal to Oriana’s father.

VALOIS
Insolence! It must be bandits. Deserters who have grown bold.

MARIUS
Bandits do not move with such precision, my lord. They do not target a strategic pass and melt away into the mountains. This was a military operation. Small, but professional.

DUKE
[He paces the room, his hands clasped behind his back.]
And the dispatch from the south? The one you brought me at the ball, Cassian?

CASSIAN
It was a report from a magistrate in the port city of Seraphina, Your Grace. He writes of a sudden influx of coin. Old currency, mostly. Gold Imperials from the former king’s treasury. It is being used to buy blades, leather, and horses. And to hire men. Men with military experience. He says the taverns are full of whispers. Whispers about a hawk returning to its nest.

DUKE
Pedro.
[He says the name like a curse.]
That romantic fool. I underestimated him. I thought him a poet, not a strategist.

VALOIS
He is nothing, Your Grace! A banished traitor. He has no support. The people are loyal to you.

CASSIAN
Are they, my lord? Or are they quiet? There is a difference. The people loved the old king. And they loved Pedro for his connection to him, and for his… charisma. You rule effectively, Your Grace. But you do not rule with their hearts.

DUKE
I do not need their hearts. I need their obedience. Where is he getting the gold?

[The Duke stops pacing and looks sharply at Cassian.]

DUKE
The royal treasury was almost empty when I took power. I had it all accounted for. Where did this wealth come from?

CASSIAN
There were always rumors that the old king kept a private reserve. Hidden. Its location known only to him… and perhaps to his daughter.

[A tense silence fills the room. The Duke’s eyes narrow as the implication sinks in.]

DUKE
Oriana.

VALOIS
Impossible! She is a girl. She weeps for her lost love and prepares for her wedding. We see her every day. She is a model of compliance.

DUKE
[He slams his fist on the table, making the others jump.]
A model of compliance? Or a model of deception? She has been playing a part. All of it. The grief. The sorrow. The slow, reluctant acceptance. She has been buying time.

MARIUS
If she is funding him, Your Grace, then she is as guilty of treason as he is.

DUKE
Of course she is! She has her father’s stubbornness and her mother’s cunning. A dangerous combination. I gave her three months… I handed my enemy a calendar and a purse.

VALOIS
We must act! Arrest her! Question her! She will tell us everything.

CASSIAN
And make her a martyr? The people will see it as a monstrous injustice. A powerful Duke tormenting a grieving orphan girl on the eve of her wedding. It would turn those whispers you hear in the taverns into a roar. Pedro could not ask for a better recruiting tool.

DUKE
[He breathes deeply, regaining his composure.]
Cassian is right. We cannot touch her. Not openly. Not yet. We must be subtle. Double the guards on her chambers. Watch her maid, Beatrice. Search her rooms, but make it look routine. We need proof. Something we can show the world that she is a traitor, not a victim.

MARIUS
And Pedro’s growing army? We cannot simply ignore them.

DUKE
No. You will take a legion, General. You will march to the Blackwater Pass and you will hunt them down. I want Pedro found. I do not care if you bring him back in chains or in a box. Extinguish this little rebellion before it becomes a fire.

MARIUS
It will be done, Your Grace.

DUKE
Go. All of you. Leave me. I need to think.

[Marius and Valois bow and exit. Cassian lingers for a moment.]

CASSIAN
Your Grace… be wary. An animal is never more dangerous than when it is cornered. By trying to trap her, you may only be hastening her attack.

DUKE
She is not an animal, Cassian. She is my niece. And I will not be overthrown by a child’s broken heart. Now go.

[Cassian bows and exits, leaving the Duke alone. He walks to a large window overlooking the city. His face is a mask of cold fury and dawning realization. He has been outmaneuvered, and he knows it. The game is afoot, and his opponent is far more dangerous than he had ever imagined.]

[SCENE ENDS]

[A new scene begins. The chambers of PRINCESS ORIANA. The room is lavish but tastefully appointed, filled with books and tapestries. It feels more like a study than a princess’s boudoir. ORIANA is standing by a window, looking down at the courtyard, her back to the room. She is the image of serene grief, dressed in muted silver and grey. Her maid, BEATRICE, a woman of similar age but with a perpetually worried expression, is wringing her hands near the door.]

BEATRICE
They are still there, my lady. Two of them. At the end of the hall. They do not even pretend to be on a routine patrol. They just stand. And watch.

ORIANA
[Without turning]
Let them watch. Let them grow bored. Let them report to my uncle that I do nothing but gaze at the sky and sigh. It is what he expects.

BEATRICE
But what if they search the room again? That book… the ledger…

ORIANA
It is hidden where no soldier would think to look. In plain sight. It is a book of poetry, Beatrice. Verses on the seasons. They are looking for maps and letters, for daggers and poisons. They will not find treason in a sonnet about autumn leaves.

BEATRICE
I pray you are right. Every footstep in the hall makes my heart leap into my throat. He knows. The Duke. He must suspect something. Why else double the guard?

ORIANA
[She turns from the window. Her face is calm, but her eyes hold a chilling resolve.]
He suspects. He does not know. There is a world of difference between the two. Suspicion is a cloud. Knowledge is a blade. He is trying to force our hand. He wants us to panic, to make a mistake.

BEATRICE
And are we not? Panicking? My hands have not stopped shaking since dawn.

ORIANA
Your hands may shake. Mine will not. My father taught me that the mind is a fortress. As long as its walls are strong, it does not matter how the body trembles. Now, is Madame Verron on her way?

BEATRICE
She is. Her carriage was just admitted. The guards checked her patterns and her pins as if she were carrying state secrets.

ORIANA
In a way, she is. Go. See that she is not kept waiting. And Beatrice… compose yourself. Your fear is a scent, and my uncle has the nose of a bloodhound.

[Beatrice curtsies, her movements jerky with anxiety, and exits. Oriana walks to a small writing desk. She picks up a quill, but does not dip it in the ink. She simply holds it, her knuckles white. She closes her eyes, taking a single, deep breath. A moment later, Beatrice reenters, followed by MADAME VERRON, the royal dressmaker. Verron is a severe looking woman in her fifties, her face a professional mask of neutral politeness. She carries a large, flat box.]

MADAME VERRON
Your Highness. I have the silks you requested for the wedding gown. The finest weave from the southern provinces.

ORIANA
Show me.

[Madame Verron places the box on a large table and opens it. She lifts out a swathe of shimmering white fabric. It is exquisite. Beatrice gasps.]

BEATRICE
Oh, my lady. It is like moonlight on snow.

ORIANA
It is very… white.

MADAME VERRON
It is traditional, Your Highness. For a bride.

ORIANA
[She runs a finger over the fabric, her touch light, almost dismissive.]
Tradition is a cage built by those who came before us. Sometimes, it is a safe one. Sometimes, the door is rusted shut. I find this color lacks… conviction.

MADAME VERRON
Conviction, Your Highness?

ORIANA
Yes. It is the color of surrender. I am not surrendering. I am making a bargain. There is a difference.

[Oriana moves to a chest in the corner of the room and lifts the lid. She rummages inside for a moment before pulling out a small, wooden spool.]

ORIANA
Here.

[She hands the spool to Madame Verron. It is wound with a thread of pure, shimmering gold.]

MADAME VERRON
Your Highness, this is… it is heavy. This is not for embroidery. This is bullion thread. It is worth a fortune.

ORIANA
I want it woven into the hem. A single, delicate line. And into the cuffs. A symbol. Something only I will know is there.

MADAME VERRON
[Her eyes dart towards the door, then back to Oriana.]
It will be difficult. To weave it into the fabric itself… it requires a special technique. It will be almost invisible to the eye.

ORIANA
That is the point. I do not wish for it to be seen. I wish for it to be felt. A line of strength. A promise. Can you do it?

[A silent understanding passes between the two women. This is more than a discussion of dressmaking.]

MADAME VERRON
It will require more time. And the cost of the labor will be… significant.

ORIANA
The cost is already accounted for. You have received the advance for your troubles? From the merchants’ guild, as we discussed?

MADAME VERRON
I have, Your Highness. A most generous commission for tapestries to adorn the Great Hall for the wedding feast. Far more than the work is worth.

ORIANA
The guild appreciates the patronage. They understand the importance of a beautiful celebration. Ensure this thread is woven with the utmost care. Let us say… enough for three gowns. This one, and two others. For my ladies in waiting. The same design.

MADAME VERRON
[She nods slowly, understanding the coded instruction.]
Three gowns. With a golden promise woven into each. It will be done. The extra material from the guild’s commission will be more than sufficient.

ORIANA
Good. You may go. Leave the silk. I wish to… accustom myself to it.

[Madame Verron bows deeply, packs her empty box, and exits, escorted by Beatrice. Oriana is left alone with the shimmering white silk. She picks it up, holding it against herself, and looks at her reflection in a silver mirror. Her face is a mask of cold duty. She is not looking at a wedding dress. She is looking at a uniform.]

[SCENE ENDS]

[A new scene begins. A windswept encampment in the foothills of the Blackwater Pass. The terrain is harsh and rocky. The wind howls. GENERAL MARIUS stands over a campaign table, a map weighted down with stones. His face is weathered, his armor practical and scarred. A young LIEUTENANT, nervous and eager, stands beside him.]

LIEUTENANT
No word from the scouts, General. The storm is getting worse. The locals say no man can cross the pass in this weather. They say the mountain spirits will claim them.

MARIUS
The locals also say that a seventh son can talk to sheep. I put little stock in it. Pedro is up there. And he is no spirit. He is a man. And men get cold. They get hungry. They get careless.

LIEUTENANT
But to lead a legion into that… it is a death trap. The path is narrow. An ambush would be devastating.

MARIUS
[He stabs a finger at the map.]
An ambush requires an army. Pedro does not have an army. He has a rabble. Farmers with pitchforks, disgraced knights with grudges, and young fools drunk on his name. They are a mob, not a military force.

LIEUTENANT
But their numbers grow. The reports from the villages… they say men flock to his banner. They call him the True King’s son.

MARIUS
They can call him the emperor of the moon for all I care. A title does not stop a crossbow bolt. The Duke wants this rebellion crushed. He did not say it had to be elegant. We are not here to fight a battle. We are here to perform an execution.

LIEUTENANT
And the Princess? The rumors in the capital…

MARIUS
[He looks up from the map, his eyes hard as flint.]
We are soldiers, Lieutenant. We do not concern ourselves with rumors from the capital. We concern ourselves with the enemy in front of us. The Duke gives the orders. We obey them. It is the simple, brutal arithmetic of our profession. Is that understood?

LIEUTENANT
Yes, General.

MARIUS
Pedro’s weakness is his own legend. He thinks he is a hero from the songs. He will try to make a grand stand. He will seek a battle to rally his followers. He will pick a place like the Stone Bridge, or the old fortress at Greyfang. He wants a stage.

LIEUTENANT
And we will deny him one.

MARIUS
We will do more than that. We will ignore the stage entirely. We will not march up the main pass. [He traces a new route on the map, a treacherous, winding path through the lesser known goat trails.] We will take the Widow’s Climb. It is dangerous. We may lose men to the mountain. But they will never expect it. We will be in his rear before his sentries have finished their morning wine. We will surround him. We will starve him. And when his glorious rebellion is nothing but a thousand frozen men gnawing on their leather belts, I will offer him terms.

LIEUTENANT
You will let him surrender?

MARIUS
I will offer him a choice. His life, in exchange for the name of every man, every guild, every noble house that gave him so much as a single copper coin.

LIEUTENANT
He would never betray them. His honor would not allow it.

MARIUS
[A grim smile.]
Honor is a luxury for the well fed, Lieutenant. Let us see how honorable he is after two weeks of eating rocks and snow. Now, send word to the captains. We break camp in an hour. And tell the quartermaster to distribute the climbing ropes. The mountain spirits are about to have company.

[The Lieutenant salutes and hurries off. Marius stares at the map, the howling wind whipping at his cloak. He is not a man of politics or intrigue. He is a tool of the state, and he is about to be put to use.]

[SCENE ENDS]

[A new scene begins. The Duke’s private study. The same room as the first scene. It is late. Rain streaks against the window panes. The DUKE is sitting behind his large desk, a stack of documents before him. He looks tired, but his energy is coiled, like a waiting serpent. VALOIS stands before the desk, looking uncomfortable.]

DUKE
You look troubled, Valois. Does the rain offend you?

VALOIS
It is not the rain, Your Grace. It is this… this waiting. Marius is in the mountains. The Princess is in her chambers. We sit here, shuffling papers. It feels like inaction.

DUKE
Inaction? Look at this desk. This is not inaction. This is the real war. Not the clash of swords you are so fond of, but the scratching of quills. The careful audit. The signed decree. This is how power is truly held. Not by shouting, but by signing. I have spent two days reading every treasury report from the last year of my brother’s reign.

VALOIS
And what have you found, Your Grace?

DUKE
I have found that my brother was either a financial genius or a magnificent thief. Huge sums, Valois. Withdrawn from the treasury for ‘royal commissions’. Tapestries that were never woven. Sculptures that were never carved. A new wing for the library that was never built. All paid for. All documented. The money simply… vanished.

VALOIS
Into his private reserve. As Cassian suggested.

DUKE
Precisely. But a king cannot simply walk out of the treasury with a wagon full of gold. It must be moved. Laundered. Disguised as legitimate commerce. And for that, he would need partners. Who controls the flow of wealth in this city, Valois?

VALOIS
The merchants’ guilds, Your Grace. The Weavers, the Goldsmiths, the Spice Traders.

DUKE
Exactly. My brother showered them with royal charters and tax exemptions. They owed him everything. They would have been his willing accomplices, hiding his gold in their own accounts, moving it for him when he asked. And now, they are moving it for his daughter.

VALOIS
We should arrest the guild masters! Seize their books!

DUKE
And start a panic that would cripple the city’s economy? No. We do not need to use an axe when a scalpel will do. We need leverage. One man who knows the truth and is more afraid of me than he is of her.

[The Duke rings a small silver bell on his desk. A moment later, a guard enters, followed by a trembling, grey haired man in the robes of a royal functionary. This is LOREN, the Royal Treasurer. He bows so low his forehead almost touches the floor.]

LOREN
Your Grace. You sent for me.

DUKE
Loren. You served my brother for twenty years as Royal Treasurer. A model of diligence, I am told.

LOREN
I have always tried to be, Your Grace. A humble servant of the crown.

DUKE
Humility is a fine quality. Honesty is finer still. I have been reviewing your old ledgers. Tell me about the King’s Commission for Urban Beautification. I see an expenditure of eighty thousand gold crowns. A vast sum. Yet, I walk through the city and I see no new fountains. No great plazas. Where did the money go, Loren?

LOREN
[Sweating profusely]
The King… his late Majesty… he oversaw those projects personally, Your Grace. The plans were… fluid. He dealt directly with the guild masters. I was merely instructed to release the funds upon his signature.

DUKE
His signature. I see. So you asked no questions. You, the guardian of the kingdom’s wealth, simply signed away a fortune and did not once ask to see a blueprint or a builder’s contract?

LOREN
It was not my place to question the King, Your Grace.

DUKE
It is your place to answer to me. My niece is getting married. A cause for great celebration. I think it is time the city’s merchants showed their loyalty to their new Duke. I am levying a special tax. A ‘celebration tithe’. Effective tomorrow. All major guilds are to contribute ten percent of their declared assets to the state. To pay for the wedding and to fund General Marius’s campaign against the bandits in the mountains.

LOREN
[His face goes pale.]
Ten percent? Your Grace, that is… that is ruinous! They will protest! The city’s commerce will grind to a halt!

DUKE
Only if their declared assets are what they truly possess. But if, for example, a guild was holding a large amount of… unofficial capital… for a friend… well, ten percent of that would be a catastrophic loss. A loss so great, it might make a guild master rethink his loyalties. He might come to his Duke and tell him a few interesting stories to have this tax… reconsidered.

VALOIS
[Understanding dawns on his face.]
You are squeezing them.

DUKE
I am reminding them where their allegiances lie. Oriana is using her father’s hidden network to fund her lover. She pays them with her father’s gold, and they move it for her. I am going to make that gold a liability. A poison in their vaults.

LOREN
I… I do not understand, Your Grace.

DUKE
[He leans forward, his voice dropping to a silken threat.]
Yes, you do, Loren. You are a man of numbers. You know exactly where the bodies are buried, because you helped draw the map. You will deliver the notices of the new tax yourself. First thing in the morning. Go to the Master of the Weavers’ Guild first. Tell him the Duke is keenly interested in the sudden boom in the market for golden thread. Then you will wait. And one of them, perhaps all of them, will come to me. And they will give me the proof I need to crush my sentimental, treacherous little niece. Now get out.

[Loren bows again, stumbling backward out of the room, his face a mask of pure terror. The Duke leans back in his chair, a cold smile touching his lips. He picks up a quill and signs a document with a flourish.]

DUKE
You see, Valois? No armies. No arrests. Just a little ink on a piece of parchment. And a kingdom can be brought to its knees. The game is so much more interesting this way.

[Valois watches him, a new respect, and a new fear, in his eyes. The Duke has set his trap. The rain continues to beat against the glass, like the drumming of a distant, approaching war.]

[SCENE ENDS]
