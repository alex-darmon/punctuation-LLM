Enter Lysander and Felix.

FELIX
I pray thee, good Lysander, leave this mood.
The market throngs with men of every humour,
And thine is fit to turn the sun to ice.

LYSANDER
My humour? Why, I am the soul of peace. But thou, Felix, thou art a man for trouble. Thou wouldst pick a quarrel with the moon for changing her shape. Thou wouldst sue a man for having a shadow longer than thine own past noon. Dids’t thou not challenge a baker for the uneven browning of his loaf, claiming it did mock the patches on thy jerkin? And yet thou art here to school me in the art of calm!

FELIX
And I were half so ready for a brawl, my life’s lease would be up ere the next church bell.

LYSANDER
The lease! O, a short term. See, here comes what will surely break it.

Enter Corvinus and his retainers.

FELIX
By my faith, the Cassians. Lysander, let us hence.

LYSANDER
Let a dog hence. I will stand my ground.

CORVINUS
A good day to you, gentlemen. I would have speech with one of you.

LYSANDER
And but one of us? Your company is so scant you cannot spare a word for each? Or is your wit so thin it cannot stretch to two?

CORVINUS
My wit is for my friends. For my foes, I have my sword. And you shall have the point of it, sir, if you give me cause.

LYSANDER
Cannot you find a cause unless it be given? Is your invention so barren?

CORVINUS
Lysander, you keep company with Lucian.

LYSANDER
Company? Do you make us players on a stage? Well, if we be players, look for a tragedy. Here is my prompter. [Touches the hilt of his sword.] It speaks but one line, and that is ‘dance.’

FELIX
For heaven’s sake, we are in the public eye.
Withdraw to some quiet corner, if you must,
And speak your grievances with tempered reason,
Or else go separate ways. The Duke’s decree
Is fresh against these brawls.

LYSANDER
Let them look. A man’s eye is for seeing. I’ll not stir for any Cassian’s stare.

Enter Lucian.

CORVINUS
Well, talk be done, sir, here comes my quarry.

LYSANDER
But I’ll be damned, sir, if he answers to your call. He’s no man’s quarry but his own. Go to the forest, you may find a deer more willing.

CORVINUS
Lucian, the honour of my house, stained by thee,
Can find no word so fitting as this: thou art a traitor.

LUCIAN
Corvinus, the reason that I have to wish you well
Doth overcome the anger such a word should start.
Traitor I am not. Therefore, I leave you.
I see you know me not.

CORVINUS
Worm, this shall not excuse the slanders thou hast spread.
Therefore, turn and defend thy wretched life.

LUCIAN
I do protest I never slandered thee,
But hold the name of Cassian in respect
Until you know the reason for my peace.
And so, good Cassian, which name I value
Almost as my own, be satisfied.

LYSANDER
O vile patience! O dishonourable calm!
[Draws.]
The passado means to have it all.
Corvinus, you hunter of rats, will you walk?

CORVINUS
What do you want with me?

LYSANDER
Good King of Vermin, nothing but one of your wretched lives, which I intend to take, and then, depending on my mood, beat the honour out of the rest of your name. Will you draw your steel out of its leather sheath? Be quick, lest mine be rapping at your skull ere yours sees daylight.

CORVINUS
[Drawing.]
I am for you.

LUCIAN
Gentle Lysander, put your weapon up.

LYSANDER
Come, sir, your counter. [They fight.]

LUCIAN
Draw, Felix, beat their swords down to the ground.
For shame, gentlemen, cease this brutish rage.
Corvinus, Lysander, the Duke expressly hath
Forbidden swordplay in the city’s heart.
Hold, Corvinus! Good Lysander!

[Lucian steps between them. Corvinus, under Lucian’s arm, stabs Lysander.]

CORVINUS
[Exit Corvinus with his men.]

LYSANDER
I am struck. A pox on both your banners. I am done.
Is he gone and has no scratch?

FELIX
What, art thou hurt?

LYSANDER
Ay, ay, a wound, a wound. But ’tis enough.
Where is my servant? Go, you knave, find a physician. [Exit Servant.]

LUCIAN
Courage, man, the hurt cannot be deep.

LYSANDER
No, ’tis not so wide as a river, nor so deep as a mine, but it will serve. Inquire for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a still man. I am seasoned, I warrant, for this earth. A pox on both your banners. Zounds, a cur, a fiend, a coward, that strikes from behind a shield of peace! Why, in God’s name, did you come between us? I took the blow beneath your arm.

LUCIAN
I thought to keep the peace.

LYSANDER
Help me to some house, Felix,
Or I shall faint. A pox on both your banners.
They have made dust of me. I have it, and soundly too.
Your banners! [Exeunt Lysander and Felix.]

LUCIAN
This gentleman, the Duke’s own favoured cousin,
My truest friend, hath met his mortal wound
For my own sake. My honour has been stained
By Corvinus’s slander, Corvinus, that I sought
To call my brother. O my sweet Elara,
Your gentle soul hath made a coward of me
And in my spirit softened valour’s iron.

Re-enter Felix.

FELIX
O Lucian, Lucian, brave Lysander’s dead.
That fiery spirit has sought out the heavens,
Which too soon here did find the world unworthy.

LUCIAN
This dark day’s work shall cast a shadow long;
This is the start of woe that others now must end.

Re-enter Corvinus.

FELIX
Here comes the furious Corvinus back again.

LUCIAN
What, back in triumph, and Lysander slain?
Then gentle mercy, get you to the sky,
And let molten fury be my guide!
Now, Corvinus, take the ‘traitor’ back again
That you did give me, for Lysander’s soul
Is but a moment’s journey overhead,
And waits for thine to bear it company.
Either thou, or I, or both, shall follow him.

CORVINUS
Thou miserable boy, that wert his friend,
Shalt join him now.

LUCIAN
This blade shall settle that. [They fight; Corvinus falls.]

FELIX
Lucian, away, be gone!
The watch is raised, and Corvinus is slain.
Stand not confounded. The Duke will sentence thee
To death if thou art taken. Hence, away!

LUCIAN
O, I am fate’s own jester!

FELIX
Why do you wait? [Exit Lucian.]

Enter Watchmen.

FIRST WATCHMAN
Where is the man who struck Lysander down?
Corvinus, that fiend, which way did he run?

FELIX
There lies that Corvinus.

FIRST WATCHMAN
Up, sir, you come with us.
I charge you in the Duke’s great name, obey.

Enter Duke, attended; Lord Valerius, Lady Cassian, and others.

DUKE
Who are the wretched starters of this brawl?

FELIX
O noble Duke, I can explain it all,
The sad beginning of this fatal strife.
There lies the man, slain by young Lucian’s hand,
That slew your kinsman, bold Lysander.

LADY CASSIAN
Corvinus! My son! O, my sister’s child!
O Duke! O husband! See, the blood is spilled
Of my dear boy! Duke, as you are just,
For Cassian blood, let Valerius blood be dust!
O, my sweet son!

DUKE
Felix, who began this bloody business?

FELIX
Corvinus, here slain, whom Lucian’s hand did kill.
Lucian spoke him gently, bade him think
How grave the matter was, and spoke besides
Of your high anger. All this he did say
With placid voice, calm mien, a peaceful heart,
But could not make a truce with the hot rage
Of Corvinus, deaf to reason, who then tilts
With venom’d steel at brave Lysander’s heart.
Who, just as hot, turns deadly point to point,
And with a warrior’s pride, with one hand knocks
Death’s chilly hand aside, and with the other
Sends it to Corvinus, whose quick skill
Returns it. Lucian, he cries aloud,
‘Hold, friends! Friends, part!’ and faster than his words,
His nimble arm beats down their fatal points,
And rushes in between; beneath whose arm
A hateful thrust from Corvinus struck the life
Of stout Lysander, and then Corvinus fled.
But soon comes back again to Lucian,
Who had but then embraced a burning vengeance,
And to it they do go like thunder-claps, for ere I
Could draw my sword to part them, proud Corvinus fell,
And as he dropped, did Lucian turn and fly.
This is the truth, or let your servant die.

LADY CASSIAN
He is a Valerius, loyal to his house.
His love for them makes him a liar, he speaks not true.
I’ll warrant twenty of them fought this day,
And all those twenty could but kill one man.
I cry for justice, which you, Duke, must give.
Lucian slew Corvinus, Lucian must not live.

DUKE
Lucian slew him, but he slew Lysander.
Who now the price of that dear blood must answer?

LORD VALERIUS
Not Lucian, Duke, he was Lysander’s friend.
His fault was but to do what law would do,
And take the life of Corvinus.

DUKE
And for that crime,
We do immediately banish him hence.
My own heart has an interest in your feud;
My blood for your barbaric brawls lies spilled.
But I will punish you with such a fine
That you shall all repent this loss of mine.

I will not have it so! I’ll stand for this, To keep my word, my law, and my decree. Your brawls have stained our city’s ancient stones With purple rivers, and for that you pay. The fine is set. My kinsman’s life is gone. And Lucian, for his part, must greet the dawn In other lands, a stranger to his own. Let this hard lesson to your hearts be known. My judgment stands. Remove these men of clay. And let us hence, to mourn this bitter day.

[Exit Duke, attended. The Watchmen bear the bodies of Corvinus and Lysander away. Lord Valerius and Lady Cassian remain, with their few attendants. They stare at one another across the stage.]

LADY CASSIAN
A fine, my lord? He pays our grief with gold?
My son’s warm blood, that is not yet full cold,
Is valued at a price, a sum, a fee?
And your boy lives, though he must turn and flee.
This is not justice, this is but a trade.
A coward’s peace by a weak ruler made.

LORD VALERIUS
Madam, your son was first to seek the fray.
He drew on armed Lysander in the day,
A crime which by the Duke’s own law is death.
My Lucian did but draw a vengeful breath
That any man of honour would have drawn.
He is my son, and now my son is gone.
You have your grief, and I, my lady, mine.
Let that suffice. This is the Duke’s design.

LADY CASSIAN
Your son is gone, but he can be brought back.
Mine lies upon a cold and final rack
Of endless silence. Do not speak to me
Of your small sorrows. Mine’s a boundless sea
That will engulf your house and all your line.
Mark me, Valerius. Vengeance will be mine.

[Exit Lady Cassian with her followers.]

LORD VALERIUS
Her words are daggers, poisoned at the tip.
What bitter brew will pass that woman’s lip?
Felix, my friend, my son’s most loyal friend,
Where did he go? Where will his journey end?

FELIX
I saw him turn towards the friar’s gate,
To seek some counsel on his wretched state
Before the sun commands him to depart.
He went with heavy step and heavier heart.

LORD VALERIUS
The good Friar Thomas. He will give him aid.
A righteous man, of no one’s wrath afraid.
Let us go home. The night will bring no rest.
Our house is wounded, put to fortune’s test.
Come, Felix. We have much to think upon.
A son is banished, and our peace is gone.

[Exeunt.]

[SCENE II. A cell in Friar Thomas’s monastery. It is sparsely furnished with a wooden cot, a small desk, and a crucifix on the wall. A single candle burns.]

Enter Friar Thomas, a man of fifty years, with a kind but weary face. He is followed by Lucian, who looks distraught.

FRIAR THOMAS
Come, rest you here. The world outside is mad.
This holy place will soothe a soul so sad.
The Duke’s decree is harsh, but it is life.
You are preserved from this unholy strife.
Banishment is not the grave, my son.
Your race is not yet permanently run.

LUCIAN
You call this life? To be a walking ghost?
To leave behind what I do love the most?
To be a leaf torn from its native tree
And blown about by winds of misery?
The name of exile is a gentle sound
For being dead and walking on the ground.
Here in this city is my heart, my soul,
My every part that makes a perfect whole.
And I must leave it, like a thief in night,
Torn from myself and banished from the light.

FRIAR THOMAS
Your grief speaks loud, as is the right of youth.
But listen now to an old man’s plain truth.
The world is wide, and time can wonders do.
The Duke’s hot anger may in time cool too.
This storm will pass, these clouds will break apart.
You must hold courage in your lonely heart.
Patience, my boy. For what seems black as pitch
Can be a field, fallow and newly rich,
Where you may plant the seeds of a new fate
And learn to live, and for redemption wait.

LUCIAN
Redemption? When my very name’s a curse?
Could any new life be aught but a hearse
For all my joys? I am a branded man,
The living emblem of a broken clan.
O, Isabella! Must I lose you too?
My love, my life, what will my absence do
To your soft heart, caught in this cruel design?
A lamb beset by wolves, and you are mine!

[A soft knock at the cell door.]

FRIAR THOMAS
Be still. Who comes at such a late-won hour?
Perhaps some soul who seeks confession’s power.
Stay in the shadow. I will see who calls.
Few friendly feet do walk these sacred halls
So close to midnight.

[The Friar opens the door. A cloaked figure stands there. It is Isabella.]

ISABELLA
Good Friar, pardon my intrusion late.
I heard the news… I could not bear to wait.
I had to know if he was safe, or fled.
My heart is full of terror and of dread.

[Lucian steps from the shadows.]

LUCIAN
Isabella!

ISABELLA
Lucian! O, my love, my own!
I feared that I should have to weep alone.

[They embrace.]

FRIAR THOMAS
My children, careful. Even stones have ears.
And your two houses nurse a thousand fears
And twice a thousand hates. To be found here
Would turn your sorrow to a fate more drear.

ISABELLA
I do not care. Let them but find me now.
To see your face, to know that you still vow
Your love to me, is worth a mortal price.
They told me you were banished. In a trice,
My world dissolved to ash and bitter smoke.
The cord that binds my very spirit broke.

LUCIAN
And I must go. Before the morning star
Has faded from the sky, I must be far
From these dear walls, this air that you do breathe.
My love, a crown of thorns is what I wreathe
To place upon your brow by loving you.
My presence brings you only grief anew.

ISABELLA
Then let me grieve. But let me grieve for one
Whose heart is mine beneath the lonely sun.
O, Lucian, where will you go? To what shore
Will your sad ship of exile come? What door
Will open to a man without a name,
A home, a friend, a fortune, or a fame?

LUCIAN
I do not know. To wander is my doom.
The world will be my cold and spacious tomb.
Unless… unless you give me cause to live.
A hope, a promise, is all you can give.
Swear you’ll be mine. Swear that you will not let
Your mother’s anger teach you to forget.

ISABELLA
Forget you, Lucian? I would first forget
My own name, how to speak, how to beget
A single thought that was not born of you.
My love is constant as the heavens’ blue.
Though clouds of hate may hide it from your eye,
It is eternal, and it cannot die.
Here, take this token.

[She takes a small silver locket from her neck.]

Keep it next to you.
Within it is my likeness, painted true.
And when you look on it in foreign lands,
Remember her who waits with empty hands,
And prays for your return with every breath.
My faith is yours, in life and after death.

LUCIAN
And you take this.

[He removes a ring from his finger, a simple band with the Valerius seal.]

It is my father’s crest.
Let it lie hidden, sleeping at your breast.
A secret sign that you are pledged to me.
A wife in spirit, if not by decree.
I swear by all that’s holy in this cell,
My love for you is an un-sounded well,
So deep that fate itself cannot run dry
The endless fount of tears when I must fly.

FRIAR THOMAS
My children, this is sorrow’s sweetest art,
To pledge the hand when you must give the heart
To separation. But I have a plan.
Despair is not the final state of man.
Lucian, you will not wander without aim.
I have a brother, who holds dear my name,
In Mantua. A scholar, wise and good.
He keeps a quiet house. I understood
From his last letter he requires a scribe,
A man of letters, far from any tribe
Of warring kinsmen. I will write for you
A commendation, honest, strong, and true.
You’ll go to him. There you can safely stay
Until the anger here has passed away.

LUCIAN
Good Friar, you are heaven’s instrument.

FRIAR THOMAS
And for you, daughter, when my words are sent
To Lucian, I will find a secret way
To send your letters also. You may say
Your heart’s true message, and receive his own.
You shall not be entirely alone.

ISABELLA
A drop of water in a burning drought!
A single star when all the lamps are out!
O, thank you, Father. You have given me
A thread of hope to which my soul can flee.

FRIAR THOMAS
But you must be as cunning as a fox.
Your mother’s house is built on vengeful rocks.
Lady Cassian, I have known her long,
Is not a woman to forgive a wrong.
She will watch you. Be circumspect and wise.
Let duty be the mask before your eyes.
Visit the church, speak of repentance, prayer.
Let no one guess the hope that you keep there
Within your heart. For if she were to know,
A convent’s gate is where you’d surely go.

LUCIAN
The dawn approaches. I can feel its chill.
The birds begin to stir upon the hill.
Each moment that I linger here and wait
Adds one more link to the long chain of fate
That pulls me from you. I must go.

ISABELLA
Not yet.
One more embrace before my sun has set.

[They hold each other tightly.]

O, cruelest day! O, night that was too brief!
My joy is stolen by a hateful thief.
Go, then, my love, since you are forced to go.
May angels guard you from all worldly woe.

LUCIAN
And you, my dearest. Farewell. Live in hope.
My love will give my spirit endless scope
To bear this burden. Friar, I am in your debt
A sum so great I can’t repay it yet.

FRIAR THOMAS
Go with God’s peace, my son. Be swift and sure.
The eastern road is safest, though obscure.
Avoid the main path till the sun is high.
Now go. Away with you. Fly, Lucian, fly.

[Lucian gives Isabella one last look, then exits quickly.]

ISABELLA
He’s gone. The candle flickers in the draft.
My life’s frail ship is rudderless, un-graphed.
How can I live?

FRIAR THOMAS
You live because you must.
Your love’s a sacred and a holy trust.
You’ll live for him. Now dry your weeping eyes.
You must be home before the city rise,
And seem as one who slept the whole night through.
Go, child. And my prayers will go with you.

[Exit Isabella. The Friar stands alone, sighs, and kneels to pray as the stage darkens.]

[SCENE III. A room in Lord Valerius’s house. The next morning. Lord Valerius is at a table covered in papers. His wife, Lady Elara, stands by a window.]

LADY ELARA
No word? No sign? No message from the gate?
He’s gone then. Left to his uncertain fate.
My son, my only son, cast out to be
A beggar in a land of strangers. He
Who was the pride and jewel of my eye,
Now has no roof but the unpitying sky.

LORD VALERIUS
He is a Valerius. He will endure.
His spirit is resilient and pure.
The Friar will have given him good advice.
He will not fall prey to misfortune’s dice.
I pray he will be safe. I pray he’s wise
Enough to hide his name and his proud eyes
From those who’d seek to do him injury.

LADY ELARA
You speak of prayers. I speak of misery.
And what of this?

[She gestures to the papers on the table.]

More sorrow for our house?

LORD VALERIUS
The Duke is true to his proclaimed vows.
This is the schedule of the fine we owe.
A bitter seed from which no good can grow.
He strips our coffers bare. He takes the rent
From half our lands. The punishment is meant
To break our power, Elara, not just mend
The city’s peace. The Duke was not our friend
In this decree. He bled us with his pen.

LADY ELARA
The Cassians pay the same?

LORD VALERIUS
They do. But then,
Their wealth is newer, drawn from trade and loans.
They do not feel it in their ancient bones
The way we do. To lose a field, a farm
That has been ours since my great grandsire’s arm
First cleared the woods… it is a deeper wound
Than any sword could make in this sad ground.

Enter Felix.

FELIX
My lord, my lady. I have come to see
If I can be of service.

LADY ELARA
O, Felix, we are in a stormy sea.
Our ship is tossed, our guiding star is lost.
What is the temper of the town? What cost
Besides this gold must we be forced to pay
In whispers, glances, at the light of day?

FELIX
The town is quiet, like a sleeping beast
That is not sated from its bloody feast.
They talk in taverns and the market square.
Some blame Corvinus, some do not forbear
To curse the name of Lucian. It is split,
As is the council. But the worst of it
Is what I hear from servants of their house.
The Cassians mutter, and they take new vows
Not in a church, but in the shadowed lanes.
Lady Cassian’s grief has burst its reins.
She is not satisfied with Lucian’s flight.
She speaks of justice of a darker night.

LORD VALERIUS
I knew it. She is not the weeping kind.
A lust for blood has occupied her mind.
She will not trust the law, but her own hand.
My son is hunted in this very land.

LADY ELARA
You must send word! You must send riders out
To find him, warn him, clear his path of doubt
And danger! Give him gold and give him guard!

LORD VALERIUS
And have my men arrested? Have them barred
From leaving by the Duke’s own watch? My dear,
To act so openly confirms our fear
And makes us traitors to the Duke’s command.
We must be subtle. We must understand
Our foe. Felix, be you my eyes and ears.
Learn what you can. Assuage my lady’s fears
If it be possible, but bring me truth.
We must protect the future of our youth.

FELIX
I will, my lord. I’ll be a walking shade,
And listen to the plots that they have made.
I’ll learn the names of every friend they keep,
And watch them even when they think they sleep.

LORD VALERIUS
Good man. Our house depends on such as you.
Now leave us. We have grieving left to do.

[Exit Felix.]

LADY ELARA
He will be killed. I feel it in my soul.
A mother’s instinct, that I can’t control.
They’ll find him on some lonely, dusty road,
And add his body to their bloody load.

LORD VALERIUS
Have faith. He is my son, but also yours.
He has your courage, which for aye endures.
He has my cunning. He will not be caught
So easily. But this new, venomed thought
From Cassian’s mind must be our first concern.
We have a bitter lesson yet to learn.

[They stand together, looking out the window as the scene ends.]

[SCENE IV. A dark, tapestry-hung chamber in the Cassian house. Lady Cassian is dressed in black. She stares at a large portrait of her son, Corvinus. Marcus, a grim man of forty with a soldier’s bearing, stands waiting.]

LADY CASSIAN
Do you see him? Do you see the life
That danced within those eyes, before the knife
Of a Valerius coward stole his breath?
He was my sun. And now I live in death.
The world is shadow. Food has lost its taste.
My heart is but a dry and barren waste.
They tell me, weep. They tell me, time will heal.
They do not know the hatred that I feel.
It is a fire that consumes the tears,
And burns away the weakness of my fears.
It is my food, my drink, my only air.
A mother’s vengeance is a fearful prayer.

MARCUS
My lady, I was servant to your lord,
And pledged to him my loyalty and sword.
Now he is gone, that service falls to you.
Command me. Tell me what I am to do.
My hand is ready, and my heart is hard.
Your will shall be my only guiding card.

LADY CASSIAN
The Duke has played the fool. He prattles on
Of law and order, now that blood is drawn.
He fines us. Fines! As if his coins could fill
The gaping hole in me that bleeds here still.
He banishes the murderer. A gift!
A chance to live, to find a new life’s thrift
In some soft southern town, and to forget
The face of him he killed. I’ll not have it.
The Duke’s law is a cobweb for the weak.
My law is sorrow, and the words I speak
Are written in my dear Corvinus’ blood.
A life for a life. It is understood?

MARCUS
I understand.

LADY CASSIAN
They think that he has fled
Beyond our reach. They think the words I said
Were empty rage. They are mistaken, Marcus.
My rage is not a fire, but an arcus,
A well aimed bow of purpose, cold and bent.
This is the arrow that I have you sent.

[She goes to a heavy chest and takes out a bag of gold, which she gives to him.]

Here is the payment for your loyalty.
Take two good men, of silent quality.
Men who can ride, and who can keep their tongue
Held fast between their teeth. Go you among
The roads that lead out from this cursed place.
He will be on one. Find his hated face.

MARCUS
And when we find him?

LADY CASSIAN
Do not bring him back.
I do not want him broken on the rack.
I do not want a trial, or a plea.
I want his breath to stop. I want him to be
As cold and silent as my murdered son.
I want his race of arrogance to be run.
Let his exile be a shallow grave
On some forgotten path. No priest to save
His wretched soul. No stone to mark the spot.
Just let him perish. Let his body rot.
Is that too plain for you?

MARCUS
It is as clear
As winter stars. You need have no more fear
That he will live to mock your honest grief.
His life, I promise, will be very brief.
We ride at dusk.

LADY CASSIAN
Good. You have always been
A faithful servant to our house. The sin,
If sin it is, is mine and mine alone.
I am the soil in which this seed is sown.
You are the hand that sees the harvest done.
Go now. And do not fail me. For my son.

[Marcus bows and exits. Lady Cassian turns back to the portrait, a look of terrible resolve on her face.]

LADY CASSIAN
Sleep well, my child. Your mother watches now.
And soon, my love, you’ll have a shade to bow
And be your servant in the lands of night.
I will set this disordered world to right.

[She touches the face on the canvas as the stage goes to black.]

ACT I
SCENE II

[The Duke’s study. A grand but austere room, dominated by a large oaken table covered in maps and documents. DUKE ALARIC stands by a tall window, looking out over the city. He is a man in his middle years, his face lined with the cares of his office. LORD EMMERICH, his elderly and trusted advisor, sits at the table, reviewing a scroll.]

DUKE ALARIC
She will not see it. Her grief is a high wall
That I have not the means to look beyond.
She sees a murdered son, a killer freed,
And me, a weakling Duke who plants a weed
Of compromise where justice should have grown.
She does not see the seeds of chaos sown
In every noble house, if I am not
The final arbiter of every plot
And every quarrel.

LORD EMMERICH
Grief has its own laws,
My lord. It does not pause to weigh a cause
Or contemplate the balance of the state.
It is a fever, burning, full of hate
For any hand that offers measured balm.
You sought to bring a necessary calm.
Lady Cassian seeks a cleansing fire.

DUKE ALARIC
And would she see the whole realm build a pyre
To satisfy her need? Corvinus fell,
It’s true. A tragedy. I know it well.
But it was in a duel, Emmerich.
A clash of youthful pride, a foolish, quick
Resort to steel when words had lost their way.
Young Valerius will live to rue the day,
But was it murder? By the strictest code
Of honor, which these hot bloods take as goad,
It was a fight agreed. My judgment stands.
To banish him was not an act of fear,
But a surgeon’s choice to save the flesh that’s dear
By cutting out the part that has turned ill.
To kill Valerius would only spill
More noble blood. His kin would seek revenge,
And on that bloody hinge the state would turn
To civil war. My fine and banishment
Were meant to stop the wound, not be a vent
For Cassian’s rage.

LORD EMMERICH
A wise and prudent choice.
But wisdom often has a quiet voice
That cannot shout above the cries of pain.
I fear you have preserved the state in vain,
If Lady Cassian does not heed your word.
Her family is a well worn, ancient sword,
Still sharp, and fiercely loyal to her hand.
They do not think of laws that rule the land.
They think of blood that’s owed and must be paid.
I saw her at the court. She is not swayed
By reason. She is now a monument
To sorrow. All her softer feelings spent.

DUKE ALARIC
What would you have me do? Confine her then?
Make her a prisoner? Rouse her loyal men
To open anger? No. We let her grieve.
Time is the only cure, I must believe.
The winter of her rage will have to pass.
Let her smash every mirror, every glass,
And curse my name until her voice is hoarse.
The sentence is proclaimed. It takes its course.
Valerius is gone. Her vengeance has
No target left within these city walls.

LORD EMMERICH
[Rolls up the scroll slowly.]
The world is larger than your city walls,
My lord. A man on horseback finds the roads
Are open to him, if his journey goads
Him onward. So it is with vengeance too.
It is a tireless hunter, seeing through
The cleverest disguise, the longest mile.
I would not be so certain for a while
That this affair is truly at an end.
A mother’s heart is slow, so slow, to mend.

[The Duke turns from the window, his face troubled. He walks to the table and places his hands upon a map.]

DUKE ALARIC
Then we shall watch. And we shall be prepared.
The peace of this domain must not be snared
In private griefs, however deep they run.
The law is for the good of everyone,
Not just the satisfaction of the few.
Now, leave me. There is much I have to do.

[Lord Emmerich bows and exits, leaving the Duke alone with his maps and his worries. He stares down at the lines showing the roads leading out from his city.]

[SCENE END]

ACT I
SCENE III

[A desolate road under a grey, unforgiving sky. Wind whistles through sparse, skeletal trees. VALERIUS, a young nobleman with a haunted, proud face, is walking, leading his horse, which has a slight limp. His clothes are dusty and his expression is grim. His squire, LUCIAN, a youth of similar age but of less refined bearing, walks beside him.]

LUCIAN
We should have stopped at that last village, sir.
The beast is tired, and my own feet demur
At every stone. This wind has teeth. At least
An inn would offer shelter.

VALERIUS
For man and beast who bear the Duke’s decree?
They’d sooner set the dogs on you and me.
Did you not see the way the blacksmith stared?
The proclamation has been well declared
In every hamlet. I am plague and blight.
To offer me a stable for the night
Is to court trouble with the Duke’s own men.
No, we must press on. We will make a den
Among the rocks when darkness overtakes us.
There is no comfort here. The world forsakes us.

LUCIAN
It is not right. A duel is not a crime.
You met him as a gentleman. The time
And place were set. He drew his sword as well.
How could you know the fatal blow that fell
Would be your own? He called your honor cheap.
A man must answer insults that run deep.

VALERIUS
[Stops and turns to Lucian, his voice low and intense.]
Do you think that I celebrate this, Lucian?
That in my heart I am a happy man?
I see his face when I attempt to sleep.
The final look of shock, the wound so deep.
I did not wish him dead. I wished him shamed.
I wished my family’s honor was proclaimed
And his was humbled. But my anger’s heat
Forged me a sword I could not then defeat.
It moved my hand. It struck a mortal blow.
And now a shadow follows where I go.
Corvinus’ ghost, or maybe just my own.
I am a man who reaps what he has sown.

LUCIAN
His mother’s grief is what has poisoned this.
She has the Duke’s ear. Her every whispered hiss
Becomes a roar of judgment in the court.
This banishment is her own cruel sport.

VALERIUS
Lady Cassian. I knew her as a child.
She gave me sugared figs. Her look was mild.
And now her heart is iron, and her will
Is what has driven me to this bare hill.
I do not blame her. If I had a son,
And saw his life’s thread prematurely spun
And cut, I would seek ruin for the hand
That held the shears. I think I understand.
But understanding is a meager meal
When hunger gnaws and frozen fingers feel
The coming frost. We must keep moving on.
Our future lies beyond the coming dawn,
In some new land where Valerius is not
A name for murderer, a cursed spot.

[He pats his horse’s neck and begins walking again. Lucian sighs and follows, pulling his thin cloak tighter against the wind.]

LUCIAN
Then let us walk until our boots give way.
South. Is it always south we go?

VALERIUS
Yes. South. Towards the sun. Perhaps its ray
Will someday warm my blood again. Away.
We must be far away by morning’s light.
There are more things to fear than just the night.

[They trudge onward, two small figures against a vast and empty landscape.]

[SCENE END]

ACT I
SCENE IV

[A thicket of woods by a crossroads. It is dusk, the light failing. MARCUS, GARRICK, and FINN are dismounted, hidden from the road. Garrick is a large, brutish man, sharpening a long knife on a whetstone. Finn is thin and watchful, saying nothing. Marcus studies the ground.]

GARRICK
The trail is cold. We’ve ridden half the day
And seen no sign. Perhaps he went the way
That leads toward the coast. A man in flight
Would seek a ship to vanish in the night.
This southern road is plain and obvious.
He is no fool. He would not travel thus.

MARCUS
A man in flight is not a fox who knows
Each secret path, each hole where he can go.
He is a startled deer that runs from sound.
He takes the widest path on open ground.
The Duke’s decree was for the southern border.
His mind, compelled by that official order,
Will drive him south. He thinks his safety lies
In obeying. He does not surmise
A second judgment follows on his heel.

GARRICK
[Tests the blade on his thumb.]
I hope you’re right. My patience starts to feel
Worn thin. This waiting chafes. I want the feel
Of work, not endless riding. For this gold,
My hand is eager and my heart is bold.
Let’s find him, do the business, and turn back.
The taste of tavern ale is what I lack.

MARCUS
Be silent, Garrick. Your loud mouth is a bell
That tells the world our purpose. Finn, go tell
Me what you see. Your eyes are better than
This oaf’s ambition. Scan the path.

[Finn nods, and without a word, melts into the deeper shadows, moving toward the edge of the road. Marcus turns back to Garrick.]

MARCUS
Remember what the lady said. No trace.
A quick dispatch in some forgotten place.
We are not bandits robbing for a purse.
We are a consequence. A walking curse
Sent by a mother’s love. Do you see that?
This is not murder. It is tit for tat.
A balancing of some celestial scale.
So when the moment comes, you must not fail
To be as swift and silent as the grave
We mean to give him. Nothing is to save
Him from his fate. And nothing is to show
That we were ever here.

GARRICK
I know. I know.
You talk as if I were a new-weaned boy.
The taking of a life is my chief joy
And my profession. I need no fine speech
To do my work. Just put him in my reach.

[Finn returns as silently as he left. He holds up a small object in his palm.]

FINN
A silver button. From a riding coat.
The mud is fresh. Not half an hour remote.
It bears a crest. A wolf’s head, and a spear.

[Marcus takes the button and examines it in the fading light.]

MARCUS
The crest of Valerius. He was here.
And with a lame horse, by the tracks I see.
He will not get much farther. He will be
An easy quarry now. He’ll look for shelter.
An inn, a barn, a place to brave the welter
Of the coming storm. Mount up. We ride again.
The hunt is almost over for our men.

[Garrick grins, sheathing his knife with a satisfied rasp. Finn is already moving toward the horses. Marcus stares at the button for a moment longer, his face impassive, before tossing it into the bushes. They mount their horses and move out, their dark shapes quickly swallowed by the growing night.]

[SCENE END]

ACT II
SCENE I

[A chamber in the Cassian estate. It is dimly lit by candles. LADY CASSIAN is seated, staring into a fireplace where a low fire burns. She has not changed from her mourning clothes. The DUCHESS ISABELLA, wife of Duke Alaric, is announced by a servant and enters. She is a gentle, poised woman, her expression one of careful sympathy.]

DUCHESS ISABELLA
My lady Cassian. Forgive my late
Intrusion on your grief. I know the weight
Of sorrow is a burden best borne private.
But I could not stay silent. I derive it
As my duty, and my friendship, to attend
And offer what small comfort I can lend.

LADY CASSIAN
[Without turning.]
Comfort is a word for lesser pain.
A child’s scraped knee. A harvest lost to rain.
It has no meaning here. This is not pain.
It is an amputation of the soul.
A part of me is gone, and I am whole
No longer. But I thank you for your call.
It is a kindness. Though a kindness all
In vain.

DUCHESS ISABELLA
[Approaching her cautiously.]
I know. No words can build anew
The house of joy that has been torn from you.
But faith can be a shelter from the storm.
The good Lord tests us, keeps our spirits warm
With trials, so that we may turn to Him.
Corvinus is with angels, and the dim
And dusty world he left is not his care.
He is at peace.

LADY CASSIAN
[Turns now, her eyes burning.]
He is at peace? Where?
In some cold plot of earth? My son was fire.
He was the beat of life, a grand desire
For all the world could offer. Do not speak
To me of angels and of spirits meek.
He should be here. He should be breathing, laughing.
Not some pale ghost in heavenly halls, quaffing
The nectar of the blessed. I want my son.
Not your pale comforts for a race that’s run.

DUCHESS ISABELLA
His memory lives on. And in God’s time,
The sharpest edge of loss will lose its crime
Against your heart. The Duke, my husband, feels
Your sorrow deeply. What his law reveals
Is not a lack of care, but his great need
To keep the peace, to stop the bitter weed
Of vengeance from o’errunning all the land.
He asks you, for the realm, to understand.

LADY CASSIAN
The realm. The peace. The law. These are the words
That men invent to blunt the points of swords
They are too scared to wield. Your husband’s law
Is a convenience, a brittle straw
For him to grasp at. He fears blood. He fears
The anger of the great houses. His tears,
If he has any, are for his own state,
Not for my boy, who met a coward’s fate.

DUCHESS ISABELLA
It was a duel, not a coward’s strike.
A tragic outcome, born of pride alike
In both the men.

LADY CASSIAN
And so the killer walks?
He breathes the air, he laughs, he dreams, he talks,
While my son’s mouth is filled with common clay?
The Duke’s law lets the wolf go on his way
And fines the shepherd for the missing sheep.
My law is older. It is buried deep
In every mother’s heart since time began.
A life for a life. It is a simple plan.
Your husband’s plans are tangled, weak, and grey.
Now, if you please. I wish to be alone to pray.

DUCHESS ISABELLA
To pray for what, my lady? For your son?

LADY CASSIAN
No. He is past all praying. That is done.
I pray for justice. Swift and sharp and true.
A prayer I have no doubt will be seen through.

[The Duchess Isabella looks at her, a flicker of alarm in her eyes. She sees the unyielding resolve in Lady Cassian’s face and knows her mission of peace has failed. She gives a small, formal curtsy and withdraws, leaving Lady Cassian to her dark vigil by the fire.]

[SCENE END]

ACT II
SCENE II

[The common room of a shabby, remote inn called The Traveller’s Rest. A smoky fire gutters in the hearth. VALERIUS and LUCIAN sit at a rough wooden table in a corner. Valerius drinks from a cup of wine, his movements slow. Lucian is eating a piece of bread.]

LUCIAN
This place is grim, but it is better far
Than sleeping out beneath a hostile star.
The innkeeper asked nothing, took our coin.
Tomorrow, with the horse’s leg re-joined
By rest, we can be miles from here. We’re safe
For one more night.

VALERIUS
Are we? This world’s a strafe
Of unseen dangers. Every friendly face
Could hide a spy. Each quiet, resting place
Could be a trap. The Duke’s men might still seek
To follow me, to make sure I am weak
And broken by my exile. Or perhaps
The Cassian family has laid its traps.
They are not ones to let a slight go by.
They have long arms and an unblinking eye.

LUCIAN
You think too much on it. We are small fish
Now, swimming in a river. Their one wish
Is that you vanish. We are doing so.
Drink up your wine. Let your dark spirits go.
Think of the south. They say the sun is warm,
The wine is rich, and life has its own charm
Apart from all these feuds of ice and stone.
We can begin again, just we alone.
Find service with a new lord, make a name
That is not stained by this unfortunate fame.

VALERIUS
[A small, sad smile touches his lips.]
You have a hopeful heart, my friend. I thank
You for it. It is like a sturdy plank
Across a chasm. Yes. A new life.
Perhaps I’ll find a home, and take a wife
Who does not know my history, my brand.
A farmer’s daughter in a sun blessed land.
And I will learn to till the soil, and forget
The feel of steel, the burden of this debt.
[He raises his cup.]
To a new life, then. Free of ghosts and pride.
Where a man’s worth is not in how one died,
But in the way he lives.

[As he speaks, the inn door creaks open. MARCUS, GARRICK, and FINN enter. They are cloaked and travel stained. They do not look at Valerius, but move to the bar to speak quietly with the innkeeper, who glances nervously toward Valerius’s table. Garrick’s hand rests on the hilt of his sword. Finn positions himself near the door, a silent sentinel. Marcus turns and his eyes meet Valerius’s across the room. There is no mistaking the cold purpose in his gaze.]

LUCIAN
[His voice a whisper.]
My lord. These men.

VALERIUS
[Slowly puts down his cup. His hand moves to rest near his own sword.]
I see them, Lucian. It appears my toast
Was premature. We have a different host
Tonight than we expected. Stay behind me.
Do nothing foolish. Let us see what tree
Has borne this bitter fruit.

[Marcus walks slowly toward their table. Garrick and Finn remain where they are, cutting off any escape. The few other patrons in the inn grow silent, sensing the sudden, deadly tension.]

MARCUS
Lord Valerius.
A long ride you have had. We found it thus
A wearying chase. But all things have an end.
I have a message for you that I must send.

VALERIUS
I know no message that requires three armed
And grim faced men to see it is unharmed
In its delivery. Who sends you, friend?
The Duke, to hound me to my journey’s end?

MARCUS
The Duke is not our master in this thing.
We do not serve the justice of a king.
Our justice is more intimate. It comes
Not from a throne, but from the silent rooms
Where grief resides. We serve the Lady Cassian.

[Valerius pales slightly, but his posture remains defiant. He stands up slowly.]

VALERIUS
I see. So this is how her passion
For revenge is stated. Not with law,
But with hired knives in inns. The final straw
Of honor has been broken here tonight.
I am a nobleman. I have the right
To face my foes in daylight, sword to sword.

GARRICK
[Laughs, a low, ugly sound.]
You’ll get a sword, my lord. Upon my word.
You’ll get more steel than you can stomach.

MARCUS
Quiet.
[To Valerius.]
The lady does not wish a public riot.
She does not wish a duel. She wishes you
A quiet grave. Your life is overdue.
She paid the debt, and we are here to claim it.

VALERIUS
Then you are murderers. You cannot name it
As aught besides. Come then, and do your work.
But know that in the shadows where you lurk,
Your own souls die with every blow you strike.

LUCIAN
[Drawing his own short sword.]
You will not take him!

[Before anyone can react, Lucian lunges at Garrick. Garrick, surprised but swift, sidesteps and brings the pommel of his own sword down hard on Lucian’s head. Lucian crumples to the floor, unconscious or dead. Valerius shouts in rage and draws his sword, lunging not at the brutish Garrick, but at the leader, Marcus.]

VALERIUS
Cowards! For that you’ll pay!

[The room erupts. Valerius and Marcus engage in a furious, desperate duel. Valerius is skilled, but he is one against three, and his spirit is weary. Marcus is a cold, efficient fighter, parrying every blow. From the side, Garrick draws his long knife and circles, looking for an opening. Finn blocks the door, ensuring no one interferes. The clash of steel echoes in the silent inn. Valerius fights with the fury of a cornered animal, but Marcus gives no ground. Finally, as Valerius is fully engaged with Marcus, Garrick lunges from the side, plunging his knife deep into Valerius’s ribs. Valerius cries out, staggering back. His sword drops from his grasp. He clutches his side, staring at Marcus with wide, betrayed eyes.]

VALERIUS
No honor… even in this…

[He sinks to his knees. Marcus steps forward, his face a mask of grim duty, and runs his sword through Valerius’s heart. Valerius collapses onto the floorboards and lies still. There is a moment of absolute silence, broken only by the crackle of the fire.]

MARCUS
[Wipes his blade on the dead man’s cloak.]
The debt is paid. The lady’s will is done.
His race of arrogance has now been run.
[To the terrified innkeeper.]
You saw a brawl. A drunken argument.
Nothing more. Is that well understood? Forget
Our faces. We were never here.

[He throws a heavy purse of coins onto the bar. Garrick prods Lucian’s body with his boot.]

GARRICK
What of the boy?

MARCUS
He saw too much. He cannot be a toy
For questioning. Make sure his breath is gone.
Then we will take them both before the dawn
And find a place where they will not be found.
A shallow grave on unconsecrated ground.
The lady was specific. Let them rot.
No priest to save them, and no stone to spot.

[Finn nods and moves to help Garrick with the bodies. Marcus stands over Valerius for a moment, his expression unreadable. He looks at the dead man’s face, peaceful now in death, and a fleeting, almost imperceptible shadow of something other than duty crosses his own. He quickly banishes it.]

MARCUS
Come. Our work here is finished. We ride north.

[The stage goes to black.]

[SCENE END]

ACT II

SCENE I

[The scene opens on a dark, windswept road. A crooked moon hangs in the sky, casting long, skeletal shadows from barren trees. MARCUS, GARRICK, and FINN are on horseback, riding at a steady, unhurried pace. The sound of hooves on hard earth is the only sound for a moment. GARRICK rides with a swagger, while MARCUS is rigid in the saddle, staring straight ahead. FINN is a shadow between them.]

GARRICK
The work is done. The northern air feels clean.
A cleaner job I think I have not seen.
He fell so fast, the proud and noble lord.
One little prick from my good knife, ignored
Until it bit him deep and stole his breath.
He looked so shocked to find a common death.

MARCUS
Be silent, Garrick. Gloat upon your prize
When we are paid and far from prying eyes.
The road has ears, the trees have sight to see.
Your boasting tongue is our worst enemy.

GARRICK
My tongue? My tongue did not complain or whine.
It was my knife that cut his lordly spine.
He fought you well, I grant the man his due.
But skill in arms will not see a man through
When odds are stacked and purpose holds the hand.
He died for pride, in his own foolish stand.
And for the boy, a rap upon the skull.
A life made brief, a story rendered dull.
A pity, that. He had a fire in him.
A foolish fire, now forever dim.

MARCUS
You took too much enjoyment in the deed.
It was a task, born of a client’s need.
No more, no less. We are the instruments.
Our feelings are but useless sentiments.
We take the coin, we see the target fall,
And then forget. We must forget it all.

GARRICK
Forget? I think not. I’ll remember well
The look he gave as he descended to his hell.
That high born soul, who thought himself so grand,
Brought low by dirt and my unwashed right hand.
These are the things that make the payment sweet.
The final tremor of a lord’s defeat.

[FINN, who has been silent, speaks in a low, rasping voice.]

FINN
A raven cried when his last breath was spent.
A single cry, on the night breezes sent.
A bad sign, that. A soul that will not rest.
A lingering curse to haunt the killer’s breast.

GARRICK
[Laughs, a harsh, ugly sound.]
A curse? From him? Let his pale spirit try.
I’ve butchered men beneath a darker sky
And slept as soundly as a swaddled child.
Your peasant fears are pitiful and wild.
The only spirits I have cause to fear
Are those that live in bottles of strong beer.
And as for curses, gold is my good charm.
A heavy purse will keep a man from harm.

MARCUS
Enough. Both of you. Your bickering is a chain
That scrapes and clinks and echoes on the plain.
Finn, keep your omens. Garrick, hold your tongue.
The night is old, but we are not yet done.
We ride to see the lady, take our gold,
And hear her words, that are both sharp and cold.
She will not wish to hear of ravens’ cries,
Nor of the pleasure sparkling in your eyes.
She wants to know the debt has been repaid.
The final stone upon his coffin laid.

GARRICK
And laid it is. So deep he’ll not be found.
He and the boy, tucked in the chilly ground.
No marker for them, just as she desired.
A fitting end for one so much admired.
He’s with the worms, his honor turned to dust.
His fine steel sword will soon be claimed by rust.

MARCUS
He had his honor. Let that be his stone.
A thing you kill but cannot ever own.
Now ride. The dawn is not so far away.
We must be gone before the light of day.

[They fall into silence again, spurring their horses on. Marcus’s face is tight, a mask of control, but his gaze seems fixed on something distant and unseen in the darkness ahead. The hoofbeats echo, fading as the stage goes to black.]

[SCENE END]

SCENE II

[The chamber of LADY ISOLDE. It is a room of severe and expensive taste. Tapestries depicting grim historical battles hang on the stone walls. A single, tall window looks out onto a manicured but lifeless garden under the moonlight. The furniture is dark, heavy wood. LADY ISOLDE stands by the window, her back to the room. She is tall, poised, her dark gown making her seem part of the shadows. Her lady in waiting, ELARA, a young, nervous woman, stands near the door.]

ELARA
My lady, it grows late. The fire is low.
Perhaps some rest? The morning cock will crow
Within the hour. You have not slept at all.
You pace and wait within this lonely hall.

ISOLDE
[Without turning.]
Sleep is a gift for those with peaceful minds.
A truce the weary soul but rarely finds.
Tonight, my mind is not at peace, Elara.
It is a field of battle, a sahara
Of waiting. Go to bed. Your duty’s done.

ELARA
I cannot rest while you, my lady, shun
Your own comfort. Is there no task for me?
A cup of wine? Some calming herbal tea?
Your hands are cold. Your face is pale as bone.
It is not right for you to wait alone.

ISOLDE
[She turns slowly. Her face is beautiful but hard, like a carving in ivory. There are no soft lines.]
Alone is what I am. What I have been
Since treachery destroyed my house, my kin.
Do not mistake this vigil for a weakness.
It is a forging. In this mortal bleakness,
I have been hammered into what you see.
A will of iron. A final decree.
Tonight, a judgment that was long delayed
Is carried out. A promise that was made
Upon a tombstone will at last be kept.
A bitter harvest, sown while justice slept.

ELARA
Justice, my lady?

ISOLDE
Yes. A word you say
As if it were a game for children’s play.
But it is not. It is a sword, a fire.
It is the crest of a funeral pyre.
The man they call Valerius, the great,
The honorable, sealed my family’s fate
With pretty words and a betrayer’s kiss.
He built his life upon a dark abyss
He dug for us. He stole our name, our land.
You cannot hope to know or understand
The depth of it. The poison in the root.
Tonight, we pluck the foul and bitter fruit.

ELARA
The men you sent… they are… of low repute.
Their work is violence, their manner brute.
To deal with such… it troubles my poor heart.
To see you play this dangerous, dark part.

ISOLDE
Then guard your heart, and do not look too deep.
Some things are better left to lie asleep.
A surgeon’s knife is not a gentle tool,
But it can cut away the rot. The fool
Is one who lets the sickness spread and grow
For fear of spilling blood. Now, you must go.
Their horses I will hear upon the stone
When they arrive. And I will meet them. Alone.

ELARA
Yes, my lady.

[Elara curtsies, her eyes filled with a mixture of fear and awe. She exits quietly, closing the heavy door behind her. Isolde turns back to the window, her hands clasped tightly before her. She does not move, a statue of perfect, chilling patience.]

ISOLDE
The debt is paid. Or soon it will be so.
A seed of vengeance, planted long ago.
Come, darkness. Bring me news. Bring me my peace.
Bring me his death. And my release.

[The stage goes to black.]

[SCENE END]

SCENE III

[The same chamber, a short time later. LADY ISOLDE stands by the massive fireplace, where the embers now glow a dull red. The door opens and MARCUS, GARRICK, and FINN enter. They are travel stained, and their presence seems to soil the pristine coldness of the room. Garrick looks around with a greedy, appraising eye. Finn stays near the door, a sentinel of shadow. Marcus walks to the center of the room and stops, his gaze meeting Isolde’s.]

ISOLDE
You are returned. The journey was a swift one.

MARCUS
We do not waste the hours, my lady. None.
We did as you instructed. We were there
Before the moon had climbed the highest stair
Of heaven.

ISOLDE
[Her voice is level, devoid of emotion.]
And the outcome? Do not speak in rhyme
Or riddle. I have had enough of time.
I want the fact. Is it concluded? Yes or no.

MARCUS
It is concluded. Your intended foe
Will trouble you no more. Valerius is dead.

[A flicker of something, perhaps relief, perhaps triumph, crosses Isolde’s face. It is gone as quickly as it came.]

ISOLDE
Dead. It is a small word for so much dread.
And tell me how. I wish to know the way
The final act of his life’s tragic play
Was brought to its conclusion. Was it clean?
A duel of honor? A more sordid scene?

GARRICK
[Steps forward, unable to restrain himself.]
He fought like a trapped wolf, my lady. Brave.
But bravery won’t keep a man from the grave.
Marcus engaged him, steel on ringing steel.
A pretty dance. But it was not quite real.
The final blow was not some fencing art.
It was my knife that went straight to his heart.
Or near enough. It let his lifeblood out.
It ended all his noble, proud self doubt.

[Isolde’s cold gaze shifts to Garrick. He falters slightly under its intensity.]

ISOLDE
I asked for a report, not for a boast.
You are the hand. He is the mind. At most
You are a weapon, pointed and then used.
A tool’s pride is a thing I am not amused
To witness.
[Her eyes return to Marcus.]
You. Continue. Was he alone?

MARCUS
He was not. A boy was with him. Barely grown.
His squire or ward. He tried to intervene.
He drew a sword and made a foolish scene.

ISOLDE
A boy? You did not mention this before.
A loose thread can unravel a whole war.
What of him? Did he flee? Or did he see
Your faces? Tell me. Speak with honesty.

GARRICK
I struck him down. A tap is all it took.
He fell without a second glance or look.
A fragile thing. We took him with the lord.
His tale is finished. That you have my word.

ISOLDE
And where are they? The bodies must not be
A source of questions for eternity.
A martyr’s tomb can grow a bitter creed.
I want them gone. I want to plant no seed
Of future vengeance.

MARCUS
They are laid to rest
Where none will think to look. The earth’s cold breast
Is now their blanket. In a thistle patch
Beyond the town, beneath a crumbling thatch
Of an old sheepfold. No one walks that ground.
They will not be discovered. Not be found.
We left no trace. The innkeeper is paid.
His fear of us is stronger than his trade.
He will say nothing.

ISOLDE
Good. That is well done.
The long night ends. A new day has begun.
[She moves to a heavy wooden chest and unlocks it. She removes two heavy purses.]
This was our bargain. The remainder of your fee.
Your work is finished. You are free of me.
Ride out. Ride far. Forget my name, this place.
Let no man connect me to this disgrace.

[She hands one purse to Marcus, who takes it with a nod. She offers the other to Garrick, who snatches it eagerly.]

GARRICK
Disgrace, my lady? I would call it sense.
A righteous and a just recompense.
A pleasure to have served you in this thing.

[Isolde ignores him, her focus entirely on Marcus.]

ISOLDE
One final question. Did his last breath bring
A curse upon me? Did he speak my name
And call for vengeance from a holy flame?
What were his final words? I wish to know.

[Marcus hesitates for a fraction of a second. He remembers Valerius sinking to the floor, his eyes wide.]

MARCUS
He spoke of honor. How there was none. So.
He died believing he had been betrayed
By codes he lived by. He was not afraid.
Just… disappointed. Then the last breath fled.
Those were the final words the dead man said.

ISOLDE
[A strange, bitter smile touches her lips.]
Honor. He spoke of honor to the last.
The very word with which his doom was cast.
How fitting. He was ever such a fool.
To think the world obeys a single rule.
Very well. Your work is done. Be gone.
Do not be here to see the coming dawn.

[Marcus nods. He turns, and with a gesture to the others, they move to the door. Finn slips out first, then Garrick, who pauses to weigh the purse in his hand with a grin. Marcus is the last to leave. He stops at the threshold and looks back at Lady Isolde, who has already turned away from him to stare into the dying fire.]

[The stage goes to black.]

[SCENE END]

SCENE IV

[The rear of the inn. A pile of refuse, empty barrels, and straw. It is the hour just before dawn, a grey, misty twilight. The air is cold. LUCIAN is lying half covered by a discarded horse blanket, utterly still. For a moment, it seems he is part of the refuse. Then, the back door of the inn creaks open. The INNKEEPER, a stooped, terrified man, peers out. He holds a lantern, but its light is feeble against the gloom. He shuffles over to the place where the assassins dumped Lucian before carrying him and Valerius away. He sees nothing, and is about to retreat in relief when he hears a low moan from the refuse pile.]

INNKEEPER
[Whispering to himself.]
Spirits of morning, what was that I heard?
A stray dog whining? Or a wounded bird?
They said they’d take them. Take them far away.
And I’d be safe to see another day.
I saw naught. I heard naught. I do not know.
The price of silence pays for bread and snow…
For wood to burn… but not to warm the soul.
This fear has dug in me a burning hole.

[Another, louder moan. Lucian stirs, pushing aside the blanket. His head is caked with dried blood, and his face is pale, but his eyes flutter open. The Innkeeper gasps and stumbles back, dropping his lantern, which sputters and goes out.]

LUCIAN
[His voice is a weak croak.]
Valerius? Master? Where… the light is gone.
I feel so cold. Is it not yet the dawn?

INNKEEPER
Hush, boy! Be quiet! Do you want us dead?
They’ll hear you! Every word that you have said
Is like a nail hammered into my head!
I thought you gone! They swore your life was sped!

[The Innkeeper scrambles to Lucian’s side, his fear warring with a sliver of pity.]

LUCIAN
They? Who? I remember… a sword. A fight.
The big one… struck me. Stole away the light.
And Valerius… he drew his sword for me!
He fought them! Master! Where is he? Is he free?

[Lucian tries to sit up, his eyes wild with dawning horror and memory. The Innkeeper pushes him gently back down.]

INNKEEPER
He is at peace now, boy. His fight is done.
His final battle has been lost and won.
There is no freedom for him. Nor for you
If you remain. You must see this thing through
And live.

LUCIAN
No. No. He cannot be… they could not…
He was the finest man. The only spot
Of goodness in this world. They murdered him.
Those cowards… in the shadows, dark and grim.
I must go to him. I must…

[He begins to sob, a raw, wrenching sound of a boy’s heart breaking.]

INNKEEPER
There is no body here for you to find.
They took him. Left his memory behind.
And left you, too. An oversight, I think.
A careless killer on destruction’s brink.
They thought you dead. And dead is what you’ll be
If you stay here for anyone to see.

[The Innkeeper quickly looks around, then pulls a small loaf of bread and a waterskin from inside his coat.]

INNKEEPER
Here. Take this. It is not much, I know.
But you must run. Find some new place to go.
South. North. It does not matter. Disappear.
Forget this night. Forget what happened here.

LUCIAN
[His voice hardens, the tears stopping.]
Forget? You ask me to forget his face?
The man who gave me shelter, hope, and grace?
I will not run. I will not hide in shame.
I will find them. I will learn their name.
And hers.

INNKEEPER
Hers?

LUCIAN
I heard them. In the room.
They spoke of her, who authored his dark doom.
A lady. She who paid the blood-debt. Claimed
His life was forfeit. She will be the named,
The hunted. Tell me, did you hear them speak?
A clue, a direction for the one I seek!

INNKEEPER
[Shakes his head, terrified.]
I heard nothing! A brawl, that’s all I saw!
I am a simple man, I heed the law!

LUCIAN
[Grabs the innkeeper’s sleeve, his young face contorted with a man’s fury.]
You lie! Your fear is etched upon your skin!
You know more than the place this horror’s in!
They rode north. I heard the leader say it. North.
Before the darkness took me. From this day forth,
My life is but a weapon to be aimed.
Tell me what you know, or you will be blamed
And haunted by his ghost until you die!

[The Innkeeper flinches, looking at the boy’s burning eyes. His resolve crumbles.]

INNKEEPER
I… I heard a word. As one of them passed by.
He grumbled of the ride. A long way still.
To see the Lady of Blackwood… on the hill.
Blackwood Manor. That is all I know!
Now let me go, boy! Please, you have to go!

[He pulls his arm free and scrambles back toward the inn door.]

INNKEEPER
They gave me gold to keep my mouth well shut.
But you have eyes that see my spirit gut.
Take my advice. Let vengeance be. It eats
The soul. It promises but only cheats.
Live, boy. That is the best revenge of all.
Before your pride becomes your final fall.

[The innkeeper disappears inside, bolting the door. Lucian is left alone in the growing grey light. He slowly gets to his feet, clutching the bread and waterskin. He sways, but his stance is resolute. He looks to the north.]

LUCIAN
Blackwood Manor. On the hill.
My soul can starve. But my revenge I will
Feed till it’s strong. Valerius, you taught
Of honor, justice, battles to be fought
With clean hands and a pure and noble heart.
But I must learn a new and darker art.
I thank you for the bread. But it is hate
That I will feast upon to meet my fate.

[He wraps the ragged blanket around his shoulders like a cloak and stumbles out of the alley, a lone, determined figure against the bleak dawn.]

[STAGE END]
