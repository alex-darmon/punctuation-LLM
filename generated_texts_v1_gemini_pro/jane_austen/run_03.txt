He gave a low laugh, and regarded the volume in his hand with an air of prodigious satisfaction. “One can only wonder at the author’s perspicacity, Miss Vance. To have captured a sentiment so precisely, and with such feeling. I should very much like to make his acquaintance. Do you not think, Miss Finch, that he must be a man of uncommon sensibility?” He directed his gaze towards the corner where Catherine Finch sat, her needlework having fallen quite still in her lap. She started, and a faint colour rose in her cheeks. “I am hardly qualified to judge an author’s character by his words, sir. It is, I suppose, a well written verse.” “Well written! You are severe in your praise. For my part, I find it a work of very particular genius. It speaks of feelings one might imagine were known only to oneself. ‘The silent heart has secrets of its own.’ A powerful line, is it not? I fancy the author had a very specific silent heart in mind when he composed it.” He turned the page with a significant slowness, his eyes never leaving her face. Eleanor, much diverted by this display, saw the blush deepen on Miss Finch’s cheek, and perceived in her averted gaze and the slight tremor of her hand a most definite consciousness. This quiet, unassuming Catherine Finch was evidently the subject of some gentleman’s secret literary devotion. Mr. Audley, it was clear, had guessed the whole affair and took an impertinent delight in tormenting her with his discovery. “Indeed,” said Eleanor, playing along, “it must be a great comfort to any lady to know her own heart so well understood, even by a stranger.” “A stranger?” repeated Mr. Audley, with a smile of private meaning. “Ah, but that is the very question. Is he a stranger? Or is he, perhaps, an acquaintance who dares not speak, and so takes up the pen instead? One can only conjecture.” “There is little profit in such conjecture,” said Miss Finch, her voice so low it was almost lost. “The book is published for all to read. The meaning must be a general one.” “Perhaps, perhaps. But some of us prefer a more particular application. I should like to conjecture, for instance, what our good friends in Bath would make of this mystery. I dare say it is the talk of the Pump Room. I wonder if your aunt, Mrs. Allen, has formed any theory on the matter?” At the mention of Bath, Miss Finch’s discomposure was complete; she looked for a moment as if she might be quite overcome, and Eleanor felt a pang of compunction for her own part in the game. He was too merciless. She was about to change the subject when the parlour door opened, and Mrs. Finch announced the arrival of Mr. Davison. He entered with his usual gravity, his bow encompassing the whole room before his eyes settled, with a look of quiet concern, upon Miss Finch. “I hope I do not intrude,” he said, his steady gaze a marked contrast to Mr. Audley’s sparkling mischief. “I was passing and wished to inquire after your health, Miss Finch. You seemed fatigued at the concert last evening.” “So very good of you, Mr. Davison!” cried Mrs. Finch. “Do take a seat. We are all admiration for this new book of poems. You must read it. Mr. Audley believes the author must be a local gentleman, which is such a romantic notion, is it not?” Mr. Davison’s eye met Mr. Audley’s for a brief, unreadable moment. “I am not much given to romance, madam. I am more concerned with a cough I thought I heard last night. I hope, Miss Finch, you are taking care against the evening damp.” He spoke with a simple, earnest sincerity. Miss Finch looked up at him, and the agitated blush subsided, replaced by a look of something like gratitude. “You are very good, sir. It was nothing.” Mr. Audley, who had been momentarily silenced, closed the book with a soft snap. “Well, I am answered. If a gentleman’s most heartfelt effusions are to be answered with concerns about the evening damp, he had best put away his pen and purchase a stock of shawls instead.” Eleanor took the opportunity of his moving towards the window to whisper, “You are too plain, Mr. Audley. You will distress her to no purpose.” “I intend her to be distressed,” he whispered back, his smile returning. “It is the only way to make her sensible of her own power. She must learn that silent hearts do not remain secret forever.” “But you do not truly know anything for certain.” “I know that she spent a month in Bath, and that this volume appeared a month after she returned. I know that she reads it when she believes herself unobserved. That is knowledge enough for me. Leave her to her blushes. If she has a secret, she must bear the consequences of it.” “She is not the only one with a secret, I think,” remarked Eleanor, glancing towards the earnest and attentive Mr. Davison, who was now examining a potted geranium with an air of profound interest, though well within earshot of Miss Finch’s chair. “Perhaps not,” Mr. Audley allowed. “But his, I wager, is a far more tedious story.”

Mr. Davison, having completed his inspection of the geranium, turned his attention back to the company. His expression was one of perfect composure, yet Eleanor felt that his brief study of the plant had been a strategic retreat, a moment taken to assess the altered atmosphere of the room. He did not address Mr. Audley’s last remark directly, but instead spoke to Mrs. Finch.

“The damp is a great enemy in these houses, madam,” he said, his voice calm and even. “Especially where there is a delicacy of constitution. I have always found that a flannel waistcoat is of more service than all the poetry in the world when a chill is in the air.”

Mrs. Finch, who considered any talk of undergarments a matter of extreme private delicacy, gave a little flustered laugh. “Oh, Mr. Davison! You are so very practical. But surely a little romance is good for the soul. Think of it, a secret poet in our very midst! It is almost as thrilling as the talk of that highwayman on the London road.”

“I believe the poet is a lesser threat to the general welfare, madam,” Mr. Davison replied, with the ghost of a smile. He then turned to Miss Finch, his manner softening. “But you are quite recovered from your fatigue?”

“Quite recovered, I thank you, sir,” she murmured, her eyes fixed upon her lap. It was clear to Eleanor that Mr. Davison’s steady presence acted as a shield. He had brought the conversation down from the dangerous heights of poetic passion to the safe, flat ground of weather and health. He was a wall, plain and sturdy, against which Mr. Audley’s sparkling waves of insinuation broke in vain.

Mr. Audley watched this exchange with an air of theatrical resignation. He strolled back from the window and picked up his hat and gloves. “I see I am defeated,” he declared, his tone light but with a definite edge. “The forces of flannel and common sense are arrayed against me. I shall retreat before I am advised to take a curative broth for my overwrought imagination.” He bowed to Mrs. Finch. “Your servant, madam.” He gave a slighter, more formal bow to Miss Finch. “I trust your cough will not worsen.” His gaze lingered on her for a fraction of a second, and in that moment, Eleanor saw not mischief, but a flicker of genuine, almost frustrated, curiosity. He was a man who delighted in solving puzzles, and this one was proving unexpectedly resilient.

Eleanor rose to take her leave as well, thanking Mrs. Finch for her hospitality. As they stepped out into the bright, cool air of the street, Mr. Audley was silent for the first several paces. Eleanor, expecting a triumphant or witty summary of the encounter, was surprised by his contemplative mood.

“Well,” he said at last, not looking at her but at the grey stone of the opposite houses. “Mr. Davison is a formidable opponent.”

“An opponent? I thought him a gentleman showing a proper and kind concern for a friend.”

“It is the same thing,” Mr. Audley said, turning to her with a wry smile. “He wishes to keep her safe. I wish to see her free. His method is to wrap her in shawls and silence. Mine is to hand her a sword and see if she will use it.”

“A sword? You call this relentless teasing a sword? You mistake your own amusement for a noble purpose, I believe. You saw how you distressed her. Her hands were trembling.”

“And did you not see how she composed herself the moment he entered the room? How she looked to him for protection? He is her sanctuary, which means she is his prisoner. A willing one, perhaps, but a prisoner nonetheless. She has a fire within her, Miss Thorpe, a fire that can produce such lines as, ‘The quiet heart, a cage of gold, where unheard melodies are told.’ She writes of cages because she lives in one. Mr. Davison is merely the kindest of her gaolers.”

Eleanor was taken aback by the sudden passion in his voice. He had quoted from the book, a line she herself had admired for its painful accuracy. His perception was more than mere mischief; it ran deeper, and she found it both unsettling and compelling.

“And you believe your method of rattling the bars of her cage is the best way to help her?” she asked, her tone softening.

“It is the only way. To be told you are a poet is a challenge. To be told you have a cough is an excuse to remain indoors. Which is the greater compliment to her spirit? Which assumes she is a creature of intellect and which a mere bundle of frailties?” He stopped and faced her fully on the pavement, forcing a passing chairman to swerve his sedan chair with a grunt of annoyance. “Tell me, Miss Thorpe, if you had written such a volume, would you wish it to be ignored out of a misplaced sense of delicacy? Would you not, in some small, secret part of your soul, long for someone to see through the disguise and recognise the truth of you?”

His question was so direct, so personal, that Eleanor found herself unable to answer immediately. She thought of her own private sketches, the drawings she made of people and landscapes that she never showed to anyone, fearing they would be judged amateurish or presumptuous. The idea of someone seeing them, and understanding them, understanding her through them, was at once terrifying and deeply desirable.

“I… I cannot say,” she answered honestly. “I am not a poet.”

“No,” he said, his gaze intent. “But you are a critic. A very fine one. I see you watching, weighing, judging. You judged me a brute in that parlour, and you were not entirely wrong. But you misjudge my motives. I am not her enemy.”

They resumed their walk, the silence between them now filled with a different sort of tension. It was no longer the easy rapport of two people sharing a joke, but the more complex connection of two minds that had suddenly glimpsed the workings of the other. Eleanor felt as though a door had been opened, revealing a view she had not expected. Mr. Audley was not simply a rogue; he was a rogue with a philosophy.

A few days later, Eleanor accompanied her aunt to the circulating library in Milsom Street. The library was a favoured hub of Bath society, a place where one could procure the latest novel, and, more importantly, the latest gossip. The room was abuzz with low chatter, the rustle of turning pages, and the clink of subscribers’ tokens. Mrs. Allen was immediately drawn into a conversation with a lady whose daughter was suffering from a most peculiar nervous ailment, and Eleanor was left to browse the shelves.

Her eyes, almost of their own accord, found the slim blue volume of *Echoes of the Heart*. There were several copies, a testament to its popularity. She took one down, its cover cool and smooth beneath her fingers. As she did so, she heard a familiar voice, raised in confident pronouncement.

It belonged to Mrs. Partridge, a woman whose chief occupation was the management of everyone else’s affairs.

“I have it on the very best authority,” Mrs. Partridge declared to a small, captive audience, “that the author is none other than Lord Byron himself, writing under a false name to test the waters, you see. The passion, the melancholy… it is his mark entirely!”

A collective murmur of impressed agreement went through the little group. Eleanor had to suppress a smile. To attribute the delicate, almost feminine sensibilities of the poems to the notoriously scandalous Lord Byron was an absurdity of the highest order.

“I think not, madam,” a calm voice interjected. Eleanor turned. Mr. Davison was standing by the counter, a sober-looking volume of sermons in his hand. Beside him, looking even more timid and withdrawn than usual in the public space, was Miss Finch. “The style is entirely different. The author of this work has a subtlety, a restraint, which Lord Byron, for all his genius, has never seen fit to cultivate.”

Mrs. Partridge looked affronted. “Indeed, Mr. Davison? And what makes you such an expert on the subject?”

“I have merely read both,” he said simply, as if that were a complete and unassailable argument. He then turned to Miss Finch. “The book you were seeking is not here. Perhaps we should try again tomorrow.” It was a clear attempt to extract her from the conversation, and from the public eye.

But it was too late. The subject had been raised, and at that very moment, Mr. Audley entered the library, his arrival timed with the unerring instinct of a predator sensing a wounded animal. He surveyed the scene in a single glance, his eyes lighting up with delight as he saw all the players assembled.

“Good morning, ladies, Davison,” he said, with a cheerful bow. “Are we discussing the great literary mystery of the age? Mrs. Partridge, I cannot allow you to claim Lord Byron. His sorrows are of a far more theatrical, and I daresay expensive, variety. The author of *Echoes* grieves over a lost glove, not a lost reputation.”

He walked towards the group, his presence immediately shifting the room’s centre of gravity. He picked up the copy of the poems that Eleanor was still holding, his fingers brushing hers as he took it.

“Ah, Miss Thorpe, you too are a student of the anonymous heart.” He opened the book, seemingly at random, though Eleanor suspected nothing he did was ever random. “What do we think of this one? ‘The Crescent Moon.’ It speaks of watching the lights of the Upper Rooms from a window far below, of hearing the distant music of the ball, a pleasure tasted only by the ear, not the foot. A very particular sort of Bath melancholy, is it not? One might almost think the author had stood at just such a window, perhaps recovering from a chill, and watched the happier world go by.”

He did not look at anyone as he spoke, his gaze fixed on the page. But the effect was electric. Eleanor saw Miss Finch flinch, a tiny, almost imperceptible movement. Mr. Davison’s hand moved to rest lightly on her elbow, a gesture of both support and restraint. She remembered now that Miss Finch’s visit to Bath had been curtailed by a sudden illness. The poem was not just a flight of fancy; it was a memory, thinly veiled.

Mrs. Partridge’s eyes widened. “The Upper Rooms! Why, that is specific. Perhaps it is someone who lives on Milsom Street itself! Or in the Royal Crescent!” Her mind was off, chasing a new and more promising scent.

Mr. Audley closed the book gently. “Who can say? The author is clever. He, or she, leaves clues that only a few will understand. It is a game, of a sort. A private letter written to the world.” He placed the book back on the shelf, his work for the day apparently done. He had not accused anyone. He had merely held up a mirror, and for one brief, terrible moment, Miss Finch’s reflection had been perfectly, undeniably clear.

Mr. Davison’s face was a mask of polite neutrality, but his jaw was tight. “If you will excuse us,” he said, his voice low and firm. “Miss Finch is not well. We must be going.” He guided her towards the door, a shepherd protecting his most vulnerable sheep from a particularly clever wolf. Miss Finch did not look back.

As they left, a silence fell over the little group. Even Mrs. Partridge seemed momentarily subdued, as if sensing she had been a pawn in a game far more subtle than her own. Eleanor found she was holding her breath. She looked at Mr. Audley, who was now idly examining a map of the city, the picture of innocence.

“You are cruel,” she said, her voice barely a whisper, for his ears only.

“I am a truth-seeker,” he corrected her, his voice equally low. “There is often a degree of cruelty in the truth. Did you see his face? Davison’s? He knows. I am certain of it. He knows her secret, and his sole purpose is to help her keep it buried. He would smother her talent with his kindness.”

“And you would expose it to the harsh judgment of the world, for your own sport!”

“Not for sport,” he said, turning from the map to look at her. His eyes were serious now, all trace of mockery gone. “For its own sake. A voice like that deserves to be heard, not muffled in a sickroom. Think of the injustice, Miss Thorpe. A man may publish his thoughts, his passions, his very soul, and be celebrated for it. A woman does the same, and she must hide behind anonymity, for fear of being thought forward, or unwomanly, or, heaven forbid, of having actually experienced the feelings she writes about. It is a monstrous hypocrisy.”

He gestured around the library, at the shelves filled with books predominantly written by men. “This is the world’s great conversation. She has earned a place at the table, and yet she is hiding in the kitchens, terrified someone will notice she is not merely washing the dishes. I, for one, intend to pull out her chair and announce her name.”

Eleanor stood, silenced by his unexpected vehemence. He had, in a few sentences, articulated a frustration that she had felt but never dared to name. He was not just a tormentor; he was a champion, albeit a reckless and dangerous one. His methods were questionable, his manner infuriating, but his cause, she was beginning to realise, was strangely, unexpectedly, just. The game was no longer about a simple secret. It was about a woman’s right to her own voice. And Eleanor, despite her better judgment, found herself wanting to know who would win.

The silence that followed their departure was heavy and thick, like the air before a storm. It was Mrs. Partridge, naturally, who broke it. Her capacity for being subdued was remarkably finite. “Well, I never,” she declared, fanning herself with a new and vigorous energy. “Such a display. The poor dear, her nerves are quite shattered. It is what comes of too much thinking, I have always said. A woman’s constitution is not made for it.” She looked towards Mr. Audley, her expression a mixture of disapproval and a grudging, fascinated respect, as one might look at a snake charmer. “A rather pointed demonstration, Mr. Audley. Though I confess I am not entirely certain what point you were endeavouring to make.”

Mr. Audley turned his gaze from the map, his smile returning, though it did not quite reach his eyes this time. “Merely that great art can have a powerful effect on a sensitive soul, Mrs. Partridge. A compliment, in its way, to both the poet and the reader.”

“A compliment that nearly sends the reader into a swoon,” she sniffed. “I prefer a novel with a happy marriage at the end. It is much more settling for the digestion.”

Eleanor paid her no mind. Her thoughts were a whirlwind, catching on the sharp edges of Mr. Audley’s declaration. A champion. A dangerous one. He spoke of justice, yet his methods felt like a violation, a breaking and entering of a private, shuttered room. “And what will you do now?” she asked him, her voice still low. “Having fired your warning shot, do you intend to lay siege to the fortress?”

“A fortress of his own making,” Mr. Audley murmured, beginning to pull on his gloves. “Mr. Davison has built a very comfortable, very handsome prison for her, and convinced her it is a sanctuary. My only intention is to show her the key is in her own hand.” He met Eleanor’s gaze, and his was challenging, as if he were issuing her a dare. “The question is not what I will do, Miss Thorpe. It is what you believe ought to be done. Should a songbird be kept in a gilded cage for its own protection, or should it be allowed to fly, even with the risk of cats and hawks? Do not answer me now. Think on it. It is a question of some importance.”

With a polite bow to both ladies, he took his leave, the bell above the bookshop door tinkling softly to announce his exit. He left behind him a wake of disruption and a profound sense of unease. Mrs. Partridge was already speculating on which doctor Mr. Davison would call for Miss Finch, but Eleanor was no longer listening. She was thinking of the gilded cage, and of the fierce, wild world that lay beyond its bars.

The days that followed were quiet, yet the quiet was charged with unspoken things. Eleanor saw Miss Finch and Mr. Davison once, walking in the Crescent gardens. From a distance, they were a picture of gentle solicitude. He held a parasol to shield her from the mild afternoon sun; he pointed out a particularly fine bloom; his head was inclined towards her as if listening to a precious secret. But as Eleanor drew a little nearer, pretending to admire a different flower bed, she saw the reality was less idyllic. Miss Finch’s face was pale and strained, her eyes fixed on the gravel path before her. She was not speaking. She was merely being spoken to, a passive recipient of a steady, low stream of kindness that seemed to weigh upon her like a heavy cloak. Mr. Davison’s expression, when he thought no one was watching, was not one of simple affection. It was watchful, anxious, the face of a man guarding a fragile, priceless treasure that might crumble to dust at the slightest touch. It was the face of a loving jailer.

Eleanor found her sympathies swaying like a pendulum. When she thought of Mr. Audley’s arrogance in the bookshop, his casual cruelty in using Mrs. Partridge as his instrument, she felt a surge of loyalty towards the quiet dignity of Mr. Davison’s protection. He was, after all, only trying to shield a vulnerable woman from a world that had little patience for female genius. He was kind. He was decent. But then, she would recall the fire in Audley’s eyes, his talk of monstrous hypocrisy, of a great conversation from which half the world was excluded. She would look at her own modest watercolours, the safe, pleasing landscapes she was permitted to paint, and feel a sudden, sharp pang of kinship with the anonymous poet. What was the greater cruelty? To be exposed to the storm, or to be denied the sky altogether?

Her thoughts were interrupted by an invitation. A musical evening was to be held at the Assembly Rooms, a grand affair that promised string quartets and Italian arias. It was the sort of event the whole of Bath society would attend, a place to see and be seen. Eleanor knew instinctively that they would all be there. The players of this subtle drama would be gathered under one roof, and she felt a tremor of anticipation that was nearly indistinguishable from dread.

The Assembly Rooms were a blaze of candlelight and a cacophony of polite chatter. Fans fluttered like trapped birds, and the scent of perfume and beeswax hung in the air. Eleanor, standing beside her aunt, surveyed the room. She spotted Mrs. Partridge almost immediately, holding court near the refreshment table, her voice a distinct, carrying whisper. And then she saw them. Miss Finch and Mr. Davison were seated near the front, a little apart from the main throng. Miss Finch was dressed in a simple grey silk that made her look more ethereal than ever, a moth among butterflies. Mr. Davison sat beside her, a bulwark of quiet strength, his attention focused solely on her. He adjusted her shawl. He offered her a glass of water. He was a perfect portrait of devotion.

Then Mr. Audley entered. He did not make a grand entrance, but his presence seemed to alter the very temperature of the room. Heads turned. Conversations lulled for a moment before resuming with renewed vigour. He was dressed impeccably, and he moved through the crowd with an easy confidence that Eleanor found both admirable and infuriating. His eyes scanned the room, and when they found her, he gave a small, almost imperceptible nod, a gesture of complicity that made her cheeks grow warm. He was not looking for her, however. His true target was clear. His gaze settled on the couple in grey, and a hunter’s stillness came over him.

Eleanor expected a repeat of his performance in the bookshop, some direct and theatrical confrontation. But Mr. Audley, she was learning, was a more versatile strategist than that. He did not approach them. Instead, he made his way to a small group that included Sir John Middleton, a man known throughout the city for his literary pronouncements and his booming voice.

Eleanor discreetly moved closer, feigning an interest in a nearby potted palm.

“Sir John,” Mr. Audley began, his voice pitched to be heard by those nearby, “I was hoping for your opinion on a matter of some debate. The new collection of poems, ‘Echoes from a Shuttered Room’. Have you had the pleasure?”

Sir John, who had an opinion on everything, puffed out his chest. “A fine work, a very fine work indeed. Full of feminine sensibility, but with a surprising strength of line. The work of a true poet, whoever she may be.”

“She?” Mr. Audley said, with a hint of theatrical surprise. “You are convinced it is a woman? I have heard some speculate it is a man, writing under a female guise to capture a certain fashionable melancholy.”

“Nonsense!” Sir John boomed, precisely as Mr. Audley must have known he would. The sound carried. Several people turned to look, including Mr. Davison, whose back stiffened. “No man could write with such delicate insight into the private sorrows of the heart. It is a woman’s voice, I tell you. A voice of immense, if tragically hidden, talent. I would give a hundred guineas to know her name, to praise her in person for the gift she has given the world!”

Eleanor stole a glance at Miss Finch. She had gone utterly still. Her hands were clenched in her lap, her knuckles white. She was not looking at the speakers, but at the floor, as if the intricate pattern of the parquet might offer her an escape. Mr. Davison leaned closer, murmuring something in her ear, his hand hovering near her arm.

“A gift she seems reluctant to claim,” Mr. Audley continued, his voice smooth as silk. “One wonders why. Is it modesty? Or is it fear? Perhaps she has been told her gift is a sickness. Perhaps she has been encouraged to smother it, to hide it away lest it offend the world’s delicate sensibilities. What a tragedy that would be, Sir John. To have a voice like that, a voice that could move a nation, and be condemned to silence by the misguided kindness of a friend.”

Every word was a perfectly aimed dart. He never looked at them, yet his speech was directed at them as surely as if he had been standing before them. It was a public accusation disguised as a general debate. Eleanor felt a cold knot of anger and pity form in her stomach. It was brilliant. It was vicious.

She could not bear to watch any longer. Needing air, needing a respite from the suffocating tension, she made her way towards the long French windows that opened onto a small stone balcony. The cool night air was a relief. As she stood there, watching the distant lights of the city, a quiet voice spoke beside her.

“He is a cruel man.”

Eleanor turned to find Mr. Davison standing there. He must have seen her leave and followed her out. His face, in the pale moonlight, was etched with a profound weariness. He was no longer the polite guardian; he was a tired soldier in a long and losing war.

“He believes he is serving the cause of truth,” Eleanor offered, feeling a strange need to explain, if not defend, Mr. Audley’s position.

Mr. Davison gave a short, bitter laugh. “The truth? What does a man like that know of the truth of a person’s soul? He sees a talent and thinks of it as a public commodity, a thing to be paraded and celebrated. He does not see the vessel that holds it. He does not know how fragile it is.” He looked back through the glass at Miss Finch, who sat alone and exposed, a small grey island in a sea of colour and noise. “He talks of injustice. Does he know what it is to be so finely attuned to the world that its beauty and its cruelty are a constant, physical pain? To feel so much that the only way to survive is to build a wall around your heart? The poems are her release. They are the cracks in that wall through which the pressure escapes. To tear down the wall, to expose her to the world he champions, would not be to free her. It would be to destroy her.”

His sincerity was overwhelming. Here was the other side of the argument, presented not with intellectual flourish but with the raw pain of experience. He was not a jailer. He was a shield.

“She is not strong enough for the world,” he said, his voice dropping to a near whisper. “And so I must be strong enough to keep the world from her. Mr. Audley can call it a prison if he likes. But he does not have to live with the consequences if the walls should fall.”

Before Eleanor could form a reply, a small commotion erupted inside the ballroom. A woman gasped. A chair scraped against the floor. Mr. Davison’s head snapped around, his expression instantly transforming from weary sorrow to sharp alarm. He pushed past Eleanor and hurried back inside.

Eleanor followed. A small crowd had gathered around Miss Finch’s chair. She had not fainted, but she was trembling violently, her face ashen, one hand pressed to her chest as she struggled to breathe. Her small, beaded reticule had fallen to the floor, its contents spilling across the polished wood: a silver vinaigrette, a lace handkerchief, and a small, folded piece of paper.

Mr. Davison was at her side in an instant, his voice a low, soothing murmur as he knelt before her. “Clara,” he said, the use of her first name a shocking intimacy. “Clara, breathe with me. It is all right. I am here.” He ignored the onlookers, ignored Sir John’s concerned blustering, ignored the triumphant, watchful stillness of Mr. Audley across the room. He had eyes only for her. With infinite gentleness, he helped her to her feet and began to lead her from the room, his arm a secure fortress around her.

The whispers started at once. Mrs. Partridge was already composing the narrative of a tragic, nervous collapse. As the crowd began to disperse, chattering about the unfortunate scene, Eleanor’s gaze fell upon the items still scattered on the floor. A footman was beginning to gather them, but his attention was clumsy. He picked up the vinaigrette and the handkerchief, but the small, folded piece of paper lay half hidden beneath the leg of the abandoned chair.

On an impulse she could not explain, Eleanor moved forward. With a swift, subtle movement, she stooped, her own handkerchief "accidentally" falling from her hand. As she retrieved it, her fingers closed around the folded paper. She straightened up, her heart hammering against her ribs, and slipped it into her own reticule.

No one had noticed. Mr. Audley was now engaged in a seemingly placid conversation, his face a mask of polite indifference, but Eleanor saw the hawk-like intensity in his eyes. He knew he had struck his target.

It was not until she was home, in the privacy of her own room, that Eleanor dared to look at what she had taken. Her hands trembled as she unfolded the paper. She expected a line of poetry, a fragment of some beautiful, melancholic verse.

But it was not a poem. It was a short, sharp, scribbled sentence, the handwriting jagged and desperate. It was not addressed to anyone, yet it felt like a scream.

It said: He will not let me publish the new one.

The silence of her room was a living thing, a vast, hollow space where the ticking of the mantel clock was as loud as a blacksmith’s hammer. Eleanor sat before her vanity, the folded paper lying on the polished mahogany surface. The candlelight caught the frantic scrawl of the ink, making the words seem to twitch with a desperate life of their own. *He will not let me publish the new one.*

It was a key. She felt it with a certainty that chilled her to the bone. It was the key to the gilded cage Mr. Davison had so sorrowfully described, the cage he guarded with a martyr’s heavy sigh. This was not about a delicate constitution or a mind too fragile for the world. This was about a voice being silenced. Mr. Audley had not caused Miss Finch’s distress with a cruel word or a shocking revelation. He had simply delivered a message, a confirmation of her imprisonment. The note was not an accusation; it was the final rattle of a chain.

Eleanor folded the paper carefully, its sharp creases a testament to the urgency with which it had been written. She slid it between the pages of a book of pressed flowers, a bland and sentimental volume no one, not even a curious housemaid, would ever think to open. The secret felt hot and heavy, a stolen jewel she did not know how to return or what to do with. Mr. Davison’s words echoed in her mind. *I must be strong enough to keep the world from her.* But the note suggested a different, more terrible truth. Perhaps he meant to keep *her* from the world, to keep her thoughts, her creations, her very soul, locked away where they could not be seen.

Sleep was a distant country she could not reach that night. Every shadow in the corner of her room seemed to hold the watchful stillness of Mr. Audley, every creak of the floorboards the hesitant step of Miss Finch. She saw again the scene in the ballroom: the triumphant gleam in Audley’s eyes, the fortress of Davison’s arm around his ward, and the small, pale face of the woman at the center of it all, a ghost at her own haunting.

The next morning brought with it a suffocating blanket of social decorum. It arrived in the form of Mrs. Partridge, who called upon Eleanor’s mother under the pretext of discussing the upcoming church bazaar but whose true purpose was to dissect the events of the previous evening. Eleanor was summoned to the drawing room, a reluctant witness to the postmortem.

“A terrible shame,” Mrs. Partridge declared, her voice a symphony of feigned sympathy and ravenous curiosity. “Poor, dear Miss Finch. Her nerves, you know. They have always been her affliction. A mind too finely wrought for this coarse world.”

Eleanor’s mother nodded sagely. “Mr. Davison is a saint. Truly, a saint. The patience he shows, the constant care. It is a burden few men would be willing to bear.”

“And that Mr. Audley,” Mrs. Partridge continued, lowering her voice conspiratorially. “I cannot say I care for him. He has the look of a man who enjoys stirring the pot simply to watch others get scalded. They say he was speaking with her just moments before her collapse. What could he possibly have said to such a delicate creature?”

Eleanor remained silent, stitching a small, pointless flower onto a piece of linen. The needle moved in and out, a tiny, repetitive act of order in a world that suddenly felt chaotic and false. She listened to them construct their story, a comforting narrative of female fragility and noble male sacrifice. It was a story told in every drawing room in the city, a tale that smoothed over the ugly, jagged edges of the truth. It was a lie, and every polite nod and murmur of agreement in the room felt like a betrayal of the woman who had scribbled that frantic message.

“Eleanor, dear, you were standing quite close,” her mother prompted. “Did you see anything?”

Eleanor looked up from her needlework, her expression carefully neutral. “Only what everyone else saw, Mother. Miss Finch appeared to be overcome. It was most unfortunate.”

Mrs. Partridge leaned forward, her eyes gleaming. “Was she frightened? Did Mr. Audley threaten her?”

“I could not say,” Eleanor replied, her voice cool and even. “His back was to me. He seemed to be speaking in a low voice. Then she grew pale. That is all.” She would give them nothing. She would not feed their gossip, and she certainly would not reveal the one piece of evidence that contradicted their entire comfortable worldview. The secret she kept was hers alone, a small, hard stone in the pit of her stomach.

When Mrs. Partridge finally departed, leaving a wake of speculation and sanctimonious pity, Eleanor knew she could not remain idle. The note was not just a piece of paper; it was a responsibility. But what was she to do? Confront Mr. Davison? He would dismiss her with his weary, patronizing sorrow. Approach Mr. Audley? He was playing a dangerous game, and she had no desire to be one of his pawns.

No, she had to understand. She had to know what it was that Mr. Davison was so desperate to suppress. If Miss Finch was a writer, then she must have published before. The thought was a sudden, brilliant flash of light in the fog of her confusion. She would find Miss Finch’s work. She would read her voice, even if she could not hear it.

That afternoon, on the pretense of needing new ribbons from the haberdasher’s, Eleanor directed her carriage to a far less fashionable part of town, to a street of cramped and dusty shops that catered to more intellectual pursuits. She found a bookseller’s establishment tucked between a watchmaker and a purveyor of maps, its windows filled with leaning towers of leather and paper.

The air inside was thick with the scent of aging glue and vanilla scented decay, a smell Eleanor found deeply comforting. An elderly man with spectacles perched on the end of his nose looked up from a massive ledger.

“Good afternoon, miss. How may I help you?”

Eleanor felt a sudden tremor of nervousness, as if she were a conspirator engaged in a clandestine act. “I am looking for a book of poetry,” she began, her voice quieter than she intended. “I believe the author’s name is Finch. A Miss Clara Finch.”

The bookseller’s eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. He peered at her over his spectacles. “Miss Finch? An excellent choice. Though not one I am asked for often these days. A singular talent. A bit… dark for most tastes.”

He shuffled from behind his counter and led her toward a dim corner of the shop. “She has only the one published volume, you know. Put out several years ago. There was talk of another, but it never materialized.”

He ran a gnarled finger along a row of slender spines. “Ah, here we are.” He pulled out a small, slim volume bound in dark green cloth. There was no decoration, only the title and the author’s name embossed in faded gold leaf. *The Shadowed Glass. By C. Finch.*

Eleanor took the book. It felt surprisingly heavy in her hands, as if the pages were weighted with more than just ink. She paid the bookseller, her heart thumping a strange, excited rhythm. He wrapped it for her in plain brown paper, and she carried the small parcel back to the carriage as if it were a bomb.

Back in the sanctuary of her room, she unwrapped the book with trembling fingers. The paper was thick and creamy, the typeface elegant. She opened to the first page and began to read.

It was not the sort of poetry she had expected. It was not filled with the gentle romanticism or pious sentimentality that graced the pages of ladies’ albums. It was sharp, strange, and breathtakingly beautiful. The poems were knives wrapped in velvet. They spoke of rooms with covered mirrors, of gardens where the flowers had no scent, of birds that sang only in the dark. There was a profound and aching loneliness in every line, but also a fierce, unyielding intelligence. She wrote of being a reflection, a specter in her own home, of watching the world through a pane of glass that warped and distorted everything it showed.

Eleanor read for hours, oblivious to the fading light outside her window. She felt as if she were not merely reading words, but listening to a voice, a soul laid bare on the page. This was the mind Mr. Davison sought to protect the world from. Not because it was fragile, but because it was powerful. Because it saw too much, and it spoke the truth of what it saw. To read these poems was to understand that Miss Finch was not a delicate invalid. She was a visionary, trapped in a life that sought to blind her. The “new one” was not just another book. It was another piece of her soul she was trying to send out into the world, and he, her guardian, her protector, was holding the door shut.

A week passed. The scandalous collapse of Miss Finch faded from the forefront of society gossip, replaced by a rumored elopement and a dispute over a new church steeple. But for Eleanor, the world had shifted on its axis. She carried the knowledge of Clara Finch’s genius and her silent suffering with her everywhere. She reread *The Shadowed Glass* until she had nearly memorized it. The book was her secret companion, a constant reminder of the cage she had glimpsed and the woman trapped inside.

She knew she had to do something more. Reading the poems was not enough. She had to breach the wall Mr. Davison had built. Her opportunity came unexpectedly, during a musicale at the home of Lady Danbury. The affair was crowded and overwarm. Eleanor, seeking a breath of fresh air, slipped out onto a stone terrace that overlooked the gardens. The cool night air was a relief. She was not alone. Leaning against the balustrade, a silhouette against the moonlit topiary, was Mr. Audley.

He turned as she approached, a faint, knowing smile on his lips. “Miss Croft. Escaping the caterwauling?”

“I was merely seeking a moment of quiet,” she replied, her tone crisp. She would not let him think he could draw her into his cynical orbit.

“Quiet is a rare commodity,” he said, his gaze intense. “Especially when one has a great deal to think about.”

They stood in silence for a moment, the sound of a strained soprano aria drifting from the house. Eleanor’s heart was beating far too quickly. This was her chance.

“You know Miss Finch’s work, do you not, Mr. Audley?” she asked, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands.

His smile widened, but it did not reach his eyes. “I consider myself one of its few true admirers. It is a crime that there is not more of it.”

“A crime?” she echoed, testing the word.

“What else would you call the suppression of genius?” he asked, turning to face her fully. The moonlight cast sharp shadows on his face, making him look both handsome and predatory. “What else would you call a man who keeps a nightingale in a box for fear its song is too melancholy for the world?”

Her breath caught in her throat. He knew. He knew that she understood.

“I do not know what you mean,” she said, the denial automatic and weak.

“Do you not?” he countered, taking a step closer. “I think you do. I saw you, Miss Croft. At the ball. I saw your handkerchief fall. A very fortunate accident.”

The blood drained from her face. He had seen everything. She was no longer an observer; she was a player in his game, whether she wished it or not.

“You used her,” Eleanor said, her voice low and laced with an anger that surprised her. “You caused her that distress deliberately. You passed her that note.”

“I passed her a confirmation of what she already knew,” he corrected, his voice losing its playful edge and taking on a harder, more serious tone. “I confirmed that her latest manuscript, which she contrived to send to a publisher through a sympathetic servant, had been intercepted and returned. To her guardian. He told her it had been rejected, that her work was unwanted. A lie, of course. The publisher was ecstatic. I simply told her the truth.”

The cruelty and the compassion of the act warred in Eleanor’s mind. He had hurt Clara, but he had armed her with the truth.

“Why?” Eleanor asked, the single word encompassing a dozen questions. Why her? Why this public spectacle? What is your purpose in all of this?

“Because Mr. Davison and I have a long and unpleasant history,” Audley said, his gaze drifting toward the dark garden. “And because I believe that art, true art, should not be held captive by the fears of lesser men. Davison does not fear for her sanity. He fears her clarity. He fears the things she writes because he fears they are about him. He has built a prison of pity and concern, and he is both its warden and its chief inmate.”

He looked back at her, his eyes searching hers. “She needs an ally, Miss Croft. Someone he will not suspect. Someone who sees past the fragile doll he presents to the world.”

Eleanor stood frozen. He was asking her to help. He, a man she instinctively distrusted, a cynical manipulator, was asking her to join his crusade. But it was not his crusade, she realized. It was Clara Finch’s.

Before she could answer, the glass doors to the terrace opened. Mr. Davison himself stepped out, his face a grim mask in the half light. He had not seen them at first, but then his eyes adjusted, and he froze. His gaze flickered from Audley to Eleanor, and a look of profound, cold displeasure settled on his features.

“Miss Croft,” he said, his voice perfectly modulated but laced with ice. “I had not realized you were acquainted with Mr. Audley.”

“We were just discussing the chill in the air,” Audley said smoothly, his smile returning, sharp as a razor.

Davison’s eyes remained on Eleanor. It was a look of appraisal, of suspicion, of warning. He did not need to say a word. The message was clear. He had seen them together, and he had drawn his own conclusions. The walls of the prison were expanding, and he was watching to see if she would try to scale them. He was no longer just Clara’s warden. He was now hers as well.

Eleanor felt the silence on the terrace stretch into a tangible thing, a viscous substance in which all three of them were trapped. Mr. Davison’s disapproval was not a fleeting emotion; it was a physical presence, a drop in temperature that had nothing to do with the night air. It radiated from him, a cold and heavy pressure.

“I was not aware an introduction had been made,” Davison said, his words clipped and precise. He took a deliberate step forward, subtly positioning himself between Eleanor and Audley, a human shield or a barricade. He was reclaiming his territory.

“Mr. Audley was kind enough to introduce himself,” Eleanor managed, her voice steadier than she felt. She would not be cowed into acting the part of a guilty schoolgirl caught in some transgression. She had done nothing wrong, a small, defiant voice insisted in her mind, even as every instinct screamed that she had stumbled into the very heart of a private war.

Audley smiled, a lazy, unbothered expression that seemed designed to provoke. “A brief acquaintance, Davison, nothing more. We were admiring your garden. It is so meticulously arranged. Not a single weed dares to show its face.”

The comment hung in the air, a thinly veiled metaphor. Davison’s jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. He ignored Audley completely, his attention fixed solely on Eleanor. It was a calculated dismissal, a way of showing both of them who held the power. “The evening has grown quite cold, Miss Croft. I would not want you to catch a chill. Allow me to escort you back inside.”

It was not a request. It was a command wrapped in the soft velvet of propriety. He offered his arm, a gesture of gentlemanly concern that felt like the closing of a manacle. Eleanor’s mind raced. To refuse would be an open declaration of defiance, an act of social suicide that would cement his suspicion and likely end her position in his household. To accept was to walk back into the prison, to cede this small patch of contested ground, and to leave Audley to his solitary amusement.

She had no real choice. With a slight inclination of her head, she placed her fingertips on his proffered arm. The fabric of his coat was fine and smooth, but she imagined she could feel the rigid tension of the muscle beneath it.

“Thank you, Mr. Davison,” she said.

As he turned her toward the light of the ballroom, Audley spoke again, his voice carrying easily in the stillness. “A pleasure to meet you, Miss Croft. I do hope we have the opportunity to continue our conversation. I find conversations about art, and its proper place in the world, to be most illuminating.”

Davison did not pause, but Eleanor felt the pressure of his hand increase fractionally on her arm. He guided her through the glass doors, back into the warmth and the noise of the party. The music, a lively waltz that had seemed so charming moments before, now sounded manic and hollow. The laughter of the guests grated on her nerves. The entire scene, the glittering chandeliers, the swirl of silk gowns, the clinking of crystal, felt like a preposterous lie. It was a beautiful, intricate cage, and she had just been reminded of the bars.

They did not speak as he led her through the throng. His grip was a constant, firm reminder of his authority. Heads turned as they passed. She saw the speculative glances, the whispered asides. She was the companion, the quiet observer, and to be seen on the arm of the master of the house in such a a way, with such a grim expression on his face, was to be the subject of immediate and intense curiosity. She felt her cheeks burn, not with shame, but with a hot, simmering anger. He was making a point, displaying his possession.

He led her to a small, unoccupied alcove near a towering floral arrangement. Only then did he release her arm. He stood before her, his height and posture creating an imposing barrier to the rest of the room.

“Mr. Audley,” he began, his voice low and devoid of any warmth, “is not a man whose company I would recommend for a young lady of your station, Miss Croft.”

“He seemed perfectly civil,” Eleanor replied, meeting his gaze. She would not look away. She would not be intimidated.

A flicker of something, perhaps surprise at her directness, crossed his face before the mask of cold displeasure settled again. “Civility is a cloak some men wear to hide a multitude of sins. Audley delights in disruption. He finds sport in the discomfort of others. He is a cynic and a manipulator, and his interests are rarely aligned with the well being of those he professes to befriend.”

The hypocrisy of his statement was breathtaking. He was describing himself, painting a perfect portrait of his own actions toward Clara. He was warning her against the fox while acting the part of the wolf.

“I shall bear your warning in mind, sir,” she said, the formal words feeling like ash in her mouth.

“I expect you to do more than that,” he said, his tone sharpening. “I expect you to heed it. Your role in this house is to be a companion to my ward. That is a position that requires discretion, loyalty, and an unimpeachable sense of propriety. It does not involve fraternizing with uninvited guests on dark terraces.”

The accusation was clear. He believed she had arranged the meeting, that she was already an agent of Audley’s. The walls were not just expanding; they were closing in, stone by cold stone.

“It was a chance encounter, Mr. Davison,” she said, her voice remaining level. “Nothing more.”

He studied her for a long moment, his eyes like chips of flint. He was weighing her words, searching for the lie. Whether he found it or not, she could not tell. “See that it remains that way,” he said finally. He gave a curt nod, a gesture of dismissal, and turned, melting back into the crowd of his guests as if the entire exchange had never happened.

Eleanor stood alone in the alcove, her heart hammering against her ribs. The scent of lilies from the floral arrangement was cloying, funereal. She felt a profound sense of isolation, more acute than any she had ever known. She had been warned. She had been threatened. Davison had drawn a line in the sand, and he had made it abundantly clear which side she was expected to stand on.

Her gaze swept the room, searching for Clara. She was nowhere to be seen. Had she been sent to her room? Locked in? Was she weeping, clutching the small piece of paper that held both her vindication and her despair? The need to know, to offer some small comfort, was a physical ache in Eleanor’s chest. But she was trapped. To seek out Clara now would be to confirm Davison’s worst suspicions.

She remained in her corner, forcing herself to sip a glass of untouched lemonade, to appear calm and composed. She watched him, Mr. Davison, as he moved through his party. He was the perfect host, all smiles and solicitous inquiries. He laughed with a portly gentleman, he bowed over the hand of a dowager, he charmed a young debutante. But Eleanor saw it now, the thing she had missed before. The smile never truly reached his eyes. There was a constant, vigilant quality to his gaze, a subtle scanning of the room, a need for absolute control. He was a performer, and his life was a meticulously staged play. Audley had not lied about that.

The hours crept by, each tick of the grand clock in the hall a small torment. Finally, the guests began to take their leave. Carriages were called, and the great house slowly emptied, the noise and light receding until only a hushed quiet and a skeleton staff remained. Eleanor waited, knowing she would not be dismissed until he was ready.

Eventually, he approached her again. The other guests were gone. The musicians had packed their instruments. The party was over.

“A successful evening, I believe,” he said, his voice carrying the false warmth of a host reviewing his triumph.

“Indeed, sir,” Eleanor replied.

“Clara became overwrought,” he said, his tone shifting to one of practiced, paternal concern. “The excitement was too much for her delicate nerves. I have had her maid give her a sleeping draught. She will be well rested by the morning.”

A sleeping draught. He had drugged his ward into submission. The cruelty of it, disguised as care, made Eleanor’s stomach clench. He was not just a warden; he was a chemist, mixing his own brand of compliance.

“I am sorry to hear she was unwell,” Eleanor said, keeping her face a neutral mask.

“Yes. It is a constant worry.” He paused, looking at her thoughtfully. “I have been thinking, Miss Croft. Perhaps part of the issue is that Clara has too much time for idle fancy. Her mind, when unoccupied, tends to drift toward morbid subjects. She needs a more structured and wholesome routine.” He looked directly at her, his eyes holding a new, insidious kind of proposition. “I believe it would be beneficial if you were to spend more time with her. Read to her from calming scripture, ensure she takes her walks only in the sunniest parts of the garden, supervise her embroidery. Keep her mind… pleasantly occupied. Your influence, I believe, could be a steadying one.”

There it was. The trap, sprung and waiting for her to step inside. He was not going to fire her. He was going to use her. He was appointing her as Clara’s personal jailer, tasking her with the very duties of suppression that Audley had described. He would use her perceived propriety and her dependent status to ensure Clara remained docile and contained. He was testing her loyalty, and the price of failure was clear.

“Of course, Mr. Davison,” Eleanor said, the words tasting like poison. “I will do whatever I can to assist Miss Finch.”

“Excellent,” he said, a real, thin smile of satisfaction finally touching his lips. “I am glad we have had this conversation. It has been most clarifying.”

He wished her a good night and ascended the grand staircase, his footsteps echoing in the cavernous hall. Clarifying. The word was a threat. He had clarified her position, her duties, her cage.

Eleanor waited until he was gone before she made her own way upstairs. The long corridor to her room was dark, lit only by a single gas lamp at the far end, which cast long, distorted shadows that seemed to writhe on the walls. She passed Clara’s door, a solid slab of dark, unforgiving wood. It was closed. Locked, she had no doubt. She could hear nothing from within, only the oppressive silence of the house.

Inside her own small, neat room, she did not light a candle. She went to the window and looked out at the dark garden where she had spoken with Audley. The moon had risen, casting a silver, ghostly light over the "meticulously arranged" flowerbeds. Not a weed in sight.

She stood there for a long time, the chill of the glass seeping into her palms. Davison’s words echoed in her mind, a litany of control and veiled menace. Audley’s words returned as well, sharp and illuminating. *He fears her clarity. He fears the things she writes.*

She had a choice. It was not a grand, heroic choice made on a public stage, but a small, silent one made in the dark of a hired companion’s room. She could obey. She could become the steadying influence, the pleasant jailer, reading scripture and supervising embroidery. She could earn her keep, ensure her own safety, and survive. She could watch as Clara Finch’s spirit was slowly, methodically smothered under a blanket of concern.

Or she could not.

She thought of Clara’s face in that moment of terrible, public revelation. The agony, yes, but also the brief, breathtaking flare of life, of pure, unadulterated feeling. She thought of a manuscript, intercepted and lied about. She thought of art held captive.

Mr. Davison believed he had clarified her position. In a way, he had. He had shown her the precise dimensions of her own cage, and in doing so, he had shown her the absolute necessity of breaking out. He thought he had recruited a warden. He had no idea that he had just armed an ally.

Her decision settled not with a crash, but with a quiet, firm click, like a key turning in a long rusted lock. She would not be his tool. She would not be his spy. She would be the person Audley had asked her to be. An ally. Someone he would not suspect.

But how? She could not confront Davison. She could not force her way into Clara’s room. She needed a weapon, a lever. Audley’s words came back to her again. *He fears the things she writes because he fears they are about him.*

The manuscript. The one that the publisher had been ecstatic about. Davison must have it. He would not have destroyed it. A man like that, a man who savored control, would keep it. It would be a trophy, a symbol of his victory over his ward’s rebellious spirit. He would keep it locked away somewhere safe.

Her gaze shifted from the garden to the dark bulk of the house itself. It was a labyrinth of secrets and locked doors. Finding a single bundle of paper would be a monumental, almost impossible task. But it was a task. It was a purpose.

A new kind of energy, cold and sharp, coursed through her. The fear was still there, a knot in her stomach, but it was now overlaid with something else: resolve. She turned from the window. Her room was small, but it was hers. She began to think not as a companion, but as a conspirator. She considered the layout of the house, the servants’ schedules, the location of Mr. Davison’s private study, a room she had never been invited to enter.

The game was no longer Audley’s. It was not even Davison’s. It was hers. And she would learn the rules, one dangerous secret at a time. Her first move would not be to find the manuscript. That was too ambitious, too risky. Her first move had to be smaller. It had to be to reach Clara. To let her know, somehow, that the truth she had been given on that slip of paper was not a solitary, painful burden. To let her know she was not alone. Eleanor did not know how she would accomplish this, only that she must. She would watch, she would listen, and she would wait for the smallest crack in the prison wall. And when she found it, she would slip a message of her own through the bars.
