He looked at her with an air of playful persecution that she found at once vexing and agreeable. “And so you will not be persuaded, Miss Vance? You will not allow that the anonymous verses which have so captivated the neighbourhood might have sprung from a gentleman’s pen? From a mind, say, of academic rigour and classical feeling?” He directed his gaze for a moment towards Mr. Alistair Finch, who was at that moment examining a potted fern with an intensity that suggested he had never before seen a leaf. “Or perhaps,” Mr. Henslow continued, his eyes returning to Eleanor, “from a spirit more worldly, one acquainted with the disappointments of the town?”

Eleanor laughed. “I allow only that you are determined to make sport of my theory. I believe the poetry to be a lady’s work, for it speaks of domestic sorrows and joys with a truth a gentleman could only guess at. Is that not your opinion, Miss Croft?”

She had spoken without thought, turning to where Catherine Croft sat quietly by the window, her needlework still in her lap. A deep blush rose at once to Miss Croft’s cheeks, and she started as if woken from a dream. “I, ma’am? I am no critic. I should not know how to form an opinion on such a matter.”

“There, you see!” cried Mr. Henslow. “Miss Croft is too modest to venture a guess. And yet, I wonder. There is one poem in particular, the one upon the old stone bridge at Miller’s Ford. It has a peculiar accuracy. One might almost imagine the author had stood upon that very spot, perhaps at twilight, sketching the view or, dare I say, composing the lines themselves. You are a great walker, are you not, Miss Croft? I believe your aunt has often remarked upon your fondness for solitary rambles.”

He paused, and the silence in the little drawing room became rather pronounced. Catherine Croft’s blush had subsided to a distressing paleness, and she picked at a loose thread on her glove. “My aunt is very kind,” she said, in a voice so low it was almost lost. “She exaggerates my little excursions.”

“Exaggerates! I am sure not. A taste for nature is a commendable thing. It shews a poetical soul.” He took up the book from the table beside him. “Let me see. ‘The moss, a velvet for a fairy queen, Doth cling to stones the winter floods made clean’. Very pretty. Very true to life. I passed that way only yesterday. It is exactly so.” He looked directly at Catherine, a smile of pure mischief on his lips. “I quite expected to see a wood nymph, pen in hand, communing with the muses.”

Eleanor wished he would desist; his game had gone on long enough, and Miss Croft was clearly not a willing player. To see her so distressed was a pain, and Eleanor felt a degree of compunction for having drawn her into the conversation at all. She was about to change the subject, to ask after Mrs. Miller’s sister or comment upon the weather, when she caught a glimpse of Catherine’s face. With all the evident confusion, with the nervous flutter of her hands, there was, for an instant, a flash of something else in her eyes. It was not anger, nor was it fear. It was a brief, unguarded look of pride, of secret and profound satisfaction. Eleanor was astonished. This quiet, unassuming, perfectly proper Catherine Croft was not merely embarrassed by the attention; she was, it seemed, enjoying some part of it.

Just then, the door opened, and their hostess bustled in, bearing a tray of biscuits. “Oh, I do hope I am not interrupting your clever talk! Mr. Henslow, you must have one of my lemon biscuits. Everyone is so good as to say they are my finest. Catherine, my dear, you have no colour at all! Are you quite well? You must not sit so near the window; I am sure there is a draught.”

Before anyone could answer this volley of kindnesses, the parlour maid appeared again at the door. “Mr. Davies, ma’am.”

Mr. Davies entered, his sensible countenance a welcome contrast to Mr. Henslow’s raillery. He bowed to Mrs. Miller and to Eleanor, but his first real look was for Miss Croft. “I was sorry to hear you had the headache this morning,” said he, his voice full of an unfeigned concern. “I hope you are recovered.”

“Oh! yes, I thank you, Mr. Davies. It was nothing. It is quite gone,” she replied, and a true, grateful colour returned to her cheeks.

“A headache?” said Mr. Henslow, shutting the book of verses with a soft snap. “The wages of intense study, no doubt. We have been trying to uncover the identity of our local genius. Perhaps you can assist us, Davies? Who do you conjecture has been writing sonnets by the river?”

Mr. Davies’s expression did not soften. “I conjecture nothing, sir. I find it an impertinent business to be pulling at a lady’s veil, for I am quite of Miss Vance’s opinion that the work is a lady’s. Whoever she may be, she has her reasons for privacy, and I for one would think it the part of a gentleman to respect them.”

He did not raise his voice, yet every word was distinct and carried a weight of reproof. Mr. Henslow merely raised an eyebrow. “A champion appears! How gallant. You would leave the poor author to blush unseen? How cruel, Davies. Fame is the spur, you know.”

“Idle gossip is the goad, Henslow, and often drives delicate spirits to misery, not fame.” He turned from him then, and taking a seat near Miss Croft, began to speak to her in a lower voice about some business of the parish.

Eleanor watched them. She saw the gentle relief in Catherine’s manner and the steady kindness in Mr. Davies’s. The amusement she had felt at Mr. Henslow’s wit curdled into a feeling very like shame. He had been clever, but Mr. Davies had been good. And Eleanor, in her own eagerness to be clever, had for a moment forgotten which of the two was of greater worth.

The silence that followed Mr. Davies’s pronouncement was of a quality quite different from the one that had preceded it. The first had been a silence of playful anticipation; this was one of social awkwardness, thick and uncomfortable as wool. Mrs. Miller, ever the enemy of quiet, rushed to fill the void. “Oh, goodness, let us not speak of such serious things! Fame and misery, it is all too much for a simple afternoon. Mr. Henslow, you have not tried a biscuit. They will be quite cold.”

Mr. Henslow, however, was not so easily deflected. He picked up a biscuit, not to eat it, but to inspect it as if it were a curious fossil. A slow, knowing smile played upon his lips. “You mistake me, Davies,” he said, his voice smooth as cream. “I am all for respecting privacy. I merely think it a pity that such talent should not receive its due approbation. Why, if our poet were a man, we should be toasting him in every tavern from here to London. But being a lady, she is to be protected, cosseted, and kept from the very world she so ably describes. It seems a strange sort of chivalry that values a lady’s blushes more than her intellect.”

He directed the final part of this speech not to Mr. Davies, but to the room at large, his eyes resting for a fraction of a second too long on Catherine Croft. She flinched, a barely perceptible movement, as if a cold wind had touched her. Eleanor felt a surge of indignation on her behalf. Mr. Henslow’s words were couched in the language of modern enlightenment, a supposed defense of female intellect, but their intent was pure malice. It was a barb, designed to make Mr. Davies’s goodness appear old fashioned and paternalistic, and to agitate its true target further.

It was Mr. Davies who answered, his tone perfectly even. “A lady’s intellect, sir, is not a public spectacle to be paraded for the amusement of others. It is her own, to be shared as she sees fit. True appreciation is not loud; it does not demand a name and a face to attach to the words that have moved it. It is a quiet communion between the writer and the reader. All else is vanity.”

With that, he turned his back fully on Mr. Henslow and resumed his conversation with Miss Croft, his manner conveying with perfect civility that, for him, the topic was now closed and Mr. Henslow himself had ceased to exist. The cut was so profound, and delivered with such quiet authority, that even Mr. Henslow had no immediate reply. He gave a short, dry laugh, placed the untasted biscuit back on the plate, and rose to his feet.

“Well, I see the tide of opinion is against me,” he declared with false cheerfulness. “I shall take my impertinent curiosity elsewhere. Mrs. Miller, your hospitality is, as ever, a balm to the soul. Miss Vance, a pleasure. Davies, do try not to be too heroic; it is a terrible strain on the constitution.” He bowed, a gesture of mockery, and took his leave.

The atmosphere in the parlour lightened at once, as if a storm cloud had passed. Mrs. Miller began to press her biscuits on Mr. Davies with renewed vigour, and the conversation turned to safer topics: the price of wool, the health of the vicar’s wife, and the upcoming assembly. Eleanor found her own attention wandering. Her mind was fixed on Catherine Croft. The girl, now engaged in a gentle, low toned discussion with Mr. Davies, seemed to have recovered her composure. The anxious pallor was gone, replaced by a soft pink in her cheeks that spoke of gratitude and, Eleanor suspected, a deeper affection. Watching them, Eleanor felt a certainty settle upon her. Mr. Davies did not merely suspect; he knew. He was not just defending a hypothetical lady poet; he was protecting the woman before him, the author of the sonnets he so admired.

Soon it was time for Eleanor to depart. As she stood to make her farewells, Catherine rose also. “If you are walking, Miss Vance,” she said, her voice still a little tremulous, “perhaps I might accompany you part of the way? I feel the need for some air.”

Eleanor readily agreed, and the two ladies set out together. For the first several minutes they walked in silence, the crisp afternoon air a welcome restorative. The events of the parlour seemed to hang between them, an unspoken acknowledgment of a shared secret. Eleanor, chastened by her earlier lapse in judgment, resolved not to pry, not to follow Mr. Henslow’s boorish example. She would wait for Catherine to speak, if she chose to.

It was Catherine who broke the silence. “Mr. Henslow is very… energetic in his opinions,” she said, choosing her word with care.

“He is fond of the sound of his own voice,” Eleanor replied, more plainly than she might have done an hour before. “And fonder still of making others uncomfortable for his own sport.”

A small, grateful smile touched Catherine’s lips. “Yes. That is it precisely. I find such company very trying. I am not quick enough to hold my own in a battle of wits.”

“I am not certain it was a battle of wits,” said Eleanor, looking at her companion. “It seemed to me more a case of kindness against cruelty. And in that contest, you had the finer champion.”

At this, a deeper blush stained Catherine’s cheeks. She looked down at the path, her hands clasped before her. “Mr. Davies is a very good man. He has always been a friend to my family.”

Eleanor sensed that to press the matter would be a violation of the very principle Mr. Davies had defended. She changed the subject, speaking of a book she had lately been reading, and was pleased when Catherine responded with an intelligent and thoughtful opinion. For the remainder of their walk, they spoke of literature, of the particular beauties of a well turned phrase, of the power of poetry to capture a feeling that prose could only circle. In those few minutes, Eleanor discovered a Catherine Croft she had never known. The shy, retiring girl of the drawing room was gone, replaced by a woman of deep feeling and a quiet, formidable intellect. The author of the sonnets was no longer a phantom; she was walking right beside her, her face animated and her eyes bright with an inner fire.

When they parted at the lane that led to Catherine’s small house, Eleanor felt a warmth toward her that was entirely new. It was a combination of admiration and a protective sort of friendship. She had seen behind the veil, not by tearing at it like Mr. Henslow, but by being granted a brief, voluntary glimpse. And she knew, with absolute conviction, that she would do anything in her power to ensure that veil remained in place, for as long as Catherine wished it.

The following week, the neighbourhood was alive with preparations for the autumn assembly. It was to be the most significant social event of the season, and even the most retiring families were expected to make an appearance. Eleanor looked forward to it with mixed feelings. She enjoyed the dancing and the general air of festivity, but she knew it would also be a prime hunting ground for Mr. Henslow. His failure to unmask the poet in Mrs. Miller’s parlour would not have deterred him; it would only have strengthened his resolve.

On the evening of the assembly, the town hall was a blaze of candlelight and a whirl of silk and muslin. Eleanor, standing with her aunt, surveyed the room. She soon spotted Mr. Henslow, holding court near the refreshment table, a small circle of listeners hanging on his every witty pronouncement. Not far away, she saw Catherine Croft, looking shy and lovely in a simple white gown. She was with her mother, a woman as quiet and unassuming as her daughter. And then she saw Mr. Davies, making his way through the crowd towards them. The look that passed between him and Catherine was brief, but it was filled with a world of understanding.

Eleanor was claimed for the first two dances by a young officer of her acquaintance and was soon caught up in the music and the movement. During a pause, she found herself standing near Mr. Henslow’s group. His voice, pitched to carry, cut through the general chatter.

“I have it on the best authority,” he was saying, “that our mysterious nightingale has a connection to the church. The language, you see. So full of piety and elevated sentiment. A clergyman’s daughter, I would wager. Or perhaps someone who spends a great deal of time in a clergyman’s company.”

He let his gaze drift meaningfully across the room to where Mr. Davies was now standing beside Catherine, fetching her a cup of punch. Several people followed his look, and a murmur of speculation went through the group. Eleanor’s blood ran cold. He was not just guessing now; he was openly pointing a finger, using insinuation as his weapon. It was despicable.

Catherine, though she must have been aware of the stares directed her way, gave no sign. She accepted the punch from Mr. Davies with a quiet smile, but Eleanor could see the tension in her shoulders and the tightness around her mouth. She looked like a cornered animal, desperate for escape but too well bred to flee.

Eleanor knew she had to do something. Her old self might have found a clever, cutting remark to make at Mr. Henslow’s expense. But her new self, the one that valued goodness over wit, sought a different path. She did not want to engage him on his own terms. She wanted to invalidate his entire cruel game.

Taking a deep breath, she moved towards the group. “Mr. Henslow,” she said, her voice clear and pleasant, drawing his attention. “I hear you are playing the detective again. It is a most amusing pastime.”

Henslow turned to her, a predatory glint in his eye. He was no doubt expecting her to join in the sport. “Miss Vance! The very person. Your literary judgment is so sound. Do you not agree that the evidence points towards the rectory?”

“Oh, I have given up all speculation on the matter,” Eleanor said with a light, dismissive wave of her hand. “In fact, I have come to believe you have been leading us all on a wild goose chase from the very beginning.”

A flicker of confusion crossed Henslow’s face. “I, madam? I do not take your meaning.”

“Why, it is perfectly obvious,” Eleanor continued, smiling brightly as if sharing a delightful piece of gossip. Her voice was just loud enough for the entire circle to hear. “The author of those poems is you, Mr. Henslow.”

There was a collective gasp from the listeners. Henslow himself looked utterly flabbergasted, for once rendered speechless.

“But of course it is,” Eleanor went on, pressing her advantage. “Who else has such a command of language? Who else could write with such passion? And this whole performance, this elaborate pretense of searching for the author, it is the most brilliant stroke of all! You create a mystery, you praise the work to the heavens, and you ensure that when the truth is finally revealed, your fame will be all the greater. It is a masterpiece of self promotion. I confess, sir, I am filled with admiration.”

She delivered this speech with such an air of sincere conviction that several people in the group began to look at Mr. Henslow with new eyes. The theory, absurd as it was, had a certain theatrical logic that appealed to them. It fit his character perfectly.

“That… that is the most ridiculous thing I have ever heard,” Henslow sputtered, his face turning a blotchy red.

“Is it?” Eleanor asked, feigning surprise. “I thought it made perfect sense. All your talk of fame being the spur. And your constant quotation. It is all of a piece. You wished to test the waters, to see if your work would be appreciated before putting your name to it. How very clever you are.”

“I am not clever!” he snapped, then seemed to realize how that sounded. “I mean… I did not write those sentimental rhymes! They are the work of a woman!”

“Oh, you are too modest,” said another lady in the group, now thoroughly convinced by Eleanor’s performance. “And to take on a female persona, too. How very modern of you, Mr. Henslow.”

Mr. Henslow looked around, trapped. To deny it with any real force would involve him in the ungentlemanly act of arguing heatedly with a lady. To admit it was impossible. He was caught in a web of his own making, and Eleanor, using nothing but his own vanity as her tool, had drawn the strings tight. He saw that he had become an object of ridicule, his grand mystery deflated into a common joke about his own ego.

He muttered something about the heat of the room, turned on his heel, and stalked away, his evening’s entertainment utterly ruined.

The group around Eleanor dissolved in laughter. She had turned the hunter into the fool, and had done so without a single unkind word. She had simply used his own character against him. A great sense of relief washed over her. She glanced across the room and saw Catherine Croft looking at her. Her eyes were wide, and in their depths was a look of profound, astonished gratitude. Beside her, Mr. Davies inclined his head slightly towards Eleanor, a gesture of respect and complete understanding.

Later in the evening, as the dancers were gathering for the final cotillion, Mr. Davies approached her.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his sensible face serious but for a slight warmth in his eyes. “May I say that I have never witnessed a more elegant or effective defense. You showed a very rare quality tonight.”

“And what is that, Mr. Davies?”

“You showed that true wit is not a weapon for causing pain, but a shield for protecting the innocent. You have a good heart, Miss Vance. And a very quick mind to serve it.”

Eleanor felt a blush rise to her own cheeks, but it was not one of shame or embarrassment. It was a blush of pleasure, of having earned the approbation of a man whose opinion she had come to value more than any other. She had not sought to be the cleverest person in the room. She had only sought to be good. And in doing so, she had found a far deeper and more lasting satisfaction. As she gave her hand to her partner for the dance, she caught Catherine’s eye one last time. Catherine gave her a small, secret smile, the smile of a fellow conspirator, a friend. And Eleanor knew that some bonds, like the finest sonnets, are not written for public applause, but are all the more precious for being kept in the quiet of the heart.

The quiet satisfaction of that final dance remained with Eleanor long after the music had faded and the carriage had rumbled its way home through the sleeping London streets. It was a warmth that had little to do with the exertion of the cotillion and everything to do with the settled feeling in her heart. She had acted not out of a desire to be clever, but out of loyalty, and in doing so had found herself in the company of true friends and in the esteem of a truly good man. The world, which so often felt like a stage for artifice and performance, had for one evening shown her a glimpse of its capacity for genuine connection.

The next morning, a note arrived from Catherine Croft, requesting a visit at her earliest convenience. There was an urgency to the handwriting that suggested her nerves had not been entirely settled by the previous night’s victory. Eleanor, accompanied by her chaperone, her placid and kind Aunt Gable, presented herself at the Croft residence before noon.

Catherine received her not in the formal drawing room but in her own private sitting room, a space filled with books and watercolors and the comfortable clutter of a life lived with quiet passions. She dismissed her own companion with a grateful nod and rushed to take both of Eleanor’s hands in her own.

“Eleanor, I declare, I did not sleep a wink,” she began, her eyes still wide with the memory of it all. “I replayed the entire scene a hundred times in my mind. The way you cornered him, the way you used his own pride as the trap. It was magnificent. It was terrifying.”

“It was necessary,” Eleanor said gently, allowing Catherine to lead her to a small settee by the window. “Mr. Henslow left us little choice. He intended to cause you pain, and that I could not abide.”

“But the consequences, Eleanor! What of them?” Catherine wrung her hands, a familiar gesture of anxiety. “A man like that, a man whose vanity is his entire world, will not suffer such a public humiliation lightly. He will seek retribution.”

“Let him,” Eleanor said, her voice calmer than she truly felt. “His chosen weapon was ridicule, and it has been turned back upon him. What more can he do? To challenge me openly would be to admit that he was bested by a woman in a battle of wits. His pride would never allow it. He will simply have to swallow his defeat and learn, one hopes, a lesson in humility.”

Catherine looked doubtful. “You have a better opinion of mankind than I, Eleanor. I fear his pride will not digest such a meal, but will seek to serve it back to you, seasoned with a poison of his own making.”

The thought was a sobering one, and Eleanor knew her friend’s fears were not unfounded. The society in which they moved was powered by whispers and insinuation. A reputation, so carefully constructed, could be undone by a well placed rumor. Mr. Henslow was a fool, but a well connected fool, and that could be a dangerous combination.

Their conversation was interrupted by the delivery of a tray of tea and biscuits, and the topic was turned to lighter matters. Catherine, now that her initial fear was voiced, seemed to find relief in the simple act of friendship. She showed Eleanor the sonnet she was currently composing, a delicate piece about the quiet courage of a winter rose. It was, Eleanor thought, possessed of far more genuine feeling and artistry than anything Mr. Henslow could ever hope to produce. As she read the lines, she felt a renewed sense of conviction. To have protected such a gentle talent from a boorish bully was a worthy accomplishment, regardless of the consequences.

The test of Catherine’s prediction came two nights later, at a musical evening hosted by Mrs. Albright, a woman whose primary social function was to collect and distribute the news of the ton. Her gatherings were always crowded, for everyone wished to hear the latest intelligence and to ensure that any intelligence being spread about them was favorable.

Eleanor entered the brightly lit salon with a sense of trepidation she could not entirely suppress. She saw Mr. Davies almost immediately, standing in conversation with a group of serious looking gentlemen near the fireplace. He met her eyes across the room and gave a small, reassuring nod, a silent acknowledgment of their shared understanding. The gesture fortified her more than she would have thought possible.

Then she saw Mr. Henslow. He was holding court near the pianoforte, surrounded by a coterie of his admirers. He was not looking in her direction. Instead, his attention was fixed upon Mrs. Finch, a woman whose tongue was known to be as sharp as her wit was dull. She was a notorious gossip, a person who relished a scandalous story above all things. Mr. Henslow was speaking to her in a low, conspiratorial tone, a smirk playing on his lips. He gestured subtly, not with his head but with a slight movement of his eyes, towards Eleanor.

Eleanor felt a chill creep over her. Catherine had been right. The beast was not sleeping; it was merely sharpening its claws in the shadows. She could not hear what was being said, but she could imagine it. He would not be accusing her of outwitting him. No, his method would be far more insidious. He would be painting her as a schemer, a woman of sharp and unfeeling wit, who took pleasure in the humiliation of others. He would twist her defense of a friend into an act of unprovoked aggression, recasting himself as the victim of her shrewish tongue.

Proof of her suspicions was not long in coming. As she moved through the room, she noticed a subtle shift in the atmosphere around her. A lady who had greeted her warmly only last week now offered only a cool, fleeting smile. A young gentleman, who had been on the verge of asking for an introduction, seemed to think better of it and turned away. The whispers that followed in her wake were too quiet to be understood, but their meaning was as clear as a shout. The poison was already spreading through the veins of the assembly.

Her Aunt Gable, blessedly oblivious, remarked on the warmth of the room and fanned herself with a gentle rhythm. But Eleanor felt a profound sense of isolation descend upon her. She had known there would be a risk, but she had not been prepared for the swiftness and efficacy of his revenge. It was a cowardly attack, one she could not fight directly. To confront the rumors would only be to give them more substance, to appear defensive and thus, guilty.

She found a seat in a slightly less crowded corner, trying to compose her features into an expression of serene indifference. She focused on the music, a rather overwrought Italian aria being performed by a flushed soprano, but the notes seemed to mock her, soaring with a confidence she no longer felt. Was this the price of doing good? To be maligned and ostracized, to have her character assassinated in quiet corners by a man she had proven to be a fool?

It was then that Mr. Davies detached himself from his group and crossed the room. His path was direct and purposeful, a clear statement to anyone who was watching. He ignored the curious glances and came to a halt before her chair.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his voice a calm anchor in the turbulent currents of the room. “I was hoping I might find you. My friends and I were just having a debate on the merits of Pope’s translations of Homer. I find myself in the minority and am in dire need of an ally. Might I borrow your excellent sense for a moment?”

His words were a lifeline. It was not just a polite rescue; it was a public declaration of support. By seeking out her opinion on a matter of literature, he was directly refuting the image of her as a frivolous, cruel gossip. He was reminding the room, and more importantly, reminding her, of who she truly was.

Eleanor felt a wave of gratitude so profound it almost brought tears to her eyes. She blinked them back and gave him a genuine smile, the first she had managed all evening.

“I should be delighted to lend my sense to such a cause, Mr. Davies,” she replied, her voice steady. “Though I must warn you, my loyalties in that particular battle are notoriously fickle.”

He laughed, a low, pleasant sound. “A fickle ally is better than none at all.” He offered her his arm. “There is a small conservatory through that door. It is quieter there, and I believe we might escape this particular aria without offending our hostess.”

She took his arm, acutely aware of the eyes that followed them as they left the main salon. The conservatory was a haven, cool and quiet, filled with the faint, earthy scent of ferns and damp soil. The music from the other room was a distant murmur. Moonlight streamed through the glass panes, illuminating the space in a soft, silver light.

For a moment, they stood in silence, simply breathing in the peaceful air.

“He is a contemptible fellow,” Mr. Davies said at last, his voice losing its formal edge. He did not need to say who he meant.

“He is a wounded one,” Eleanor replied, looking out at the dark garden beyond the glass. “And a wounded animal is often the most dangerous.”

“What he is doing,” Mr. Davies continued, his tone hardening with indignation, “is the very definition of cowardice. He cannot face you on an open field, and so he resorts to poisoning the wells. I have already heard three different versions of what occurred at the ball, each more fanciful and malicious than the last. In each, you are the villain, and he the unsuspecting gentleman set upon by your venomous wit.”

Eleanor sighed. “It is what I feared would happen. What Catherine Croft feared.”

“Miss Croft is a good friend to you. As you are to her,” he said, turning to face her more fully. The moonlight caught the earnest expression on his face. “Miss Vance, I hope you will not allow this to trouble you overmuch. The opinions of such people, the Mrs. Finches of this world, are as shifting as the wind. They are of no true consequence.”

“They feel of consequence when they cause every person in a room to look at you as if you have sprouted a second head,” she said with a touch of weary humor.

“Then those people are fools,” he stated, his conviction absolute. “They are so concerned with the surface of things, with whispers and appearances, that they have lost the ability to discern true character. What you did for your friend was an act of profound loyalty and courage. You used your intelligence not for sport, but for protection. Anyone who cannot see that is not worth your notice.”

His words were a balm to her bruised spirit. He saw her. He understood not just what she had done, but why she had done it. In a world that so often felt bewilderingly superficial, his opinion was a fixed point of reference, a North Star of integrity.

“Thank you, Mr. Davies,” she said, her voice soft. “Your good opinion means a great deal to me.”

“It is not my opinion, Miss Vance, it is the fact of the matter,” he insisted gently. “Reputation is a fragile thing, built by others. Character is a fortress, built by oneself. His whispers may cause cracks in the former, but they cannot touch the latter. You must remember that.”

They stood for a while longer, speaking of other things, of books and poetry, of the quiet beauty of the countryside versus the vibrant energy of the city. Their conversation flowed with an ease and a depth that Eleanor had rarely experienced. Here was a mind that matched her own, not in a contest of wit, but in a shared landscape of thought and feeling. She found herself speaking of her own aspirations, of her desire for a life that was useful and good, not merely decorative. He, in turn, spoke of his work with his tenants on his estate, of his belief that land ownership was a responsibility more than a privilege.

She saw in him a man of substance, a man who, like her, was trying to navigate the often treacherous waters of society without compromising the core of who he was. The attraction she had felt for him before, a nascent respect for his good sense, was now blossoming into something deeper, a profound and heartfelt admiration that was perilously close to affection.

When they returned to the salon, the soprano had finished her performance and the room was buzzing with renewed conversation. Eleanor felt a change within herself. The cool glances and whispered asides had not vanished, but they had lost their power to wound her. Mr. Davies remained by her side for some time, engaging her in a conversation so animated and interesting that several other people, drawn by its quality, joined their circle. He was, in his quiet and determined way, rebuilding her position with the bricks of his own unimpeachable reputation.

Later, as she was taking her leave, she saw Catherine Croft, who had arrived late. Catherine rushed to her side, her face a mask of concern.

“I have heard the whispers, Eleanor,” she said in a low voice. “That horrid man. I am so sorry. This is all my fault.”

“It is not your fault,” Eleanor said, placing a reassuring hand on her friend’s arm. “It is his. And it does not matter. Tonight, I have learned that the respect of one good person is worth more than the idle chatter of a hundred fools.”

As she spoke, her gaze drifted across the room and met that of Mr. Davies. He inclined his head once more, a silent promise of solidarity. Eleanor’s heart felt full. The battle with Mr. Henslow was far from over; she knew that his malice would not be so easily exhausted. But she was no longer facing it alone. She had a true friend in Catherine, and in Mr. Davies, she had found an ally whose strength and integrity were a shield far more powerful than wit alone could ever be. The path ahead might be difficult, but for the first time, she felt she was walking it in the very best of company.

The carriage ride home was a quiet affair, a marked contrast to the clamour of the party they had just left. The rhythmic clatter of the horses’ hooves on the cobblestones was a soothing percussion against the backdrop of London’s sleeping streets. Catherine, wrapped in her shawl, sat opposite Eleanor, her brow still furrowed with a lingering disquiet.

“You are certain you are not distressed?” Catherine asked, her voice a soft intrusion into the stillness. “He was so vile, so utterly without honour. To see people, people we have known for years, actually listening to him…” She trailed off, shaking her head in disbelief.

Eleanor looked out at the passing gaslights, their glow haloing the misty air. “I was distressed,” she admitted, her tone even and thoughtful. “Deeply so. I felt as if the ground beneath my feet had turned to sand. But my distress came from a place of vanity, I think. From a fear of what others thought of me.”

“That is not vanity, Eleanor, it is a natural human concern. Our reputation is all we have.”

“Is it?” Eleanor turned her gaze back to her friend. “Tonight I learned that it is not. Reputation is a reflection in a looking glass, and it can be warped by the slightest imperfection in the glass itself. Mr. Henslow is a profound imperfection. Character, however, is the thing being reflected. That is solid. That is ours. He cannot touch that.”

Catherine considered this. “Mr. Davies said something similar to you, did he not? I saw you speaking with him by the conservatory. He did not leave your side for the rest of the evening.” A small, hopeful smile touched her lips. “He is a very good man, Eleanor. And his good opinion is one that truly matters.”

“Yes,” Eleanor said, a warmth spreading through her chest that had nothing to do with the carriage’s lap blanket. “He is.”

They rode the rest of the way in a comfortable silence, each lost in her own thoughts. For Catherine, they were thoughts of her friend’s narrow escape from social ruin and the unexpected chivalry of Mr. Davies. For Eleanor, they were a complex tapestry of gratitude, newfound strength, and a burgeoning hope that was both thrilling and terrifying. She replayed her conversation with Mr. Davies, not the words about Mr. Henslow, but the ones that followed. The discussion of books, of duty, of a meaningful life. It was a conversation that had felt less like a discovery and more like a recognition, as if she had found a corner of the world that was shaped in the exact dimensions of her own soul.

The following days were a test of her new resolve. The whispers had not been contained within the walls of Lady Danbury’s salon; they had escaped, like a foul miasma, into the wider air of society. When she and Catherine went to a popular circulating library to exchange their novels, she noticed the change at once. Mrs. Gable, a woman with whom she had always shared pleasantries about the latest gothic romances, offered only a curt nod, her eyes darting away as if Eleanor’s gaze might scorch her. Two young ladies, daughters of a baronet she knew slightly, ceased their chatter as she passed, their silence more damning than any spoken word.

Catherine’s hand tightened on her arm. “Pay them no mind,” she whispered fiercely.

Eleanor forced a calm smile, though her heart beat a little faster. “I am not,” she replied, her voice steady. She walked directly to the counter, made her request to the librarian with quiet dignity, and waited. She could feel the eyes on her back, could sense the curiosity and the judgment. In the past, this would have sent her fleeing, her cheeks burning with shame. Now, she stood her ground. She thought of Mr. Davies’s words: *the fortress of the mind*. She pictured herself within that fortress, its walls high and unbreachable. The whispers were mere winds rattling the gates. They could not get in.

Later that week, a note arrived. The penmanship was strong and clear, the paper of a fine, heavy stock. It was from Mr. Davies. He wrote to inform her of a small exhibition of Italian landscapes that had recently opened in a gallery just off Bond Street. He recalled her mentioning a fondness for the works of Canaletto and wondered if she might permit him to escort her to view them the following afternoon.

Eleanor’s hand trembled slightly as she held the note. It was a simple invitation, a polite social overture. Yet she knew it was more than that. It was a deliberate, public gesture of support. To be seen in her company, after the poison Mr. Henslow had so diligently spread, was a statement. He was not just a secret ally; he was a declared one.

She wrote her acceptance immediately, her spirit soaring.

The air the next day was crisp and bright, one of those rare autumn afternoons that felt like a gift. Mr. Davies arrived punctually, his presence filling the entryway of her aunt’s modest townhouse. He was dressed impeccably but without ostentation, his manner as calm and reassuring as she remembered.

“Miss Ainsworth,” he said, with a bow that was both formal and warm. “I am glad the day has held. The light will be perfect for the paintings.”

Their conversation, as they walked the short distance to the gallery, was easy and natural. He asked after her aunt, and she inquired about his estate in the country. He spoke with genuine passion about a new method of crop rotation he was implementing, not as a landlord boasting of his innovations, but as a steward fascinated by the earth and his responsibility to it. Eleanor found herself listening with rapt attention, drawn in by his earnestness and the keen intelligence that shone in his eyes.

The gallery was a quiet, refined space, with perhaps a dozen other patrons drifting between the gilded frames. The paintings were magnificent, filled with the impossible, sun-drenched light of Venice. As they stood before a particularly grand canvas depicting the Grand Canal, Mr. Davies spoke in a low, appreciative voice.

“It is his precision that I admire,” he said. “The ability to capture such a sprawling, chaotic scene with such order and clarity. Every brick, every ripple in the water, is rendered with absolute intention. There is no artifice, only skill and a deep respect for the subject.”

“It is an honest beauty,” Eleanor agreed, her gaze on the painting but her thoughts on the man beside her. “He does not flatter the city, he simply reveals its truth. It is a kind of integrity, is it not? In art, as in life.”

His eyes met hers, and in them she saw a profound understanding. “Yes,” he said softly. “That is it precisely. Integrity.”

The moment was interrupted by a familiar, unwelcome voice. “Well, well. Davies. I did not take you for a man with a taste for such… sentimental views.”

Eleanor’s stomach tightened. Mr. Henslow was standing a few feet away, a supercilious smirk playing on his lips. He was with Lord Ashworth, a notoriously dissolute peer who seemed to collect unsavoury companions. Mr. Henslow’s eyes slid over Eleanor, dismissing her entirely, before fixing back on Mr. Davies.

Mr. Davies did not so much as flinch. His posture remained relaxed, his expression unchanged. “Henslow,” he said, his tone perfectly level. “I believe a taste for beauty is not a failing. It is a taste for ugliness that one must guard against.”

Henslow’s smirk widened, though a flicker of annoyance showed in his eyes. “Oh, I agree. One must be very discerning about the company one keeps. Reputations, like fine art, can be easily soiled. And once damaged, their value plummets, does it not? No matter how… interesting the original composition might have been.” He let his gaze drift pointedly towards Eleanor for a single, venomous second before turning his back to them and directing Lord Ashworth’s attention to a painting on the opposite wall.

The barb was unmistakable, its cruelty amplified by its public delivery. The air around them grew thick with unspoken meaning. Eleanor felt a hot blush creep up her neck. The old instinct to flee, to hide, surged within her. Her fortress was being assailed by a battering ram.

But then she felt Mr. Davies move, a subtle shift that placed him ever so slightly more in front of her, a human shield. She looked at his profile, at the firm set of his jaw, and she remembered. *The respect of one good person.* She would not allow this man, this purveyor of filth and lies, to make her cower. She would not dishonour Mr. Davies’s faith in her by proving Henslow right.

Taking a deep, steadying breath, she stepped forward from behind Mr. Davies’s comforting presence. Her voice, when she spoke, was clear and carried easily in the quiet gallery.

“That is an interesting theory, Mr. Henslow,” she said.

He turned, surprised that she had dared to address him. Lord Ashworth looked on with a bored, cruel amusement.

Eleanor met Henslow’s gaze directly. She did not raise her voice; she did not need to. Her calm was its own weapon. “Your analogy of art and reputation is flawed, however. You see, the value of a painting is indeed determined by the opinions of others. By critics, by collectors, by the whims of the market. Its worth is external.”

She paused, letting her words settle. A few other patrons had turned to listen, drawn by the quiet drama.

“A person’s character, on the other hand,” she continued, her voice gaining a quiet strength, “is not a painting to be bought and sold. It is the artist. It is the creator of its own worth. And its value is not diminished by the scribblings of a vandal in a gallery catalogue. It is diminished only when the artist themselves chooses to compromise their craft.” Her eyes held his, cool and unwavering. “I, for one, have no intention of compromising mine. Now, if you will excuse us, Mr. Davies and I were admiring the honest beauty of Venice.”

With a small, dignified nod, she turned her back on him and returned her attention to the Canaletto. It was a dismissal so complete, so utterly final, that it left no room for a reply.

For a moment, there was a stunned silence. Mr. Henslow stood, his mouth slightly agape, his face a mottled red. He had expected tears, or a frightened retreat. He had not expected this cool, intellectual dissection of his own malice. He had tried to wound her with the rapier of insinuation, and she had disarmed him with the sheer force of her character. Lord Ashworth let out a short, surprised bark of a laugh, clearly more entertained by Henslow’s public defeat than he was loyal to him. Uttering a choked oath, Henslow turned on his heel and strode from the gallery, his companion trailing behind him with a look of malicious glee.

The tension in the room dissipated. Eleanor could feel her heart hammering against her ribs, a wild bird trapped in a cage. But on the outside, she remained serene, her eyes fixed on the painted city.

“That,” Mr. Davies said, his voice low and filled with an admiration that was almost tangible, “was the most remarkable thing I have ever witnessed.”

She finally allowed herself to look at him. The respect she saw in his eyes was so profound, so absolute, that it made her feel weak in the knees. “I was merely putting your philosophy into practice,” she said, her attempt at a light tone betrayed by a slight tremble in her voice.

“You have perfected it,” he replied. He offered her his arm. “Shall we continue our tour? I find my appreciation for integrity has grown immeasurably in the last five minutes.”

As she placed her hand on his arm, she felt the strength and warmth of him through the fabric of his coat. It was a feeling of safety, of rightness, that she had never known. They completed their circuit of the gallery, their conversation returning to art, but it was changed. An invisible barrier had been broken between them. The shared threat, and her courageous vanquishing of it, had forged a new and powerful intimacy.

When he walked her home, the afternoon sun was beginning to slant, casting long shadows across the square. They walked slowly, neither of them seeming to want the time to end.

“Miss Ainsworth,” he began, his tone more serious than before. “I must confess, my invitation today was not entirely about the art. I had heard the whispers continued, and I was concerned. I hoped my presence might offer some small measure of public support.”

“It offered a great deal more than that, Mr. Davies. And I thank you for it.”

“There is no need for thanks,” he said, stopping before the steps of her aunt’s house. He turned to face her, still holding her hand where it rested on his arm. “What you did in that gallery today, Eleanor… may I call you Eleanor?”

Her heart skipped a beat. “Yes,” she breathed.

“Eleanor,” he repeated, and her name on his lips was a beautiful sound. “You did not need my support. You possess a strength of spirit I have rarely encountered. Henslow meant to define you by his lies, and you responded by defining yourself, on your own terms, with unshakeable truth. You have an artist’s soul, just as you said.”

He lifted her hand and, for a moment, she thought he might kiss it. But he simply held it, his thumb gently stroking her glove. His gaze was direct, honest, and filled with a depth of feeling that stole her breath.

“I find,” he said, his voice dropping to a near whisper, “that my admiration for your character is rapidly becoming an admiration for the woman who possesses it. A feeling much deeper, and much more profound.”

The world seemed to shrink to the small space between them. The sounds of the city faded away, replaced by the frantic beating of her own heart. Here was the truth, spoken plainly, without artifice or game. It was the honest beauty they had spoken of earlier, but it was alive, and it was being offered to her.

“And I find, Mr. Davies,” she replied, her voice as soft as his, “that my gratitude for an ally is becoming a heartfelt affection for the man himself.”

A slow smile spread across his face, transforming his usually serious features into something boyishly handsome. It was a smile of pure, unadulterated happiness. He released her hand, though his eyes never left hers.

“Then I must hope,” he said, “that you will allow me to call again soon. So that we may discover where this admiration and affection might lead us.”

“I should like that very much, Mr. Davies.”

He bowed, a gesture that now seemed impossibly intimate. “Until then, Eleanor.”

She watched him walk away, his tall figure receding down the sunlit street. She felt a lightness in her chest, a sense of joy so pure and expansive she thought she might float up the steps. The whispers of Mr. Henslow and his ilk seemed a distant, pathetic noise now, like the buzzing of a fly in a cathedral. They could not touch her. She was safe within the fortress of her own mind, and she was no longer its sole occupant. She was walking her path in the very best of company, and for the first time, she believed with all her heart that it was a path leading somewhere truly wonderful.

She ascended the stone steps to her aunt’s front door as if walking on air. The heavy oak felt weightless as she pushed it open, and the familiar scent of beeswax and dried lavender in the entrance hall seemed to welcome her back from a long and perilous journey. She was not the same woman who had left that morning, girded for a battle she was not certain she could win. She had won, not by vanquishing an enemy, but by refusing to let him be the author of her story.

Her aunt, Mildred Thorne, appeared in the doorway of the front parlour, her face a mask of anxious inquiry. She was a woman built on a small, birdlike frame, yet she possessed a surprising fortitude. Her hands were clasped so tightly together the knuckles were white.

“Eleanor? My dear child, I have been half mad with worry. What happened? I saw that dreadful man leave the gallery, his face like a thundercloud.”

Eleanor closed the door behind her, the solid click echoing the quiet certainty that now resided in her heart. She unpinned her hat and placed it on the hall table, her movements deliberate and serene.

“He is nothing, Aunt. A bully who has been shown the limits of his power.” She turned and smiled at her aunt, a genuine, untroubled smile that reached her eyes and made them shine. “It is over. I spoke the truth, and that was all that was needed.”

Mildred’s expression softened with profound relief. She came forward and took Eleanor’s hands in her own. Her skin was cool and dry. “You spoke? In front of all those people? Oh, Eleanor, that took such courage.”

“It did not feel like courage,” Eleanor confessed, her thoughts drifting back to the moment. “It simply felt necessary. As necessary as breathing. And I was not alone. Mr. Davies was there. He stood with me.”

A knowing look flickered in her aunt’s eyes. “Mr. Davies. Yes, the solicitor. I had hoped he might prove a man of substance. It seems my hopes were well founded. He saw you home?”

“He did.” Eleanor felt a blush rise in her cheeks, a warmth that had nothing to do with the afternoon sun. “He was very kind. He… he expressed a great admiration for my conduct.”

Mildred guided her into the parlour and towards the settee. “Admiration for your conduct is only proper, my dear. What else did he express?” The question was gentle, but its aim was true. Her aunt had always been able to see past the surface of things.

Eleanor sat, smoothing the fabric of her skirt, a memory of Mr. Davies’s thumb stroking her gloved hand returning with a vividness that made her heart flutter. “He expressed an admiration for… for me. And I for him.” She looked up and met her aunt’s gaze directly. “He is to call again soon.”

For a long moment, Mildred was silent. She studied her niece’s face, taking in the newfound light in her eyes, the quiet confidence in the set of her shoulders. Then, a slow, beautiful smile spread across her own features. “Then today was a victory on more than one front,” she said softly. “I am so very glad for you, Eleanor. So very glad.” She squeezed her niece’s hand one last time before rising. “Now, you must be exhausted. I will have Cook bring up some tea and sandwiches to your studio. A triumphant artist must be sustained.”

The studio, which had for so long been a sanctuary from a hostile world, now felt like a place of celebration. The scent of turpentine and oil paint was the smell of her own sovereignty. The tall windows, which she had looked through with such trepidation that morning, now seemed to frame a world full of possibility. She did not feel tired in the least. She felt energized, vibrant, as if a spring that had been coiled tight within her for years had finally been released.

She changed into her painting smock and went not to the sombre, complex historical scene she had been labouring over, but to a fresh, untouched canvas. She did not think about composition or theme or what the Royal Academy might favour. She simply began to mix colours. Bright, luminous yellows, rich cerulean blues, a vibrant, hopeful green. She worked quickly, freely, her brushstrokes bold and assured. She was not painting a scene, but a feeling. The feeling of sunlight on a London street, of a shared, honest gaze, of a quiet voice saying her name as if it were a precious thing.

The next few days passed in a haze of joyful anticipation. Eleanor worked on her new painting with a feverish intensity she had not felt in years. It was an abstract impression of light and air, a departure from her usual style, and she knew it was the most honest thing she had ever painted. Each morning she awaited the post, and with it, the newspapers. She knew her confrontation with Henslow would not go unremarked.

The reports, when they came, were a curious mixture. Some of the more sensationalist papers painted the affair as a scandalous society spat, a jilted artist and his rebellious protégée. They hinted at improprieties with a glee that made her stomach clench. But to her surprise and relief, the more reputable journals, including the one for which Mr. Davies wrote, took a different tone. They reported Henslow’s accusations but gave equal, if not greater, weight to her calm, public rebuttal. They spoke of her dignity and her quiet fortitude. One critic, in a surprising turn, ignored the scandal almost entirely and wrote a lengthy, thoughtful piece on the very paintings Henslow had sought to claim, praising their unique vision and emotional depth. He concluded by saying that whoever held the brush, the works themselves were a testament to a singular and powerful artistic soul.

Reading those words, Eleanor felt a second wave of vindication. Her work was speaking for her, just as she had spoken for it. The fortress of her mind felt stronger than ever, its walls buttressed by the truth of her own creation.

On the third afternoon, a calling card was delivered on a silver tray. The embossed letters were simple, elegant, and perfectly black against the stark white card. Mr. Arthur Davies. Her heart gave a great leap. Arthur. It was a good, strong name. A name that felt as solid and dependable as the man himself.

He was announced for four o’clock the following day. The intervening hours felt both impossibly long and far too short. She fretted over which dress to wear, settling finally on a deep blue silk that matched her new painting, before chiding herself for her vanity. It was not her appearance that he admired, she reminded herself, but her character. And yet, she wanted to appear before him as the best version of herself, a woman worthy of the profound respect he had shown her.

When he arrived, ushered into the parlour by the maid, he seemed to bring the bright afternoon light in with him. He looked taller than she remembered, his presence filling the formal room with a quiet, confident energy. His eyes found hers immediately, and the smile that touched his lips was the same one she had held in her memory, a genuine expression of warmth that transformed his serious face.

“Miss Thorne,” he said, his voice a low, pleasant rumble. “It is good to see you again.”

“Mr. Davies. Please, do sit down.”

Her aunt was there, of course, a discreet and watchful presence by the fireplace. Propriety demanded it. The three of them exchanged pleasantries about the weather and the uncommon warmth for the season. Mr. Davies spoke with an easy intelligence about a recent concert he had attended, and Eleanor found herself relaxing in the gentle flow of the conversation. He did not treat her like a fragile creature to be coddled after her ordeal, nor did he treat her aunt as a mere conversational obstacle. He engaged them both with genuine interest.

“I took the liberty of reading the press coverage of the events at the gallery,” he said, turning his attention more fully to Eleanor. His gaze was direct, and she saw in it a flicker of concern. “I trust you were not too distressed by some of the less charitable accounts.”

“I was not,” Eleanor replied, surprised by how true the words were. “I find that once you have spoken your own truth, the noise of others’ opinions loses much of its power. And some of the reports were… surprisingly fair.”

“They were indeed,” he agreed. “Truth has a way of resonating, even in the pages of a Fleet Street paper. Your truth certainly did. You have shifted the narrative, Miss Thorne. Many people are speaking of you now, not as Henslow’s victim, but as a woman of immense integrity.”

At that moment, her aunt rose gracefully. “If you will excuse me,” she said, her voice smooth as cream, “I believe I must consult with Cook about the evening meal. The fishmonger can be so unreliable on a Thursday.” It was the flimsiest of excuses, and they all knew it. It was a gift, a small pocket of time and privacy granted by a wise and compassionate woman.

The moment the door clicked shut behind her aunt, the atmosphere in the room shifted. The formal parlour felt suddenly intimate. The ticking of the mantel clock seemed to grow louder in the quiet space between them.

Arthur Davies leaned forward slightly in his chair. “Eleanor,” he said, testing her name again, his voice softer now. “I came here today not only to pay my respects, but to say something more.” He paused, gathering his thoughts, and she admired the deliberate way he composed himself. He was not a man given to impulsive speeches. “What I saw in you at that gallery was more than just integrity. It was a rare form of courage. The courage to define oneself in the face of malicious falsehood. In my profession, I see how easily people are broken by lies, how they allow the court of public opinion to become their judge and jury. You refused. You appointed yourself the sole arbiter of your own worth. I cannot adequately express the depth of my respect for that.”

“You already have, Mr. Davies,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “You did so by standing beside me.”

“That was a simple act of decency,” he countered. “What I feel goes beyond that. These past few days, I have found my thoughts returning to our conversation, to you, with a frequency that has begun to interfere with my legal briefs.” A small, self deprecating smile played on his lips. “I had hoped you would allow me to call you Eleanor. I find myself hoping now that you might consent to call me Arthur.”

The request was so simple, so direct, yet it felt momentous. It was a bridge being extended across the formal divide that society placed between a man and a woman. “I should like that very much… Arthur.”

Saying his name felt both strange and perfectly natural. He smiled again, that wonderful, open smile, and the last of the room’s formality seemed to dissolve.

“Good,” he said simply. “Then, Eleanor, as we are to be Arthur and Eleanor, I must be bold and ask another question. I find the confines of a drawing room, however pleasant, to be somewhat restrictive for the kind of conversation I should like to have with you. I was wondering if you might permit me to escort you for a walk? Perhaps in Regent’s Park on Saturday, if the weather holds. We could speak of art, and truth, and anything else that comes to mind, with the sky for a ceiling instead of plaster.”

Her heart soared. This was not a vague promise to call again; it was a plan, a definite step forward. It was an invitation into a shared future, even if it was only the future of a Saturday afternoon. It was a courtship, beginning not with coy games or veiled intentions, but with an open, honest request for her company. It was exactly what she would have wished for.

“A walk in the park sounds wonderful,” she said, her voice filled with a joy she made no attempt to conceal. “I would be delighted.”

“Saturday, then. At two o’clock?”

“At two o’clock.”

He rose to leave, and she walked with him to the parlour door. He stopped and turned to her, his expression serious once more, but with a new light of happiness in his eyes.

“Until then,” he said, and for a breathless moment, she felt the powerful urge to reach out and touch his hand, a desire so strong it was almost a physical ache. But she held back, content for now with the promise his presence offered.

She watched from the window as he walked down the street, just as she had before. But this time, it did not feel like an ending. It felt like a beginning. The distant, pathetic noise of Mr. Henslow and the gossips of London was utterly gone, replaced by the symphony of her own hopes. She returned to her studio and stood before her new painting, the canvas of vibrant, joyful colour. She picked up a brush, dipped it in a brilliant white, and added a single, bright stroke of pure light to the very centre of the composition. It was not a depiction of the sun, but of the feeling of standing in its warmth, of turning one’s face towards it after a long, cold winter. She was walking her path in the very best of company, and for the first time, she could see the destination. It was not a place, but a person, and she was walking, with a full and grateful heart, directly towards him.
