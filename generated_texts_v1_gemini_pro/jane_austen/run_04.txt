his approbation. He turned another page of the portfolio with a deliberate air, his eyes showing a great deal of playful mischief and very little true sympathy. Presently he resumed, “What a very fortunate circumstance that you were at Sidmouth during so fine a season, Miss Abbott. One hears the winds can be quite treacherous on that coast. I trust you were in good company, and had some stout arms to rely upon should a gale have blown up unexpectedly?” He smiled, as if at some private jest. She could not pretend not to have heard; she could not, in politeness, refuse a reply. “The weather was, for the most part, perfectly agreeable,” said she, her voice betraying a slight tremor, which she instantly endeavoured to conceal. “My aunt and I were very well looked after.” “Ah, looked after, to be sure. One’s aunt is always a bastion of propriety. But I was thinking of other society. One often forms the most surprising, the most memorable acquaintances at the seaside, does one not? I wonder if your friends in Exeter have received any word of your artistic triumphs. I dare say they imagine you quite settled in your new situation, and enjoying all the peace it affords.” She made no answer, but began arranging the loose sketches with an air of finality. “Peace, yes,” he continued, taking a small brush from the table and pretending to adjust its bristles. “What a curious thing peace is, Miss Vance. One searches for it high and low, and sometimes one finds it, and sometimes one finds only a very good imitation. Miss Abbott mentioned acquaintances. There, I think the point is quite straight now. I have the satisfaction, madam, (to Mrs. Finch,) of restoring your watercolour brush to a state of perfect utility.” He was thanked with a great deal of warmth by their hostess, and to remove himself from Miss Abbott’s evident discomposure, he carried the portfolio to the window seat where Miss Vance was sitting. “If you are very good,” said he, in a lower tone, “you will tell me what you make of this one. It is a view I know rather well. Did you ever see a sky of such potent melancholy? It puts one in mind of a farewell. I believe you were not so melancholy when we walked to the Lower Mills yesterday; you seemed quite content. I would have given a fair sum, all the sums one ever has to give, for another quarter of an hour.” She looked at the sketch. “What an excellent memory you have for a particular view! If I am not mistaken, this is the very cove we discussed last week.” She looked up at him for a moment, saw the confirmation in his eyes, and coloured with vexation. He took a book of poetry from a nearby table, and turning to Eleanor, said, “Here is something I have not seen before. Lord Byron. And here is a collection of Scottish airs. A gift, I presume? This was all brought with Miss Abbott’s luggage. Very thoughtful of her aunt, was it not? She must have known Miss Abbott would have little entertainment here. I value that sort of consideration highly; it shows such a depth of feeling. Nothing done by halves; no detail forgotten. True solicitude could be its only source.” Eleanor wished he would be less transparent, yet could not forbear being entertained; and when, on glancing towards Catherine Abbott, she saw the faintest ghost of a smile, when she perceived that with all the deep flush of her agitation, there had been a smile of secret understanding, she felt less pity for her situation, and much less disapproval of him. This quiet, unassuming, perfect Catherine Abbott was evidently in possession of a significant secret. He brought the book of poetry to her, and they examined the binding together. Eleanor took the opportunity of whispering, “You are too plain. She must know your meaning.” “I sincerely hope she does. I would have her know it. I am not at all ashamed of my suspicions.” “But truly, I am half ashamed, and wish I had never mentioned the letter.” “I am very glad you did, and that you entrusted me with the puzzle. I now have an explanation for all her quiet sighs and pensive looks. Leave the shame to her. If she has been deceptive, she ought to feel it.” “She is not entirely without feeling, I think.” “I do not see much proof of it. She is describing the virtues of a good sable brush at this moment, as if her heart were entirely her own.” Shortly afterwards Mrs. Finch, passing near the window, exclaimed that she saw Mr. Davies on horseback in the lane. “Mr. Davies, I protest! I must have a word with him if I can, just to ask after his sister. I will not open the casement here; it would chill you all to the bone; but I can step into the vestibule you know. I am sure he will come in when he hears who is here. How very pleasant to have you all meet! Our little parlour so full of life!” She was in the adjoining hall while she was yet speaking, and opening the door, immediately hailed Mr. Davies, and every word of their exchange was as perfectly audible to the company as if it had been conducted in their very presence. “Good day! Good day! Very well, I thank you. So grateful for the loan of your man to look at the pump. He set it right in a moment; my own man just ready to give up. Pray come in; do come in. You will find some friends of yours here.” So began Mrs. Finch; and Mr. Davies seemed resolved to be heard in his own turn, for most firmly and audibly did he say, “How is your guest, Mrs. Finch? I wished to call upon you all, but my particular object is your guest. How is Miss Abbott? I hope her headache has quite abated. How is she to day? Pray tell me how Miss Abbott is.” And Mrs. Finch was compelled to give a direct answer before he would suffer her to say another word. The listeners were diverted; and Mrs. Croft gave Eleanor a look of some significance. But Eleanor still shook her head in resolute doubt. “So grateful! so very grateful for your man’s time,” resumed Mrs. Finch. He cut her short with, “I am on my way to the glebe. Can I deliver any message for you?” “Oh! dear, the glebe, are you? Mrs. Martin was saying only yesterday she needed to send a book to the vicar.” “Mrs. Martin has a boy to run errands. Can I deliver any message for you?” “No, I thank you. But do come in. Who do you imagine is here? Miss Vance and Mrs. Croft; so good as to call to see Miss Abbott’s sketches. Do tie your horse to the post and come in.” “Well,” said he, in a considering tone, “for two minutes, perhaps.” “And here is Mr. Beaumont too! Quite a party; so many friends!” “No, not to day, I thank you. I could not remain a moment. I must reach the glebe before the light fails.” “Oh! do come in. They will be so very pleased to see you.” “No, no; your parlour is quite full enough. I will call another time, and see the sketches.” “Well, I am so sorry! Oh! Mr. Davies, what a charming sermon on Sunday; how very affecting. Did you ever hear such wisdom? Was not it affecting? Miss Vance and Mr. Beaumont; I am sure I saw them both quite moved.” “Oh! very affecting indeed; I can say no less, for I suppose Miss Vance and Mr. Beaumont can hear every word I utter. And (raising his voice a little) I do not see why Miss Abbott should not be mentioned also. I think Miss Abbott has a great deal of sensibility; and Mrs. Finch is the very best hostess, without question, in the whole county. Now, if your friends have any sense of justice, they will say something kind about you and I in our turn; but I cannot wait to hear it.” “Oh! Mr. Davies, one more thing; a matter of some consequence, so kind! Jane and I are both so pleased about the gooseberries!” “What is the matter now?” “To think of your sending us a whole basket from your own garden. You said you had a great many, and now we hear from your gardener you have hardly any left for yourselves. We really are so pleased! Your cook may well be vexed. William mentioned it here. You ought not to have done it, indeed you ought not. Ah! he is gone. He never can abide being thanked. But I thought he would have come in, and it would have been a shame not to have mentioned.... Well, (returning to the parlour,) I have not been successful. Mr. Davies cannot stay. He is going to the glebe. He asked me if he could deliver any message....” “Yes,” said Catherine, “we heard his generous offer, we heard everything.” “Oh! yes, my dear, I suppose you might, because you know, the door was ajar, and the front door was open, and Mr. Davies has such a clear voice. You must have heard everything to be sure. ‘Can I deliver any message for you?’ said he; so I just mentioned.... Oh! Miss Vance, must you be leaving? You seem to have only just arrived, so very kind of you to call.” Eleanor found it was indeed time to be at home; the visit had already been long; and on consulting watches, so much of the afternoon was discovered to be lost, that Mrs. Croft and her companion taking their leave also, could only agree to walk with the young lady to the turning of the lane, before they parted.

The little party proceeded down the lane in a silence that was at first most welcome after the constant stream of cheerful observations that constituted Mrs. Finch’s conversation. The air, which had been close within the parlour, was now found to be refreshingly cool upon their cheeks; and the light of the declining sun cast long, agreeable shadows across the path, making the familiar hedges and trees seem altogether new and picturesque. Mrs. Croft, a woman of comfortable proportions and a placid disposition, was the first to speak.

“I do believe,” she began, with a gentle sigh of amused exhaustion, “that there is no kinder soul in the parish than dear Mrs. Finch; though her kindness does find expression in a quantity of words that quite outmatches any I have ever encountered. My head is quite full of gooseberries and sermons.”

“She means only for the best,” said her companion, a younger lady of a sensible but less charitable countenance. “But I confess, Aunt, I was very nearly overcome when she began to praise Mr. Davies to his face for his own sermon. I did not know where to look.”

“Oh, but he gave as good as he got,” replied Mrs. Croft, chuckling softly. “Praising us all in our turn, and raising his voice so we should not miss a word of it! I think Mr. Davies possesses a very dry sort of wit. Do not you think so, Mr. Beaumont?”

Mr. Beaumont, who had been walking beside Eleanor with a quiet, unobtrusive attentiveness, started slightly, as if roused from a private reverie. “Indeed, ma’am. He is not a man who can abide flattery, however well intentioned. I believe his compliments to the room were his own singular method of defence.”

Eleanor smiled. “I think you are right, sir. He built a fortress of praise around himself, so that Mrs. Finch’s own offerings could not find a way in.”

“A very apt description, Miss Vance,” he said, turning his gaze upon her with an expression of quiet approval that she found unaccountably pleasing. “A fortress of praise. I shall remember that.”

For a few moments they walked on, the gentle conversation lapsing once more into a comfortable quiet. Eleanor found herself very aware of Mr. Beaumont’s presence beside her. He was not a man of many words, unlike the other young gentlemen of the neighbourhood who seemed to think a constant flow of chatter was a necessary part of any social engagement. Mr. Beaumont’s silences were never awkward; they were thoughtful, and seemed to invite companionship rather than demand entertainment. She had often found, when in his company, that she was more at ease with her own thoughts, and less inclined to say something merely for the sake of breaking a pause.

They soon reached the turning where Mrs. Croft’s path diverged from their own. “Well, this is where we must leave you,” said the elder lady. “It has been a pleasure, Miss Vance, Mr. Beaumont. I hope we shall have the good fortune to meet again very soon. Perhaps you will call on us on Thursday? We shall be at home all afternoon.”

An invitation was duly accepted, goodbyes were exchanged, and Mrs. Croft and her niece proceeded on their way, their figures diminishing down the shaded lane until a bend in the path took them from sight.

Now Eleanor and Mr. Beaumont were quite alone. The lane ahead of them stretched towards the village, bordered on one side by a low stone wall and on the other by a thicket of hazel and oak. The birds were beginning their evensong, and the whole world seemed to have settled into a state of profound and lovely peace. For the first time, a small measure of self consciousness crept into Eleanor’s composure. The silence, which had been so comfortable in a party of four, now seemed to possess a new weight, a new significance.

It was Mr. Beaumont who spoke first, his voice calm and even. “Mrs. Finch was correct about one matter, at least. You were, I believe, affected by the sermon on Sunday.”

It was not a question, but a statement of fact, and it took Eleanor by surprise. She had thought her own feelings quite private. “Was I so very obvious?” she asked, a slight warmth rising in her cheeks.

“Not obvious to a casual observer, perhaps,” he replied. “But I have noticed, Miss Vance, that you are a very attentive listener. You do not merely hear the words; you consider them. I saw a certain thoughtfulness in your expression that went beyond mere politeness.”

Eleanor felt a thrill of a very particular kind of pleasure. It was the pleasure of being seen, of being understood. “It was the subject,” she said, looking at the path before her. “The idea of small kindnesses having a wider influence than we can ever know. It struck me as a very beautiful and a very truthful sentiment.”

“It is,” he agreed. “And a difficult one to practice. It is far easier to perform some grand, conspicuous act of charity than to maintain a consistent habit of small, unobserved gentleness. The one brings praise, like Mrs. Finch’s, and the other, for the most part, brings nothing but its own reward.”

“Like Mr. Davies and his gooseberries,” Eleanor suggested with a small smile.

He returned the smile, and it transformed his usually serious features. “Precisely. He could not escape praise today, much to his chagrin. But I imagine he gives away his fruit and his vegetables very often, with no one but his vexed cook the wiser.”

They walked on, the conversation flowing more easily now. They spoke of books they had read, of the changing seasons, of the particular quality of the light upon the distant hills. Eleanor found that his opinions often mirrored her own, yet he expressed them with a clarity and depth that made her see them anew. He did not offer compliments, but his attentive listening and thoughtful replies were a far more potent form of flattery. She felt her mind, so often obliged to concern itself with the small, domestic trifles of her household, expanding in his presence, reaching for ideas and sentiments that had long lain dormant.

“You did not stay to see the sketches after all,” she remarked, recalling Mrs. Finch’s earlier invitation.

“No,” he said. “I confess, my primary purpose in calling was not to see Miss Abbott’s drawings, competent though I am sure they are.”

He did not elaborate, and Eleanor’s heart gave a little flutter. Could it be that his purpose had been to see her? It was a presumptuous thought, and she chided herself for it, yet the hope remained, a small, warm ember in her chest. To conceal her momentary confusion, she said, “I believe they are mostly of landscapes. She is very fond of sketching the old ruins by the river.”

“A picturesque subject, certainly,” he said. “Do you ever draw, Miss Vance?”

“A little,” she admitted. “I am not very accomplished, but I find a great deal of pleasure in it. It makes one look at the world with more attention, do you not think? You notice the shape of a branch, or the exact colour of a stone, in a way you otherwise would not.”

“I do think so,” he replied with some warmth. “It is one of the great benefits of any art. It trains the eye and the heart to see what is truly there. I should very much like to see some of your sketches one day, if you would permit it.”

The request was made so simply, with such genuine interest, that Eleanor could not feel it was an empty civility. “You would be very welcome to, sir,” she said, her voice a little softer than she intended. “Though I fear you would find them very humble productions after all.”

“I am sure I should find them full of interest,” he answered, and then they fell silent once more, but it was a silence brimming with unspoken understanding.

The path had narrowed slightly, and a gnarled root of an ancient oak tree had pushed its way through the earth, creating an uneven patch. Lost in her thoughts, Eleanor did not see it, and her foot twisted awkwardly. She stumbled, and would have fallen had not Mr. Beaumont, with a swift and steadying motion, put his arm out to catch her.

His hand was firm on her elbow, holding her securely until she had regained her balance. The moment lasted only a few seconds, but it seemed to Eleanor to stretch into an eternity. She was intensely aware of his closeness, of the strength in his grip, of the faint, clean scent of wool and fresh air. She looked up at him, her thanks dying on her lips as she saw the look of concern in his eyes.

“Are you hurt, Miss Vance?” he asked, his voice low.

“No, not at all. Only startled. How very clumsy of me,” she said, stepping back and smoothing down the fabric of her dress with hands that trembled slightly. “Thank you, Mr. Beaumont. You saved me from a nasty fall.”

“It was nothing,” he said, though he did not immediately release her arm. His gaze held hers for a moment longer, and in it she saw something she had not seen before: a deep and serious tenderness that made her breath catch in her throat. Then, as if recollecting himself, he let his hand fall to his side and stepped back. “We must take more care. The light is beginning to fade.”

They continued the rest of their walk with a renewed, almost formal, politeness, speaking of inconsequential matters. Yet for Eleanor, everything had changed. The brief touch, the look in his eyes, had confirmed what her heart had only dared to hope. A current of unspoken feeling now flowed between them, as real and tangible as the stone wall beside them. The air itself seemed charged with a new electricity.

When they reached the gate of her father’s house, she turned to him. “I must thank you for your escort, Mr. Beaumont. It was a very pleasant walk.”

“The pleasure was all mine, Miss Vance,” he said. He did not offer to come in, for which she was grateful. She wanted to be alone with her thoughts, to hold this new and precious feeling to herself for a little while longer.

He tipped his hat to her. “Until Thursday, then.”

“Until Thursday,” she echoed.

She watched him walk away down the lane, his tall figure straight and purposeful, until he was out of sight. Only then did she turn and enter the house, closing the door softly behind her. She felt as though she were walking in a dream.

The house was quiet. She found her younger sister, Clara, in the sitting room, engrossed in a book by the fading light of the window. Clara looked up as she entered.

“Oh, Eleanor, you are back at last! I thought you had decided to take up residence with Mrs. Finch. Did she talk you into a stupor? Mama has gone to visit Mrs. Green, whose headaches are troubling her again.”

“The visit was very pleasant,” Eleanor said, her voice still holding a trace of the dreamlike quality she felt. She removed her bonnet and gloves, placing them carefully on the side table.

Clara, who was of a far more inquisitive and romantic nature than her sister, narrowed her eyes. “Pleasant? That is all you have to say? You have a look about you, Eleanor. A particular look. Your cheeks are pink. Did something happen?”

“Do not be silly, Clara. The walk was brisk, that is all.”

“The walk,” Clara repeated slowly, savouring the word. “Did you walk home alone?”

“Mrs. Croft and her niece were with us for a part of the way.”

“A part of the way,” Clara pounced on the admission. “Which means that for the other part, you were not with them. Who, then, was with you? It was not Mr. Davies, for he was going to the glebe. It must have been Mr. Beaumont! Was it Mr. Beaumont, Eleanor? Did you walk home alone with Mr. Beaumont?”

Eleanor could not suppress a small smile. To deny it would be pointless, and in truth, she did not want to. “Yes, Clara. I walked home with Mr. Beaumont.”

Clara clapped her book shut and leaned forward, her eyes wide with eager excitement. “Oh, I knew it! And what did he say? Did he speak of his feelings at last? Did he mention his prospects? Did he say anything of consequence at all? You must tell me everything, from the very beginning. Do not leave out a single word.”

Eleanor sat down opposite her sister, the last rays of the sun illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air between them. She looked at Clara’s expectant face, and the quiet joy within her own heart swelled. She would not, of course, tell her everything. The look in his eyes, the feeling of his hand on her arm, those were hers alone, too precious to be shared just yet. But she could share the rest.

“Well,” she began, a gentle composure settling over her features, belying the rapid beating of her heart. “It was a most agreeable conversation. We spoke of Mr. Davies’s sermon, and of the nature of kindness.”

Clara’s face fell in an expression of the most profound disappointment. “Kindness? He spoke to you of kindness? Eleanor, that is what one discusses with the vicar, or with a maiden aunt who is hard of hearing. It is not a subject for a young man who is, I trust, on the verge of declaring himself. You must have misunderstood him. Perhaps he said ‘likeness,’ and was about to confess that he saw your very likeness in the evening stars.”

Eleanor gave a small, genuine laugh, the sound light in the quiet room. “No, Clara, it was most certainly kindness. We were speaking of Mr. Davies’s point that true charity is not merely the giving of alms, but a disposition of the heart. Mr. Beaumont quite agreed. He said that he has found more genuine kindness in a simple cottage than in many a grand London drawing room.”

“Well, that is something, I suppose,” Clara conceded, though her tone was grudging. “It shows he has a good heart. And did he say anything else? Anything at all that was not related to a sermon?”

“He did,” Eleanor said, her gaze drifting towards the window, where the sky was now streaked with pale rose and lavender. “He asked after my own reading. He has lately finished a volume of poetry by Mr. Scott and thought I might enjoy it. He means to lend it to me.”

“He will lend you a book of poetry!” Clara’s spirits visibly revived. “That is a very good sign, Eleanor. Poetry is the language of love. And when will he give it to you? He cannot simply send it over with a servant, that would be far too impersonal. He must bring it himself.”

“He is to call for it at the bookseller’s in the village. He suggested that I might accompany him there on Thursday, as Mr. Henderson is expecting a new shipment of books from London. He thought I might find something to my own taste.”

Clara’s eyes were now shining with triumph. “He has invited you on an outing. A specific, planned outing. Oh, this is better than I had dared to hope. It is as good as a declaration. Mama must be told at once. She will be so pleased.”

“Let us not be too hasty,” Eleanor cautioned, though a warm tide of pleasure was rising within her. “It is only an invitation to look at some books.”

“It is never only an invitation to look at books,” Clara insisted with the worldly wisdom of her seventeen years. “It is an opportunity. It is an assignation. You must wear your new muslin dress, the one with the blue sprigs. It makes your eyes look like the summer sky.”

Their conversation was interrupted by the sound of the front door opening and the gentle, familiar voice of their mother. Mrs. Sutton entered the sitting room, looking weary but serene. She was a woman of elegant composure, whose placid exterior concealed a keen and observant mind.

“Good evening, my dears,” she said, untying the ribbons of her own bonnet. “Mrs. Green is feeling much improved. A little broth and a quiet room was all she required. Clara, have you been practicing your scales?”

“Oh, Mama,” Clara burst out, unable to contain her news a moment longer. “You will never guess what has happened. Eleanor has had an adventure!”

Mrs. Sutton raised a perfectly shaped eyebrow and looked at her elder daughter. Eleanor felt the colour rise in her cheeks once more. “Clara exaggerates, Mama. It was hardly an adventure.”

“Eleanor walked home from Mrs. Finch’s house with Mr. Beaumont,” Clara announced, taking the telling of the tale into her own hands. “Alone. For a very great part of the way. And he spoke to her of poetry, and he has invited her to the bookseller’s on Thursday to choose a new novel.”

Mrs. Sutton’s calm expression did not change, but her eyes rested on Eleanor with a new intensity. She waited for her daughter’s own account.

“Mrs. Croft and her niece turned for home at the crossroads, Mama,” Eleanor explained quietly. “Mr. Beaumont was continuing in this direction, and so he offered me his escort. It would have been improper to refuse.”

“Yes, of course,” Mrs. Sutton said slowly. “That was quite correct. Mr. Beaumont is a gentleman. And this invitation for Thursday?”

“He mentioned that new books were expected. He thought I might be interested,” Eleanor said, feeling suddenly as though she were defending a most audacious act.

“I see.” Mrs. Sutton was silent for a moment, her gaze thoughtful. She was not a woman given to romantic fancies like Clara, but she was deeply invested in her daughters’ happiness and security. Mr. Beaumont was a man of good family and impeccable manners. He was handsome, intelligent, and amiable. He was, in many respects, an ideal suitor. There was, however, the matter of his situation.

“He is a very agreeable young man,” she said at last, her voice neutral. “But his prospects, as I understand them, are not yet entirely settled. He is the second son, and while his family is of good standing, he must make his own way. Your father will want to be assured of his intentions before any particular encouragement is shown.”

The mention of her father brought a new and more sobering reality to the dreamlike quality of Eleanor’s afternoon. Mr. Sutton was a kind man, but he was also a man of unbending principles and deep practicality. He loved his daughters dearly and would not see them trifled with or settled into an uncertain future.

“It is only a walk to the bookseller’s, Mama,” Eleanor repeated, her voice softer now.

“Of course, my dear,” her mother replied, but there was a note of caution in her voice that Eleanor could not fail to register. “We shall see. Now, let us prepare for dinner. Your father will be home soon.”

The evening meal was a quiet affair. Mr. Sutton had returned from his business in the nearby town and spoke of ledgers and accounts, of the price of wool and the prospects for the harvest. He was a tall, spare man, whose reserved demeanour was often mistaken for coldness. Eleanor knew, however, that it was simply his way. He observed everything, speaking only when he had something of substance to say.

As the maidservant cleared away the main course, Mrs. Sutton, with her customary subtlety, guided the conversation. “Eleanor had a pleasant walk home this afternoon, did you not, my dear?”

“Yes, Mama. Very pleasant.”

“She was accompanied by Mr. Beaumont,” Mrs. Sutton added, as if it were a matter of little importance.

Mr. Sutton paused, his fork halfway to his mouth. He looked at Eleanor, his grey eyes clear and penetrating. She felt herself flush under his steady gaze.

“Mr. Beaumont,” he said. His voice was level, betraying no emotion. “He is the young man staying with his aunt at the manor.”

“Yes, Father,” Eleanor murmured.

“He seems a sensible young man,” Mr. Sutton continued, resuming his meal. “For a Londoner. His uncle, Sir William, was a fool with his money, but the Beaumonts themselves have a reputation for integrity.”

And that was all. He said no more on the subject, turning the conversation instead to a leaky roof on one of his tenant’s barns. But Eleanor knew her father. That brief assessment meant that Mr. Beaumont was now a subject of his consideration. The young man’s character, his finances, and his intentions would be quietly and thoroughly appraised. The thought was both reassuring and deeply unsettling.

Later that night, in the quiet solitude of her bedroom, Eleanor finally allowed the full measure of the day’s events to wash over her. She stood by the open window, the cool night air touching her cheeks, and looked out at the garden sleeping under a canopy of brilliant stars. The dreaminess she had felt earlier returned, but it was now tempered by the practical concerns her parents had raised.

She had not told Clara everything. She had not told her of the moment they had paused by the old stone wall overlooking Miller’s Field, the way the setting sun had caught the gold in his brown hair. He had turned to her, his expression suddenly serious, and had spoken not of kindness in the abstract, but of his search for it.

“One meets so many people in society,” he had said, his voice low and earnest, “whose words are polished but whose hearts seem made of ice. To find a true and kindred spirit, one who feels as you do, who understands without the need for lengthy explanation, that seems to me the greatest prize in life.”

As he spoke, his gaze had met hers, and in that look, Eleanor had felt a shock of recognition, a sense of rightness so profound it had taken her breath away. It was as if he were not merely making an observation about the world, but about the two of them, standing there together in the golden light. And when he had helped her over the stile a little further down the lane, his hand had closed over hers, strong and warm, and had lingered for a heartbeat longer than was strictly necessary.

Those were the moments she held sacred in her heart, the true substance of her hope. Were they real, or was she, as Clara so often did, building castles in the air? Her mother’s words about his unsettled prospects echoed in her mind. She did not care for wealth, not truly. She had always believed that a sufficiency, with love and mutual respect, was the foundation of a happy life. But she knew the world did not always agree. A man with uncertain prospects was a risk, and her father was not a man who took risks, especially with his daughters’ futures.

She sighed and turned from the window. The joy she had felt was still there, a warm and steady flame in her heart. But now it was surrounded by shadows of doubt and the cold realities of her world. Thursday seemed a lifetime away, a day that promised either the blossoming of a beautiful hope or its quiet, sensible extinguishment.

The following day passed in a haze of anticipation. Every clatter of horse’s hooves on the lane, every knock at the door, sent a flutter of nervous energy through her. She tried to occupy herself with her usual duties, arranging flowers, writing letters, and helping Clara with a difficult piece of needlework, but her thoughts were constantly straying to Mr. Beaumont.

In the afternoon, they received a visitor. It was Mrs. Finch, her bonnet adorned with an alarming number of ribbons, her face alight with the thrill of news to impart.

“My dear Mrs. Sutton, my dear girls,” she began, settling herself into a chair with a rustle of silk. “I could not rest until I came to see how you were. I saw you had a safe escort home yesterday, Eleanor. A most distinguished escort, indeed.”

She beamed at Eleanor, her eyes sharp and inquisitive. Eleanor murmured a polite, noncommittal reply, acutely aware of her mother’s watchful presence.

“He is a charming man, is he not?” Mrs. Finch barrelled on, not waiting for an answer. “Quite the talk of the neighbourhood. Everyone is wondering how long he will stay, and of course, who he might be paying his addresses to. A man of his connections and appearance will not remain unattached for long, you may be sure of that.”

She paused for dramatic effect, leaning forward conspiratorially. “I was speaking with Mrs. Croft only this morning. She happened to mention that her niece, Miss Amelia Croft, finds him quite the most fascinating gentleman she has ever had the pleasure of meeting. And Miss Croft, as you know, is a very accomplished girl. She plays the pianoforte divinely. And with a dowry of ten thousand pounds, of course, she is a considerable catch for any man, even one from a family as fine as the Beaumonts.”

The name of Miss Croft, and the pointed mention of her fortune, landed in the quiet sitting room like a stone dropped into a calm pond. Eleanor felt a cold knot form in her stomach. She had seen Amelia Croft looking at Mr. Beaumont during their walk, her expression a mixture of admiration and proprietary interest. Amelia was pretty, accomplished, and wealthy. Eleanor was quiet, passably pretty, and would have only a modest dowry.

“A large fortune is certainly a great advantage in life,” Mrs. Sutton observed smoothly, her tone giving nothing away.

“Indeed it is,” Mrs. Finch agreed with relish. “It simplifies a great many things. Mrs. Croft seems quite hopeful of a match. She has invited Mr. Beaumont to dine with them on Friday. A small, intimate party.”

The news of this dinner invitation was another blow. Friday was the day after her proposed outing to the bookseller. While she and Mr. Beaumont were looking at books, he would be anticipating an evening in the company of the beautiful and wealthy Miss Croft.

Eleanor kept her composure, her hands folded neatly in her lap, her expression serene. She discussed the weather with Mrs. Finch, enquired after her health, and offered her another cup of tea. But inside, her heart felt heavy. The simple, radiant joy of yesterday was gone, replaced by a tangle of anxiety and comparison. She saw herself as the world must see her: Miss Eleanor Sutton, a respectable but unremarkable young woman, with little to recommend her over a rival with ten thousand pounds.

When Mrs. Finch finally took her leave, an awkward silence settled over the room. Clara was the first to break it, her face flushed with indignation.

“What a horrid woman! To come here and say such things. She did it only to vex you, Eleanor, I am sure of it. As if Mr. Beaumont would prefer that silly, simpering Amelia Croft to you! And as for her ten thousand pounds, it is vulgar to even speak of it.”

“It is not vulgar, Clara,” their mother said quietly, her eyes on Eleanor’s pale face. “It is a fact of life. Men, even the best of them, must consider such things. Mr. Beaumont must establish himself in the world. A wealthy wife would make that a simple matter.”

“But he spoke to Eleanor of kindred spirits!” Clara cried. “He would not speak so to her one day, and then pursue a fortune the next. He is a man of honour.”

“I hope you are right, my dear,” Mrs. Sutton said, though her tone lacked conviction. She rose and came to Eleanor’s side, placing a gentle hand on her shoulder. “Do not let gossip distress you, Eleanor. What is meant to be, will be. Thursday will come, and you will see him. Behave as you always do, with sense and propriety. The rest is not in our hands.”

But as Eleanor looked towards the window, at the bright, unclouded afternoon sky, it seemed to her that everything had changed. The walk to the bookseller’s on Thursday was no longer a simple, joyful prospect. It had become a test. A moment where she would have to weigh every word, every look, searching for a sign that his interest was genuine, that his talk of kindred spirits was more than just the pleasant, polished conversation of a London gentleman passing a few weeks in the country. The path ahead, which had seemed so clear and bright just yesterday, was now clouded with uncertainty.

The following day, Wednesday, passed in a haze of quiet dread. Eleanor found it impossible to settle to any task. The book she had been reading lay untouched on the small table by her chair, its story of steadfast love and eventual triumph now seeming like a foolish fantasy. She attempted some needlework, but her stitches were uneven, a testament to her unsteady hands and wandering mind. Her needle pricked her finger more than once, and she watched the small, perfect bead of crimson well up with a strange sense of detachment, as if the pain belonged to someone else.

Clara, ever observant of her sister’s moods, tried to draw her out. She chattered about a new ribbon she had purchased, about a letter from their cousin in Bath, about the gardener’s cat having kittens. Eleanor responded with gentle monosyllables, her thoughts circling endlessly around the same two points: Mr. Beaumont and Miss Croft. She pictured them together at the dinner on Friday. Miss Croft, in a fine silk gown, her hair artfully arranged, would smile and laugh. Mr. Beaumont, ever the gentleman, would be attentive and charming. And in the background of this elegant scene, the ten thousand pounds would stand like a silent, unassailable chaperone, endorsing the entire affair.

“You are thinking of him,” Clara said, finally abandoning her attempts at light conversation. She came to sit on a stool at Eleanor’s feet, her expression earnest. “You are letting Mrs. Finch’s poison work on you.”

“It is not poison, Clara, it is information,” Eleanor replied, her voice low. “It is a fact. He is to dine with the Crofts.”

“And what of it?” Clara insisted. “A gentleman may accept a dinner invitation without declaring his intentions for the daughter of the house. Papa dines with people all the time, and he has not once offered for any of their daughters.”

Eleanor gave a weak smile at her sister’s attempt at humour. “That is a little different, I think. Papa is already married to Mama.”

“The principle is the same,” Clara declared, undeterred. “It is a matter of civility. And besides, he is taking you to the bookseller’s tomorrow. That is a particular sort of engagement. It is not a dinner party with a dozen other people. It is just the two of you. It speaks of a preference, Eleanor. A decided preference.”

Eleanor wanted to believe her, to cling to the fragile hope that Clara’s words offered. But her own sober assessment of the world, a sensibility she had inherited from her mother, would not allow it. An outing to a bookshop was a pleasant, inexpensive diversion. A dinner party at the home of a wealthy merchant was a step towards a serious alliance. The two were not of equal weight.

Later that evening, as they sat with their mother, Mrs. Sutton looked at Eleanor over the rim of her spectacles. “You are very quiet, my dear.”

“I am merely tired, Mama.”

Mrs. Sutton set aside her mending. “Eleanor, the world is a complicated place. A good man like Mr. Beaumont finds himself with duties, not just to his own heart, but to his family, to his name. A gentleman’s position is not maintained by good intentions alone. It requires resources.” She paused, choosing her words with care. “I do not say this to discourage you. I say it so that you might guard your heart. It is a fragile thing, and best not bestowed too quickly, or without a clear understanding of the ground on which you stand.”

Her mother’s words, meant to be kind, felt like stones pressing down on her already heavy spirit. They confirmed her worst fears. The world saw Mr. Beaumont’s situation as a calculation, a matter of resources and advantages. His talk of kindred spirits, of shared sensibilities, was perhaps just a luxury he could afford to indulge in while the more serious business of his life was conducted elsewhere.

That night, sleep offered little respite. She dreamt she was in a vast library, the walls lined with books from floor to ceiling. Mr. Beaumont was there, but he was on the other side of a great, impassable chasm. He smiled at her and held up a book, but she could not read the title. Between them, a river of gold coins flowed, glittering and cold, and the sound of them was like mocking laughter.

Thursday dawned grey and overcast, the sky a blanket of uniform dullness that perfectly matched Eleanor’s mood. She dressed with a heavy heart, choosing a simple muslin dress of a pale lavender colour. It was modest and becoming, but as she looked at her reflection, she saw only its plainness. She imagined Miss Croft preparing for her dinner, selecting from a wardrobe of vibrant silks and delicate lace. The comparison was a fresh torment.

She was sitting in the parlour, feigning an interest in a ladies’ journal, when she heard the crunch of boots on the gravel path. Her heart gave a painful lurch. A moment later, the maid announced him, and Mr. Beaumont stepped into the room.

He was precisely as she remembered: tall and well formed, with that same intelligent light in his eyes. He smiled warmly, and for a fleeting moment, the oppressive clouds in her mind seemed to part.

“Miss Sutton,” he said, his voice a pleasant baritone. “I trust I find you well. I see the weather is not as cooperative as we might have hoped, but I have been assured it will not rain.”

“Mr. Beaumont,” she replied, rising to greet him. She was proud of the steadiness of her own voice. “It is good of you to call. I am quite prepared. A little grey sky does not deter me.”

Clara, who had been hovering near the doorway, gave her sister a look of fierce encouragement before disappearing. As Eleanor gathered her bonnet and shawl, she was acutely aware of his presence in their modest parlour. He seemed to take it all in with a polite, easy grace, but she wondered if he was comparing its simple comforts with the opulence of the Croft residence.

They set off, a proper distance between them as they walked down the lane towards the village. The initial ease that had characterized their first long conversation was gone. In its place was a stilted politeness. They spoke of the weather, of the hedgerows, of the prospect for a good harvest. Every word felt measured, every topic safe and utterly meaningless. Eleanor’s mind was a whirlwind of unspoken questions. She searched his face for any sign of change, any hint of distraction, but his countenance was as pleasant and unreadable as ever.

It was he who finally broke the superficiality of their exchange. They had reached a point where the lane opened up, offering a view of the rolling hills beyond the village. He stopped for a moment, looking out at the landscape.

“There is a certain honesty to a view like this,” he said, almost to himself. “It does not pretend to be anything other than what it is. London is not like that. In town, everything is a performance. One must constantly be aware of who is watching, of what is expected.”

Here was an opening, a chance to steer the conversation towards the topic that consumed her. Eleanor’s heart beat faster. “It must be a difficult way to live,” she said carefully. “To have one’s choices so closely observed. To have to weigh every action against the expectations of others.”

He turned to look at her, a thoughtful expression on his face. “It can be. A man is not always his own master. There are obligations, pressures… considerations that have little to do with one’s own inclinations.”

His words were a confirmation. He was speaking of his duty, of the necessity of a profitable marriage. The small flicker of hope she had nurtured died within her. She felt a chill that had nothing to do with the damp air.

“And those considerations,” she ventured, her voice barely a whisper, “must they always take precedence over one’s… inclinations?”

He held her gaze for a long moment, and she saw a flicker of something in his eyes, something that looked almost like regret. “A man of honour tries to reconcile the two,” he said slowly. “He hopes that duty and desire might one day point in the same direction. But when they do not, he must often choose the path of responsibility. It is the price of a good name and a secure future.”

The test was over. She had her answer. His talk of kindred spirits had been a pleasant diversion, a brief holiday from the serious business of his life, which was to secure his future. And that future lay not in a modest country parsonage with a woman of little fortune, but in a grand house with a wife who could bring him ten thousand pounds. The pain of it was a sharp, physical ache in her chest. She had been a fool to let her imagination run away with her, to build a castle of dreams on the sandy foundation of a few pleasant hours of conversation.

She forced herself to smile, a brittle, polite expression that felt alien on her face. “That is a very practical philosophy, Mr. Beaumont. The world would surely be a more orderly place if everyone adhered to it.”

Her tone was cooler than she intended, and he seemed to notice. A slight frown touched his brow. “It is not a philosophy I particularly enjoy, Miss Sutton. Merely an observation of reality.”

They walked the rest of the way to the village in a renewed, and now much heavier, silence. Eleanor felt as if she were encased in ice, moving and speaking but feeling nothing beyond a dull, pervasive ache of disappointment.

The bookshop was a refuge of quiet and the comforting scent of paper and leather. It was a small, crowded space, with books stacked high on every available surface. Mr. Henderson, the proprietor, a kindly man with spectacles perched on the end of his nose, greeted them with a gentle smile.

“Miss Sutton, a pleasure. And Mr. Beaumont. We are honoured to have you visit us again.”

Mr. Beaumont inclined his head politely. For a time, they browsed in separate aisles. Eleanor ran her fingers over the spines of a set of histories, her mind a blank. She could hear him on the other side of the high shelf, the soft rustle of pages as he turned them. The intimacy of their shared purpose, to look for books together, now felt like a mockery. She was an actress in a play, going through the motions of an enjoyment she no longer felt.

He came around the corner of the shelf, holding a slim volume of poetry. “Have you read this, Miss Sutton? The verse is not to everyone’s taste, but I find a certain raw truth in it.”

She took the book he offered. Her fingers brushed against his, and a jolt went through her, unwelcome and sharp. She looked down at the page he had opened. It was a poem about a sailor torn between the call of the sea and the love of a woman on the shore. About duty and the vast, lonely ocean. It was so close to the topic of their conversation that it felt like a deliberate, almost cruel, choice.

“I am not familiar with it,” she said, her voice tight. She handed the book back to him. “I confess, my taste in poetry is for less melancholy subjects.”

“Of course,” he said, his expression unreadable. He did not put the book back on the shelf, but kept it in his hand.

She moved away, feigning an interest in a collection of sermons. She felt trapped. The small shop, which she usually found so comforting, now felt claustrophobic. She wanted nothing more than to be at home, in the privacy of her own room, where she could let the mask of composure fall away.

She was determined to make a purchase, to complete the purpose of their outing and bring it to a close. She selected a novel she had been meaning to read, a simple domestic story with no grand passions or impossible choices. She took it to the counter, aware of Mr. Beaumont approaching behind her.

As Mr. Henderson wrapped her book in brown paper, Mr. Beaumont placed the volume of poetry on the counter beside it. “I shall take this as well, Mr. Henderson.” Then he turned to Eleanor. “I hope you will permit me, Miss Sutton. A small token of a pleasant afternoon.”

Before she could protest, he had paid for both books. Mr. Henderson handed her the parcel containing her novel. Mr. Beaumont took the small book of poetry.

“This is very kind of you, Mr. Beaumont,” she said, the words of polite society coming automatically. “But you should not have.”

“It is my pleasure,” he said. The simple statement hung in the air between them.

The walk home was even more strained than the journey there. The weight of all that had been said, and all that had been left unsaid, was a palpable thing. Eleanor clutched her book, the sharp corners of it digging into her palm. She felt a desperate need for the encounter to be over.

When they reached the gate of her family’s home, she stopped and turned to him. “Thank you for your escort, Mr. Beaumont. And for the book. It was very thoughtful.”

“The pleasure was all mine, Miss Sutton,” he said formally. He stood for a moment, as if there was more he wished to say. The grey sky seemed to press down on them. He looked at her, his expression serious, the easy charm he usually wore completely absent.

“Eleanor,” he began, using her given name for the first time. The sound of it on his lips was a fresh pain. “I… I hope you understand that things are not always as simple as they appear.”

“I believe I understand perfectly,” she replied, her voice cool and distant. She could not bear to hear any more veiled explanations or justifications for his pursuit of a fortune. She would not make it easy for him. She would not offer him the absolution of her understanding.

She saw the flicker of hurt in his eyes before he masked it. He gave a slight, formal bow. “Then I will wish you a good day.”

He turned and walked away, his back straight, his pace measured. Eleanor watched him until he was gone, the slim volume of poetry still in his hand. She realised with a jolt that he had bought it for himself, not for her. The sailor torn between love and the sea. It was his own story he had been reading.

She went inside, her composure holding just long enough for her to reach the sanctuary of her room. She closed the door behind her and leaned against it, the wrapped book clutched to her chest. The silence of the room was absolute. There were no tears, only a profound and hollow emptiness. The test was over. She had her answer. Mr. Beaumont was an honourable man, and he would do his duty. He would pursue the secure future that Miss Croft and her ten thousand pounds represented. And she, Eleanor Sutton, a kindred spirit with an empty purse, was merely a pleasant country diversion, a line of poetry he might recall fondly one day, before turning his attention back to the serious, practical prose of his life. The path ahead, which had once seemed so bright, was now not just clouded, but plunged into a deep and impenetrable shadow.

The wood of the door was cool and solid against her back. It was a grounding sensation in a world that had suddenly become thin and unsubstantial. Slowly, Eleanor peeled her fingers from the book, her knuckles white from the pressure. The brown paper wrapping was crisp. With meticulous, almost reverent care, she untied the string and folded back the paper. The title was embossed in simple gold lettering upon a dark green cover. It was a novel she had mentioned once, in passing, a story of a woman navigating a world of difficult choices, titled ‘The Measure of a Life’. The irony was so potent it was almost a physical blow. She had spoken of it as a testament to female resilience, and he had given it to her now as a parting gift, a manual for the lonely endurance that awaited her.

She placed the book on her small writing desk, not opening it. To read it now would feel like an act of surrender, an acceptance of the narrative he had chosen for her. The emptiness in her chest was a vast, cold cavern. She had expected grief, a storm of tears, the sharp agony of a heart broken. Instead, there was only this profound and silent void, an absence of feeling that was more terrifying than any pain. It was the quiet that follows a great battle, when one is too numb to count the dead.

A light tapping at her door startled her. “Eleanor? Are you within?”

It was her younger sister, Clara. Eleanor straightened up, composing her features into a mask of placid neutrality. “Yes, Clara. Come in.”

The door opened and Clara bustled in, her cheeks flushed from the cool air, her expression one of breathless curiosity. She was two years Eleanor’s junior, and possessed a romantic, uncomplicated view of the world that Eleanor had, until recently, shared.

“Well?” Clara demanded, her eyes wide. “You were gone an age. Did you enjoy your walk? Is Mr. Beaumont not the most charming man? I saw you from the parlour window. He has such a distinguished manner of walking.”

Eleanor forced a small, tight smile. “The walk was pleasant enough, Clara. The air was fresh.”

Clara was not so easily deterred. Her gaze fell upon the new book on the desk. “Oh, he brought you a gift! How wonderful! What is it? Is it poetry? He seems the sort of man who would appreciate poetry.”

“It is a novel,” Eleanor said, her voice flat. She moved to her dressing table, pretending to adjust a pin in her hair, unable to watch Clara’s innocent enthusiasm.

“A novel! How very thoughtful. He must hold you in the highest esteem, Eleanor. Everyone says so. Mrs. Gable was telling Mama only yesterday that she has never seen a man so attentive. She is quite certain he will make an offer before Christmas.” Clara clasped her hands together, a dreamy look in her eyes. “Can you imagine it? To be Mrs. Beaumont. You would be the most handsome couple in the county.”

Each word was a small, sharp pinprick against Eleanor’s fragile composure. She had imagined it. She had allowed herself the foolish, reckless indulgence of imagining that very thing. The memory was now a source of acute shame.

“Mrs. Gable is a gossip, and you are a romantic,” Eleanor said, her tone sharper than she intended. “Mr. Beaumont was merely being polite. An escort and a small book are matters of common courtesy, not declarations of intent.”

Clara’s face fell. “But the way he looks at you. I have seen it. It is not common courtesy.”

“You see what you wish to see,” Eleanor replied, turning from the mirror. She looked at her sister’s crestfallen face and felt a pang of guilt. It was not Clara’s fault. She was merely an echo of Eleanor’s own recent hopes. “Do not build castles in the air, Clara. They have a tendency to vanish when the morning comes. Now, I have some letters to attend to.”

It was a clear dismissal. Clara hesitated, a question still hovering on her lips, but she respected her older sister’s moods. She gave a small sigh and left the room, closing the door softly behind her. Alone again, Eleanor sank onto the edge of her bed. The mask dropped away. Building castles in the air. That was precisely what she had done. And Mr. Beaumont, with his kind eyes and his talk of kindred spirits, had provided her with all the plans and materials. But the foundation, she now understood, was built on the shifting sands of his own necessity.

The following days settled into a new and difficult rhythm. Eleanor maintained her outward composure, a feat of willpower that left her exhausted by each evening. She read to her father, whose eyesight was failing, she helped her mother with the household accounts, she walked with Clara in the gardens. She performed all the small duties of her life with a meticulous precision, as if by ordering the world outside her, she could somehow quiet the chaos within. But a greyness had seeped into everything. The books she read were mere collections of words, the music she played was a sequence of hollow notes, the familiar paths of the garden were just earth and stone.

She avoided any excursion into the village, terrified of a chance encounter. The thought of having to exchange pleasantries with Mr. Beaumont, of seeing that guarded, serious look in his eyes again, was more than she could bear. She heard his name mentioned, of course. He had attended a dinner at the squire’s estate. He had been seen riding with Miss Croft. Each mention was a small, fresh wound. Clara would watch her furtively at these moments, her brow furrowed with a mixture of concern and confusion, but she did not press her sister for an explanation.

A week after her walk with Mr. Beaumont, an invitation arrived. It was from the Allens, a well respected family who lived a few miles away, for a small dinner party. Mrs. Allen was a warm and perceptive woman, and her gatherings were known for their good food and even better conversation. Normally, Eleanor would have looked forward to it. Now, the cream coloured card felt like a summons.

“The Allens have invited us to dinner on Friday next,” her mother announced at breakfast, scanning the list of guests. “How lovely. The Vicar will be there, and the Gables. And oh, Mr. Beaumont is to attend. With Miss Croft, it says.”

Eleanor’s spoon stilled over her porridge. She felt a cold dread creep up her spine. She could not go. She could not sit at a table and watch him pay court to another woman.

“I am feeling a little unwell, Mama,” she said, her voice quiet. “Perhaps I should remain at home.”

Her mother looked at her, her gaze sharp and knowing. Mrs. Sutton was not a fool. She had seen the way Mr. Beaumont had distinguished her eldest daughter, and she had seen the shadow that had fallen over Eleanor this past week.

“Nonsense, dear,” she said, her tone leaving no room for argument. “It is just the change in the weather. A pleasant evening in good company is precisely the tonic you need. We shall all attend.”

Eleanor knew there was no escape. To refuse would invite a host of questions she was not prepared to answer. It would be an admission of defeat, a public acknowledgement that Mr. Beaumont’s presence had the power to undo her. Her pride, the last intact fortress of her heart, would not allow it.

And so, on Friday evening, she found herself in the Allens’ drawing room, a polite smile fixed on her face, her hands clenched so tightly in the folds of her pale blue gown that her knuckles ached. The room was brightly lit with candles, the air warm and filled with the low hum of conversation. She saw him almost immediately. He was standing near the fireplace, speaking to Mr. Allen. He looked handsome, his dark coat fitting his broad shoulders perfectly. And then he turned, and his eyes met hers across the room.

For a fleeting second, the carefully constructed mask of polite society fell away from his face. She saw a flicker of something raw and desperate in his gaze, a mirror of the turmoil in her own soul. Then it was gone, replaced by a cool, formal acknowledgement. He gave a slight inclination of his head, and she responded with a minuscule dip of her own. The exchange lasted no more than three seconds, but it left her breathless.

Miss Croft was soon at his side, a vision in rose pink silk, her smile bright and possessive. She was a pretty girl, Eleanor had to admit, with a sweet, if unremarkable, face and an air of placid contentment. She was everything a man in Mr. Beaumont’s position ought to desire. She was safe. She was secure. She was ten thousand pounds a year.

Dinner was an exercise in torture. Eleanor was seated between the garrulous Vicar and a quiet young cousin of Mrs. Allen’s. From her position, she had a clear, unobstructed view of Mr. Beaumont and Miss Croft, who were seated opposite. She watched him attend to her, refilling her wine glass, responding to her chatter with a smile that did not quite reach his eyes. He performed his role flawlessly. He was charming, attentive, the very picture of a suitor on the verge of a proposal. But Eleanor, who had studied his face so closely, could see the tension in his jaw, the way his gaze would occasionally drift towards the middle distance, his thoughts clearly a thousand miles away. He looked like a man playing a part in a play he had not written, speaking lines that left a bitter taste in his mouth.

She contributed to the conversation around her automatically, offering opinions on the latest sermon and the quality of the harvest, her mind a maelstrom of conflicting emotions. Seeing him like this, so clearly bound by a duty he found distasteful, did not bring her the satisfaction she had expected. There was no triumph in it. Instead, there was a deep, aching pity, both for him and for herself. They were two players in the same tragedy, each trapped by their own circumstances.

After dinner, the ladies retired to the drawing room, leaving the gentlemen to their port. The moment they were gone, the conversation turned, as it inevitably did, to matrimony.

“Miss Croft seems to have made quite a conquest of Mr. Beaumont,” Mrs. Gable observed, fanning herself with vigour. “Her father must be delighted. It is an excellent match for both parties.”

“He is a very serious young man, for all his charm,” another lady added. “He understands his obligations.”

Eleanor felt a familiar coldness settle over her. His obligations. The word hung in the air, a tidy euphemism for his poverty.

Mrs. Allen, who had been quietly observing Eleanor from her seat by the fire, spoke up. Her voice was gentle, but it cut through the idle chatter. “Mr. Beaumont’s obligations extend far beyond himself, I believe.”

All eyes turned to their hostess. Mrs. Allen was not a woman given to gossip, so when she spoke, people listened.

“His father, as you know, was a terrible speculator,” she continued, her gaze resting for a moment on Eleanor’s face. “He left behind a mountain of debt when he died last year. And he left a widow and three unmarried daughters with no provision at all. Mr. Beaumont has taken it all upon himself. He is determined to see his mother and sisters securely settled before he even considers his own happiness.”

A wave of understanding, so powerful it made her feel dizzy, washed over Eleanor. The room seemed to recede, the voices of the other women fading into a dull murmur. It was not just his own future he was securing. It was the future of four other women who depended entirely upon him. The ten thousand pounds a year was not for him, not for a life of ease and comfort. It was a lifeline for his entire family.

‘Things are not always as simple as they appear.’ His words echoed in her mind, no longer a vague excuse, but a stark and painful confession. He had not been justifying his ambition; he had been trying to explain the weight of his chains. And she, in her pride and her hurt, had refused to listen. She had judged him a common fortune hunter, when in fact he was a man making an immense sacrifice. An honourable man, doing his duty. Her own words came back to mock her. She had been right, but for all the wrong reasons.

When the gentlemen rejoined them, Eleanor could not bring herself to look at him. The knowledge she now possessed had changed everything. It coloured every glance, every polite smile he directed at Miss Croft. She no longer saw a performance, but a penance. The sailor in the book of poetry was not torn between love and the sea, not an adventurer choosing between two desires. He was a man being dragged to sea by a rope of duty, while the woman he loved stood on the shore, unable to help, misinterpreting his departure as a choice freely made.

Later in the evening, as a young lady played a passable sonata on the pianoforte, Mr. Beaumont found his way to her side. She was standing near a large potted fern, seeking refuge in its shadows.

“Miss Sutton,” he said, his voice low.

She turned, her heart thumping a painful rhythm against her ribs. “Mr. Beaumont.”

“I trust you are enjoying the evening,” he said, the formal words a stark contrast to the intensity in his eyes.

“Mrs. Allen is an excellent hostess,” she replied, her own voice sounding strange and distant to her ears.

He stood beside her, his presence a warm and unsettling weight in the air. For a long moment, he said nothing, simply looking at the other guests as if searching for words. “I hope you have had a chance to begin the book,” he said at last.

“I have not,” she answered honestly. “I have been… occupied.”

He nodded, his expression unreadable. “It is a fine story. About a woman of great strength and principle.” He paused, then added, so quietly she almost did not hear him, “She reminds me a great deal of you.”

The quiet sincerity of the compliment undid her. All the coolness, all the carefully constructed distance she had maintained, crumbled in an instant. She looked up at him, and he must have seen the change in her expression, the softening of her features, because a flicker of hope ignited in his eyes.

“Eleanor,” he began, his voice urgent, forgetting their surroundings, “what I tried to say to you the other day…”

“Mr. Beaumont! There you are!” Miss Croft’s cheerful, oblivious voice cut through the air like a blade. She appeared at his elbow, linking her arm through his. “Papa is asking after you. He wishes to discuss crop rotation.”

The hope in his eyes died, extinguished as quickly as it had appeared. The mask of the polite suitor slammed back into place. “Of course, Miss Croft. Forgive me.” He gave Eleanor a final, lingering look, a look filled with a world of unspoken things, of regrets and impossibilities. Then he turned and allowed himself to be led away.

Eleanor watched them go, her hand pressed against the cool glass of the windowpane behind her. The path ahead, which had been plunged in the simple darkness of her own disappointment, was now illuminated by a terrible, pitiless light. It was a labyrinth of his duty and her poverty, and there was no way through. The test was not over. It had only just begun. And the measure of her life, she now realised with a chilling certainty, would be the strength with which she could bear to watch the man she loved sacrifice his happiness for the sake of his honour.
