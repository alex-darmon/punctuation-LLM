The chains on his wrists felt like a cold premonition, and as Captain Valerius was marched into the Hall of Judgment, he felt a grim certainty that his end was a matter not of days, but of minutes. With the Lord Regent’s decree hanging over the city like a shroud, he could be forgiven for thinking that his daring escapade had been ill conceived, a fool’s errand to which a simple hanging was a merciful alternative. Yet he found himself possessed of a strange calm, a desire only to see the farce concluded, the sentence passed, and the matter done with, since done with it must be. His captors did not keep him waiting; the journey through the torchlit corridors was brief, and he was soon thrust into a chamber of black marble and stifling incense.

The court was already assembled, and seemed more a gathering for a decadent feast than a tribunal of life and death. The Lord Regent himself, a man whose face was a ruin of past indulgences, sat slumped in a high backed chair of gilded iron, his eyes half closed and heavy with wine. To his right stood General Kael, a brute of a man in polished steel, whose expression of smug authority was the most offensive thing Valerius had seen all night. It was Kael who began the proceedings, not with a question, but with a sneer that twisted his scarred lip.

“So this is the great Captain Valerius, the Serpent of the West. I had imagined someone taller.”

“And I had imagined a general who won his battles in the field, not in the dungeons,” Valerius replied, his voice echoing slightly in the cavernous hall. “We are both, it seems, to be disappointed.”

A low murmur went through the assembled nobles, and the Lord Regent stirred, his eyes opening a fraction wider. General Kael’s face flushed a deep, mottled crimson. “Silence, traitor! You will answer the questions put to you. You were apprehended within the city walls, bearing coded missives for the exiled Duke.”

“I was enjoying a walk in the moonlight,” Valerius said with a careless shrug. “As for any papers I may have carried, I cannot be held responsible for what your ruffians may have planted in my pockets after they had beaten me insensible.”

“Lies and insolence!” roared the General, slamming a gauntleted fist upon the marble table beside him. “We have your confession, signed by your own hand!”

“A man will sign anything when his fingernails are being introduced to hot irons. I would have confessed to poisoning the moon if your torturer had been more imaginative in his questions.”

The General seemed about to leap over the table, but the Lord Regent lifted a single, beringed finger, a gesture of almost lazy command. “Enough, Kael. His guilt is as plain as the nose on your face, which, I might add, he has just tweaked quite expertly. Let us be done with this. Have the scribes prepared the warrant?”

A nervous looking clerk hurried forward, holding a scroll of parchment with a trembling hand. He passed it to the General, who scrawled his name upon it with a flourish of vicious satisfaction. “It requires only your seal, my lord, and the matter is concluded.”

The Lord Regent took the scroll, his eyes scanning it with bored disinterest. Valerius watched him, his gaze steady, his heart a cold, hard knot in his chest. This was it, the final act of the play. The Regent fumbled with a stick of black wax and a heavy signet ring, his movements clumsy from drink. He was about to press the seal into the melting wax when a new voice, clear and musical as a silver bell, cut through the tense silence.

“Must we have the execution tonight, father?”

All eyes turned to a small alcove, where a young woman had been sitting, almost unnoticed, in the shadows. She was the Lord Regent’s daughter, the Lady Elara, a creature of such ethereal beauty that she seemed entirely out of place in this grim chamber. She regarded the proceedings with the mild curiosity one might afford a passing insect.

“It is a matter of state, my dear,” the Regent mumbled, pausing in his task.

“Yes, I am sure it is,” she said, her tone light and dismissive. “But the headsman is so dreadfully noisy, and I had hoped to hear the minstrels play. His chopping always puts them off their rhythm. Let it wait until the morning. I find bloodshed so tiresome before breakfast.”

General Kael’s face was a mask of thunderous fury, and he opened his mouth to protest, but the Lord Regent merely gave a soft, wet chuckle. He tossed the unsigned warrant back onto the table.

“As you wish, my love. The state can wait. Let the minstrels play. Guards, take the prisoner back to his cell. See that he is made uncomfortable, but leave his head precisely where it is, at least until sunrise.” The Regent waved his hand, his duty done, and slumped back into his wine induced stupor. The guards, with a shared look of bafflement, seized Valerius by the arms and dragged him from the hall, leaving General Kael staring at the unsigned death warrant as if it were a serpent that had just bitten him.

The chill of the dungeons was a familiar enemy, a damp and creeping thing that sought out the hollows in his bones. Valerius felt it reclaim him as the guards dragged him down spiraling stone staircases, the sounds of the Regent’s hall fading behind them, replaced by the rhythmic drip of water and the distant groan of iron. The two men holding him were a study in contrasts. The older one, a grizzled veteran named Joric with a face like a knotted root, gripped his arm with a punitive tightness, his knuckles white. The younger, a boy barely old enough to shave named Lyam, held him with a reluctant firmness, his eyes darting about as if the shadows themselves held accusations.

“Well, that was a fine spectacle,” Joric grunted, his voice a low rasp. “Never seen the like. The Regent’s little bird chirps, and a condemned man gets to see the sunrise. She’s got him wrapped around her finger, she does.”

Lyam said nothing, merely glancing at Valerius with a look of profound confusion. He saw not a monster, Valerius guessed, but a man, bruised and weary, who had just been the subject of the most bizarre pardon in the kingdom’s history.

They reached the lowest level, a place of profound darkness and despair. The cell they thrust him into was not the same one he had occupied before. This one was smaller, the stone slick with a foul smelling slime, and the straw on the floor was thin and damp. A single, high slit of a window showed only a sliver of impenetrable black sky.

“Orders are to make you uncomfortable,” Joric said, a cruel smile twisting his lips. He shoved Valerius hard against the far wall. The impact sent a jolt of fire through his tortured back, and he gasped, slumping to the floor. “But to leave your head on. Pity, that. I was looking forward to the morning’s entertainment. Don’t get too comfortable. The headsman’s axe will still be sharp at dawn.”

He slammed the heavy iron door, and the bolt shot home with a deafening clang. The sound echoed in the small space, a final, metallic punctuation mark on his fate. Lyam’s footsteps receded with Joric’s, and then there was only silence, broken by the slow, steady drip of water somewhere in the darkness.

Valerius lay on the cold stone, allowing the pain to wash over him. It was a tide, relentless and consuming, but he refused to drown in it. He forced his mind to work, to push past the agony. The Lady Elara. Her intervention was an anomaly, a stone thrown into the placid, corrupt waters of the court. Why? He had seen her only a handful of times, a fleeting vision of silk and jewels, always distant, always untouchable. Her reason, that the headsman’s work was noisy, was so utterly absurd, so patently false, that it had to be a mask for something else. But what?

Was this a game? A new, more refined form of torture devised by the General or the Regent? To give a man hope, the brief, intoxicating scent of a future, only to snatch it away at sunrise? It was a cruelty worthy of them. He pushed himself into a sitting position, his back screaming in protest, and leaned against the damp wall. He would not give them the satisfaction of breaking him. If he was to die, he would die as he had lived, with his mind clear and his will his own.

He closed his eyes, trying to piece together the fragments of his life that had led him here. His service to the old king, his warnings about the growing power of the northern lords, his arguments against the Regent’s ruinous taxes. He had spoken truth to power, and power had answered with a torturer’s irons and a forged confession. It was the oldest story in the world.

An hour passed, perhaps two. Time in the darkness was a fluid, unreliable thing. He was drifting in a state between wakefulness and exhaustion when a new sound reached him. Not the clang of a guard’s boot, but a soft, almost soundless scrape. The peephole in his cell door, a small iron grate, slid open.

He tensed, expecting the mocking face of Joric. Instead, the dim torchlight from the corridor framed a single, luminous eye, a pale grey iris flecked with silver. He knew that eye.

“I trust your accommodations are suitably uncomfortable,” a voice whispered, as clear and musical as it had been in the hall. Lady Elara.

Valerius pushed himself away from the wall, his heart beginning a slow, heavy drumbeat against his ribs. “They are everything a man in my position could hope for, my lady. Though I confess I miss the company of the rats from my previous cell. They were better conversationalists than the guards.”

A faint smile touched the lips visible through the grate. “Wit. How refreshing. It is a commodity in short supply in this castle. My father prefers his advisors dull and his generals angry.” The eye regarded him coolly. “You are wondering why I spoke.”

“The thought had crossed my mind,” Valerius admitted, his voice low and steady. “I am grateful for the reprieve, though I confess I do not understand its purpose.”

“Its purpose,” she said, her voice dropping even lower, a conspiratorial murmur, “is that General Kael is a rabid dog, and my father is too drunk to see that he is straining at the leash. Kael arranged your downfall because you opposed his plans for a new war in the north, a war that would make him the most powerful man in the kingdom. He believes you are the last obstacle to his ambition.”

The words struck Valerius with the force of a physical blow. He had suspected as much, but to hear it confirmed, and from such a source, was dizzying. “And you, my lady? Why should you care about the General’s ambitions?”

“Because I enjoy the minstrels,” she said, and for a moment he thought she was mocking him again. But her tone was different now, edged with something hard and sharp. “I enjoy the silks from the south and the wines from the west. I enjoy a world that is not perpetually draped in black for fathers and brothers and sons lost in some pointless northern campaign. Kael’s ambition is a threat to my comfort. And I do not abide threats to my comfort.”

It was the most selfish, most honest, and most believable motive he could have imagined. She was not a savior. She was a pragmatist, cloistered in a cage of luxury, determined to protect it at all costs.

“So you spared me,” Valerius reasoned, his mind racing. “To spite the General. A temporary stay of execution to wound his pride.”

“His pride is a minor consideration,” she whispered back. “His frustration is the point. An unsigned warrant is a temporary problem for him. A missing prisoner, however, is a permanent one. An escaped traitor, a man who knows his secrets, would be a ghost that would haunt him, a threat he could never be sure was truly gone. It would make him cautious. It would make him look over his shoulder. And a cautious general is a far less dangerous creature.”

Valerius felt a surge of something he had not allowed himself to feel for weeks. It was not quite hope, for hope was a gentle, fragile thing. This was harder, sharper. It was a chance.

“You mean to help me escape,” he breathed, the words tasting strange on his tongue.

“I mean to provide an opportunity,” she corrected him. “What you do with it is your own affair. Your success would be a convenience to me. Your failure would be a minor disappointment, and you would be dead regardless. The risk is entirely your own.”

Her detachment was as chilling as it was absolute. She was offering him his life with the same casual air she might offer a dog a scrap from the table.

“What is the opportunity?” he asked, his voice tight.

“This dungeon is old. Older than the castle above it. When they built the new wing, they sealed many of the old passages, but they were not as thorough as they believed. The masons who laid this cell floor were lazy. The cornerstone in the far left corner, behind the straw, is not properly mortared. It rests on a bed of sand and gravel. The stone is heavy, but a man with sufficient desperation might be able to work it loose. My father’s records keeper is a meticulous old man with a love for ancient architectural plans. And a weakness for my smile.”

Valerius’s gaze shot to the corner of the cell. It was lost in shadow, but he could imagine it, a single stone that was the key to everything.

“Below the floor is a drainage culvert,” Elara continued, her voice a rapid, precise whisper. “It is narrow and foul, but it connects to the old sewer system. Follow the main channel west, toward the sound of the river. It will eventually empty through a grated outfall into the Blackwater Rush, on the side of the castle opposite the main gate. The grate is rusted. A strong shoulder should be enough to break it.”

She paused, and he heard a faint rustling sound. A small, dark object was pushed through the grate, falling with a soft thud onto the stone floor.

“A gift,” she said. “From the kitchens. You will need your strength.”

Then, as suddenly as it had appeared, the eye was gone. The iron peephole slid shut with a soft click. He heard the whisper of her slippers on the stone as she departed, a ghost melting back into the shadows of the castle.

For a long moment, Valerius did not move. He was suspended between disbelief and a frantic, surging energy. He crawled across the floor, his hands searching in the darkness. His fingers brushed against a rough cloth. He lifted it. It was a small, dense loaf of dark bread and a hard wedge of cheese. It was the most magnificent feast he had ever seen.

He did not devour it. He forced himself to eat slowly, methodically, chewing each bite to reclaim his strength. The simple food was a miracle, a spark of fuel for a dying fire. As he ate, his mind worked, replaying Elara’s instructions. A loose cornerstone. A drainage culvert. The river. It was a desperate plan, a mad scramble through the filth and darkness beneath the seat of power. It was perfect.

When he had finished, he crawled to the corner she had indicated. He pushed aside the thin, damp straw. His fingers, raw and broken from the torturer’s attention, probed the edges of the large flagstone. He felt it. A slight give, a gritty looseness that was absent from the surrounding stones. It was real.

He began to work. At first, he used his bare fingers, trying to scrape away the gravel and sand packed in the seam. It was slow, agonizing work. The pain was immense, and he had to bite his lip to keep from crying out. He broke two of his already damaged nails, the sharp, searing pain making his vision swim. It was no good. He needed a tool.

His eyes scanned the bleak cell. There was nothing. Stone, straw, and the iron door. He thought of the spoon that usually came with the gruel, but they had not bothered to feed him. His gaze fell upon the small, earthenware bowl that had held his water. He smashed it against the floor. It shattered with a sharp crack that sounded like thunder in the silence. He froze, listening, but no alarm was raised. He fumbled for the pieces, selecting the largest, sharpest shard. It was a crude tool, but it was better than his bare hands.

He returned to the stone and began to dig, using the shard to scrape and pry at the packed earth and gravel. The work was brutal. Sweat beaded on his forehead and trickled into his eyes, stinging. His muscles, already weakened, screamed in protest. He worked in frantic bursts, then rested, his breath coming in ragged gasps, listening always for the sound of the guards.

Hours crawled by. The sliver of sky in the high window remained a deep, starless black. He lost all track of time, his world shrinking to the rough texture of the stone, the scraping sound of his tool, and the burning in his shoulders and back. He thought of Elara, the bored angel who had offered this impossible chance. She was playing a game of thrones with his life as a pawn, but he did not care. He would be her pawn, her ghost, her vengeful spirit, if it meant he could live to see another day.

Finally, after an eternity of effort, he felt a significant shift. The shard slipped deep into a cavity beneath the stone’s edge. He had cleared enough space. Now came the hardest part. He wedged the largest piece of the broken bowl into the gap and tried to lever the stone up. The shard cracked. He swore under his breath and threw the useless piece aside. He would have to use his hands.

He dug his fingers into the gap he had made, ignoring the fresh waves of pain. He braced his feet against the wall, took a deep breath, and heaved. The stone groaned, scraping against its neighbors, but it did not lift. He tried again, putting every ounce of his failing strength into the effort, his teeth gritted, a low growl escaping his throat. It moved. Only an inch, but it moved.

Again and again he pulled, his body trembling with the strain. He was fueled by a cold, pure rage. Rage at Kael, at the Regent, at the injustice that had brought him to this pit. That rage was a fire, and it gave him a strength he did not know he possessed. With a final, desperate wrench, the stone tilted upwards, and then over, falling onto the straw with a heavy, muffled thud.

He stared into the black hole he had created. A gust of foul, stagnant air rose to meet him, thick with the smell of decay and damp earth. It was the foulest, most welcome scent he had ever known. It smelled of freedom.

Without a second’s hesitation, he swung his legs into the opening and lowered himself down. The space was tight, his shoulders scraping against the rough-hewn stone of the culvert. His feet found the bottom, a shallow, slimy channel of sluggishly moving water. The darkness was absolute, a physical presence that pressed in on him from all sides. He took a moment, orienting himself, listening. He could hear the faint, distant sound of rushing water. West. He had to go west.

He began to move, half stooped, his hands trailing against the slimy walls to guide him. The water was frigid, soaking through his thin trousers, and the sludge at the bottom tried to suck at his boots with every step. Rats, disturbed by his presence, squeaked and scurried away in the blackness ahead. The journey was a nightmare of filth and claustrophobia, but his mind was sharp and clear. Every step took him further from his cell, further from the headsman’s axe.

After what felt like miles, the narrow culvert opened into a much larger space. The ceiling was higher here, and the sound of the river was a distinct roar. He was in the main sewer channel. The air was marginally better, and he could stand to his full height. He waded through the knee-deep muck, following the sound, his heart pounding a rhythm of fear and exultation.

He heard something else then. Voices. Muffled, coming from above. He froze, pressing himself against the curved brick wall. A grate in the ceiling directly over his head cast a faint, rectangular patch of torchlight onto the filthy water.

“...heard a noise from the lower levels,” a familiar voice grumbled. Joric. “Sounded like breaking pottery.”

“Probably just rats, old man,” another voice answered, younger, uncertain. Lyam. “You’re getting jumpy.”

“The Regent wants him alive for the morning, and the General wants him dead yesterday,” Joric shot back. “That’s enough to make any man jumpy. I’m doing a final check. You stay here. I don’t want that highborn witch finding me away from my post if she decides to come visiting her new pet again.”

Valerius held his breath, unmoving, as the sound of Joric’s heavy boots faded down a corridor above. He had been so close to being discovered. The thought sent a cold shiver down his spine that had nothing to do with the freezing water. Elara’s visit had been noted. His escape would be laid at her door, one way or another. He had not only saved himself, he had embroiled the Regent’s daughter in an act of treason. The game was more complex than he had imagined.

He waited until the silence was absolute again before moving on, more cautiously this time. The roar of the river grew louder until it was a deafening thunder. He saw it then, a faint luminescence ahead. Moonlight, filtering through the outfall grate.

He slogged the final hundred feet, his legs heavy as lead. The grate was a lattice of thick, rusted iron bars set into a stone archway. Through it, he could see the wild, churning surface of the Blackwater Rush, silvered by the moon. He put his shoulder to it and pushed. The rusted metal groaned but did not yield. He took a step back, drew a breath, and threw his full weight against it. A hinge shrieked in protest. He hit it again, and then again, a desperate, battering ram of flesh and bone.

With a final, earsplitting screech of tortured metal, one side of the grate tore free from its ancient stone mooring. It swung open just enough for a man to squeeze through.

Valerius scrambled out of the sewer, falling into the shallow, fast-moving water near the riverbank. The cold was a shocking, breathtaking force, but it was clean. It washed the filth from his clothes and the despair from his soul. He hauled himself onto the muddy bank, gasping, shivering, and looked back at the fortress.

The castle was a colossal silhouette of towers and battlements against the night sky, its windows like scattered, malevolent eyes. From a high balcony, faint music drifted on the wind. The minstrels. Elara was listening to her music, her part in the drama complete.

He was free, but he was a hunted man in a kingdom run by his enemies. He had no allies, no resources, and no name. All he had was a sliver of a chance, given to him by a bored and calculating princess, and a burning need for justice. The sky to the east was beginning to soften from black to a deep, bruised purple. Dawn was coming. And with it, the hunt would begin.

He did not linger by the river. The biting wind cut through his wet rags, and the cold was a physical presence, a thing that lived in his bones and threatened to freeze him where he stood. Survival was a series of small, desperate decisions. The first was to move. He pushed himself to his feet, his muscles screaming in protest, and forced his legs into a stumbling run. He kept to the shadows thrown by the city’s outer wall, following the curve of the river downstream, away from the fortress that had been his cage.

The ground here was a treacherous mix of mud and discarded refuse. The air was thick with the smell of brine, rot, and the distant, cloying scent of the tanneries. This was the city’s forgotten edge, its dumping ground. It suited him perfectly. No one of consequence would walk these banks at this hour. His only company was the rats, bold and fat, that scurried from his path.

His mind, sharp and clear despite the exhaustion, raced ahead. Prisoner’s garb. Wet. Shivering. These things made him a beacon. He needed clothes. He needed to be dry. He needed to melt into the city’s anonymous squalor before the sun rose fully and the alarm was sounded. He knew it would come. The moment Joric went to check the cell and found it empty, the castle would erupt. Bells would toll, not for prayer or celebration, but for the hunt. The Iron Gauntlets, the Regent’s personal enforcers, would pour into the streets. They were thorough. They were brutal. And their commander, General Kaelen, was the man who had put Valerius in that cell in the first place. No, that was a mistake. His mind was fogged. General Vorlag. Kaelen was someone else entirely, a memory from a different life. The confusion was a warning. He was weaker than he thought.

He pressed a hand to his side, feeling the raw, bruised flesh under the thin fabric. The guards had not been gentle. Pain was a constant companion, a dull throb that sharpened with every jarring step. He ignored it. Pain was a luxury he could not afford.

The city began to stir around him. A lone fisherman poled a small skiff out into the current, his lantern a tiny, dancing star on the black water. A baker’s boy, yawning, pushed a cart laden with fresh loaves along a cobbled street above the riverbank, the scent of warm bread a torment to Valerius’s empty stomach. He watched from the shadows, a ghost observing a world he was no longer a part of. The cart squeaked to a halt as the boy adjusted a wobbly wheel. It was a risk, a terrible risk, but his body was screaming for fuel.

He moved with a predator’s silence, his bare feet making no sound on the damp earth. He climbed the short, muddy embankment, his eyes fixed on the cart. The boy was grunting with effort, his back to him. Valerius’s hand shot out, quick as a striking snake, and snatched a small, dark loaf from the back of the pile. He retreated into the shadows as the boy, oblivious, gave the wheel a final kick and continued on his way.

Valerius tore into the bread with his teeth. It was coarse and hard, but it was the most magnificent thing he had ever tasted. He devoured it in ragged, desperate bites, forcing himself to chew, to not choke. The small measure of strength it gave him was immediate and profound. It was a start.

Next, clothes. He pushed deeper into the warren of streets that made up the Underside, the district huddled between the city wall and the river. Here the buildings leaned against each other for support, their upper floors jutting out to nearly touch, blocking the nascent light of dawn. Laundry lines were strung between windows, a chaotic tapestry of damp rags and mended tunics. He found what he was looking for: a dark, heavy wool cloak, patched and worn, hanging from a low line over a narrow alley. It smelled faintly of fish and sweat. He pulled it down without a second thought, wrapping it around his shivering shoulders. The rough wool was abrasive against his skin, but it was dry, and it hid the hated grey of his prison shirt. He pulled the hood low over his face, instantly becoming just another shadow in a city full of them.

He was moving through the Fishmonger’s Gut now. The cobblestones were slick with scales and offal. Men with hard faces and strong hands were already at work, hauling baskets of silver-scaled fish from boats tied to rickety wooden piers. Their shouts and curses were a language of their own. No one gave him a second glance. A hooded figure in a worn cloak was not an unusual sight here. He was invisible. For now.

Then he heard it.

A single, deep bell tolling from the direction of the fortress. A pause. Then another. And another. It was not the dawn bell. It was slower, more deliberate. An alarm. A man has escaped. The hunt is on.

The mood on the docks shifted instantly. Men paused in their work, looking up towards the castle. Guards on the city walls, previously little more than statues, were now moving with purpose. He saw a patrol of the City Watch, four men in leather jerkins, marching at a quick pace down a wider thoroughfare. They were not the Iron Gauntlets, not yet, but they were a sign. The net was being cast.

Panic, cold and sharp, tried to claw its way up his throat. He forced it down. Panic was the enemy. Panic made a man run, and a running man draws the eye. He slowed his pace, forcing himself to walk with the shuffling gait of a beggar or a drunkard heading home after a long night. He kept to the narrowest alleys, his shoulders brushing against damp stone walls.

He had to get off the streets. But where to go? Every inn would be watched. Every boarding house would have a landlord eager to earn a few coins by reporting a suspicious stranger. He needed a place to lie low, to think, to let the first frantic hours of the search pass.

And then the name surfaced again, this time with clarity. Kaelen. Not the General. Kaelen the Mapmaker. He had been Valerius’s tutor, years ago, before his father’s fall from grace, before the Regent’s coup. Kaelen was a quiet scholar, a man who believed in knowledge above all else. He had been a friend to Valerius’s father, one of the few who had not publicly denounced him when the tide turned. He lived in the Rookery, the city’s most notorious slum, a place the Watch only entered in force. He kept a small, cluttered shop filled with scrolls and charts, a tiny island of forgotten lore in a sea of poverty and crime. Kaelen was discreet. He was loyal, Valerius hoped. And his shop was the one place in the city that might offer a true sanctuary.

The Rookery was on the other side of the city, a long and dangerous journey. He would have to cross the Grand Concourse, pass the barracks of the Iron Gauntlets, and navigate the labyrinthine markets. But it was a destination. A plan. And a plan was a weapon against the despair that threatened to consume him.

He began the slow, careful trek across the city. The sun was higher now, casting long shadows that offered some cover. The streets were filling with people. Merchants setting up their stalls, acolytes hurrying to the Great Sept, nobles in carriages preceded by armed guards. He was a piece of flotsam in the great, flowing river of humanity, trying desperately not to be noticed.

He kept his head down, his eyes scanning his surroundings from beneath the shadow of his hood. He saw the proclamations already being nailed to posts by city officials. He was too far away to read the words, but he did not need to. He knew they would describe him. A traitor. Dangerous. A reward for his capture, dead or alive. His face, the face of Lord Valerius of House Aris, would be plastered across the city. Or perhaps not. Perhaps the Regent would want to keep the escape quiet for a time, to avoid showing weakness. He could not be sure. He had to assume the worst.

A patrol of Iron Gauntlets rounded a corner ahead. There were six of them, clad in black plate armor that gleamed even in the grey morning light. Their helmets were fashioned into snarling beasts, and they carried heavy, steel-shod pikes. They moved with an arrogant swagger that cleared the street before them. Valerius melted back into an archway, pressing himself into the shadows, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. They passed without a glance. Their faces were grim, their eyes searching. They were hunting. They were hunting him.

He waited until they were gone, his breath held tight in his chest. The proximity to his hunters sent a fresh wave of adrenaline through him. He was a fox with the hounds on his scent.

He crossed the Grand Concourse by mingling with a crowd of pilgrims, their drab robes and pious expressions making for excellent camouflage. He kept his head bowed as if in prayer, his stolen cloak blending in with their own. He could feel the eyes of the guards on the battlements above, knew that at any moment a shout could ring out, that his fragile anonymity could be shattered. But no shout came. He made it across, his nerves frayed, and plunged back into the relative safety of the narrower streets.

The character of the city changed as he neared the Rookery. The stone buildings gave way to timber and plaster, the streets became unpaved and choked with filth. The air grew heavier, smelling of unwashed bodies, cheap gin, and despair. This was a place the sun seemed to shun. Here, the law was not the Regent’s, but the Guild’s, or whichever cutthroat was strong enough to claim a corner for his own.

He found the street. Weaver’s Lane. It was more of a cramped alley than a proper lane. He remembered it from a single visit years ago, when he had come to deliver a message for his father. Kaelen’s shop was at the very end, marked by a faded wooden sign of a quill and scroll.

He approached with extreme caution. His instincts, honed by months of imprisonment and a lifetime of courtly intrigue, were screaming at him. Something was wrong. The lane was too quiet. The usual collection of beggars and pickpockets was absent. He slowed his approach, using a pile of overflowing rubbish bins as cover. He peered around the edge, his eyes fixed on the shop.

The door was closed. The windows were shuttered. But that was not what was wrong. What was wrong were the two men standing in the shadows of the doorway across the lane. They were trying to look like common laborers, dressed in stained leather jerkins and roughspun trousers. But they were not laborers. Their posture was too rigid, their hands rested too comfortably on the hilts of the short swords at their belts. And their eyes, they never stopped moving, scanning the lane, watching Kaelen’s door.

Iron Gauntlets. Out of uniform.

A trap. They had anticipated he might come here. Kaelen was compromised. Or worse. A cold dread, far colder than the river water, seeped into Valerius’s soul. His one hope, his one sliver of a plan, had turned to ash. Kaelen was either a prisoner himself, or he was dead.

Valerius backed away slowly, carefully, not making a sound. He could not help his old friend. To even try would be suicide. He was alone again, truly alone, in the most dangerous part of the city, with the Regent’s hunters closing in on his only known sanctuary. The small victory of his escape felt hollow now, a mere prelude to a slower, more public death.

He stumbled back into the main thoroughfare of the Rookery, a place teeming with a desperate and dangerous humanity. A pickpocket eyed his cloak, a child with ancient eyes begged for a coin he did not have, a legless soldier from forgotten wars stared at him with empty sockets. The sun was fully up now, a pale, indifferent disc in the hazy sky. The city was alive and roaring, but for Valerius, it had become a cage far larger and more terrifying than the one he had just left. He had nowhere to run, nowhere to hide, and the faces of his hunters were waiting for him around every corner. The hunt had just begun.

He turned away from Weaver’s Lane, melting back into the sluggish river of humanity. The street was a cacophony, a symphony of desperation played on strings of hunger and fear. A fishmonger shouted prices for his less than fresh wares, his voice a rasping gull’s cry. A street preacher with wild eyes and a matted beard proclaimed the end of days from atop a crate, his words swallowed by the din. Children, thin and feral, darted through the crowd like rats, their nimble fingers searching for a moment’s inattention. Every shove, every accidental bump, sent a jolt of pure adrenaline through Valerius’s veins. He was a fox in a kennel of hounds, and he did not know which ones had the scent.

His cloak, the one he had thought so coarse and unremarkable when he had stolen it from the prison guard, now felt like a royal mantle. The cut of it, the way it hung from his shoulders, was wrong. The fabric, though stained and torn, was too well woven. In the dim light of the dungeons, it had been a disguise. Here, under the sickly yellow sky, it was a target. He saw the glances it drew. The calculating appraisal in the eyes of a burly man with a scarred face. The covetous stare from a woman nursing a babe with skin the color of old parchment. He was a prize sheep wandering into a wolf pack, and his fleece was his death warrant.

He had to change. He had to shed the last vestiges of the man he had been. The thought was a bitter pill. He was Valerius of House Arcanthia. His name had commanded respect, his presence had silenced rooms. Now he needed to become no one, a nameless, faceless specter in the city’s forgotten corners. He ducked into a narrower alley, the stench intensifying to a physical blow. Here, the noise of the main street was muffled, replaced by the drip of unseen water and the scuttling of vermin in the shadows. He leaned against a grimy brick wall, its dampness seeping through his tunic, and forced himself to think.

Money. He needed money. For clothes, for food, for a room where he could be invisible for a few hours. He had nothing. His pockets were empty. His signet ring, the golden band bearing the falcon crest of his house, was still on his finger. He had kept it through his imprisonment, a secret reminder of who he was. It was a connection to his father, to his name, to a life that now seemed like a dream. Selling it felt like severing his own hand. But what was a hand to a man about to lose his head?

He pushed himself off the wall, a new, grim purpose hardening his features. He needed to find a place where such a thing could be sold, a place that would not ask too many questions, or at least, a place whose questions could be answered with lies. He remembered tales from the city guards, stories of the Rookery’s black markets, of pawn shops that were fronts for thieves' guilds, of fences who could make a nobleman’s treasure disappear into the city’s gut. He began to walk again, no longer aimlessly, but with the focused gait of a predator. He observed the flow of the alleyways, the furtive figures who slipped in and out of shadowed doorways. He was looking for a sign, any sign, of the underworld’s commerce.

After what felt like an hour of navigating the labyrinthine passages, he saw it. Tucked between a gin house leaking raucous laughter and a tenement that seemed to be held up by sheer force of will, was a small shop. There was no sign, only a single, grimy window displaying a random assortment of junk. A dented brass locket, a set of mismatched knives, a single child’s boot. Above the door, nailed askew, was a preserved magpie, its glass eyes staring out with a dead, knowing glint. It was a place that traded in forgotten things, in stolen memories and desperate bargains. It was perfect.

He took a deep breath, the air thick with the smell of boiled cabbage and stale liquor, and pushed the door open. A small bell, rusted and cracked, gave a dull thud instead of a chime. The interior of the shop was even more cluttered than the window suggested. Shelves overflowed with tarnished silver, chipped pottery, and books with broken spines. Old clothes hung from the rafters like hanged men, releasing a musty scent of dust and decay. The air was still and close, as if secrets were a physical substance that had settled on every surface.

Behind a counter made of splintered, dark wood stood a woman. She was older than she looked and younger than her eyes suggested. Her hair was a wiry nest of grey and black, pulled back from a face that was a roadmap of hard living. But her eyes were sharp, the color of flint, and they missed nothing. They swept over Valerius, taking in his ragged cloak, his nobleman’s bearing that he could not quite shrug off, the desperation clinging to him like a shroud.

“Lost, lordling?” she asked, her voice a dry crackle, like autumn leaves skittering across pavement.

Valerius felt a prickle of alarm. She had seen through him in an instant. He moved closer to the counter, making sure the door was fully closed behind him. He kept his hands in view, away from his body. He knew the etiquette of places like this. Trust was a currency more valuable than gold, and no one here had any to spare.

“I’m looking to sell something,” he said, his voice lower than usual, attempting a roughness it did not naturally possess.

The woman’s lips twisted into something that might have been a smile on a different face. “Everyone who comes through that door is looking to sell something. Their goods, their honor, their last hope. What’s yours?”

He hesitated. To reveal the ring was to reveal his station. It was a risk, but a necessary one. He slowly pulled his hand from beneath his cloak and laid it on the counter, palm down. He did not need to point. The glint of gold, even in the dim light of the shop, was enough. Her flinty eyes narrowed, focusing on his finger. She did not move, did not show any surprise. She simply watched him, her expression unreadable.

“A pretty thing,” she said, her tone flat. “Heavy. Too heavy for a man trying to be light on his feet. Where did a man like you get a ring like that?”

“It was my father’s,” Valerius said, the truth feeling safer than a lie.

“And who was he?”

“A man who is dead,” he replied, the words tasting like ash.

The woman let out a short, sharp huff of air. It was not a laugh. It was the sound of a cynic having their worldview confirmed. “They all are. Take it off. Let me see it.”

Valerius twisted the ring. It was snug on his finger, a familiar weight he had known his entire life. Removing it felt wrong, a betrayal. He worked it over his knuckle and placed it on the counter. The falcon crest seemed to glare up at him. The gold shone like a captured piece of the sun, entirely out of place in this den of shadows.

The shopkeeper picked it up, not with her fingers, but with a small pair of iron tongs. She held it up to a sliver of light coming through a crack in the boarded window, turning it over and over. Her expertise was obvious. She was not just looking at the gold. She was reading its story, the quality of the engraving, the faint scratches of a lifetime of wear.

“House Arcanthia,” she murmured, her voice barely a whisper. She looked up at him, and for the first time, there was something other than weary cynicism in her eyes. It was a flicker of what might have been fear, or perhaps just calculation. “You are a long way from home, Valerius.”

His blood ran cold. She knew him. The name, spoken aloud in this place, was a death sentence. His hand instinctively went to the hilt of a sword that was not there.

“Relax,” she rasped, setting the ring down gently. “Your face has been on every crier’s notice from the Citadel to the Serpent’s Mouth. The Regent wants you back. Badly. There’s a bounty that would make a king blush.”

Valerius stared at her, his mind racing. He was trapped. She could scream. She could send a runner to the nearest Gauntlet patrol. His escape would end here, in a dusty, forgotten shop, at the hands of an old woman.

“So why haven’t you?” he asked, his voice strained.

“Because the Regent’s gold is loud,” she said, leaning forward slightly, her flinty eyes boring into his. “It draws attention. The Iron Gauntlets come, they take their prize, and they ask questions. They turn over every stone, look in every dark corner. That is bad for business. My business.” She tapped a long, bony finger on the countertop. “I prefer quiet. And you, lordling, are the opposite of quiet.”

The bell above the door gave its dull thud again, and another man stepped inside. He was huge, a mountain of muscle and greasy leather, his face a mess of scars with a broken nose set in the middle. He grunted in greeting to the woman and began to browse a rack of rusty blades, his presence sucking the air from the small room. Valerius felt the man’s eyes on his back. His escape window was closing.

“What do you want?” Valerius asked the woman, keeping his voice low.

“That ring,” she said, gesturing to it with her chin, “is a problem. It’s a marker. A beacon. Anyone who knows the great houses will recognize it. It’s too hot to sell, too dangerous to keep. It’s worth a fortune, and it’s worth nothing.”

“But you’ll take it,” Valerius stated. It was not a question.

“I will,” she confirmed. “I will melt it down. The falcon will fly no more. Its gold will become a dozen lesser things. Anonymous things. In exchange, I will give you what you need. Not what you want, mind you. What you need.”

The large man grunted again as he tested the edge of a cleaver, the sound unnervingly close. Valerius had no choice. He was a drowning man, and she was offering him a rotten piece of driftwood. It would not save him, but it might keep his head above water for another hour.

“What are you offering?” he asked.

“Clothes,” she said, her eyes flicking to his cloak. “Something a ratcatcher would be ashamed to wear. A loaf of bread, the hard kind. A heel of cheese. And a place to stay for one night. Not here. A flophouse a few streets away. You’ll be one of twenty men crammed into a room that smells of death, but no one will look at your face twice. And I will give you one piece of information. The most valuable thing of all.”

It was a pittance. The ring was worth enough to buy this entire miserable street. But it was also everything. It was anonymity. It was sustenance. It was shelter. It was hope, in its most wretched and diluted form.

“And the information?” he pressed.

“After you change,” she said. “The man in that cloak is not the man who gets to hear it.”

He nodded. “Agreed.”

She gave a curt jerk of her head toward a curtain in the back of the shop. “Through there. Leave your old life on the floor.”

Valerius walked behind the counter and pushed through the moth eaten curtain. The room beyond was a small storage space, piled high with junk. In the corner was a bundle of clothing. He picked it up. It was a collection of roughspun trousers patched with at least three different fabrics, a tunic that was more stain than original color, and a leather jerkin so stiff it could have stood on its own. The smell was appalling, a combination of sweat, mold, and something vaguely fishy.

He stripped off his own clothes. The prison tunic, the stolen cloak. They felt like a different man’s garments now. He left them in a heap on the filthy floor. As he pulled on the new clothes, he felt the change. The rough fabric scratched his skin. The fit was all wrong, tight in the shoulders and loose at the waist. But with every piece he donned, he felt the weight of Lord Valerius sloughing off. He was becoming someone else. A ghost. A nobody.

There was a shard of a mirror hanging from a nail on the wall, its surface so clouded it looked like smoked glass. He looked at his reflection. The man staring back was a stranger. His face was gaunt, smeared with grime from the sewer pipe. His eyes were shadowed with exhaustion. His high cheekbones, once considered a mark of noble breeding, now just made him look starved. Dressed in these rags, he was no longer a lord. He was just another piece of the Rookery’s refuse. He felt a pang of profound loss, a mourning for the man in the mirror he no longer was. But beneath it, there was something else. A flicker of liberation. Lord Valerius could be hunted. But who would hunt for this wretch?

He stepped back out into the shop. The large, scarred man had gone. The shopkeeper was wrapping the bread and cheese in a piece of oilcloth. She looked up at him and gave a slow, appraising nod.

“Better,” she said. “Now you just look desperate, not desperately out of place.”

She pushed the small bundle across the counter, along with a single, tarnished copper coin. “For the flop. Tell Joric that Elara sent you. He’ll give you a spot on the floor.”

Valerius took the food. His stomach rumbled in anticipation. “The information,” he reminded her.

Elara leaned on the counter again, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “The Iron Gauntlets have the whole district in a quiet lockdown. They’re shaking down the usual informants, pressing the guild bosses. They’re looking for you, and they’re being methodical. But they make the same mistake all of the Regent’s men make. They think they control the flow of this city.”

She paused, her eyes holding his. “They don’t. Information in the Rookery is a river. They can dam the main channels, but it always finds new paths. If you want to know what’s really happening, if you want to find a way out, you don’t go to the guild halls. You go to the Sump.”

“The Sump?”

“A tavern,” she explained. “Down in the deepest part of the Rookery, where the old city drains into the river. It’s not run by any one guild. It’s neutral ground. Spies, smugglers, outcasts, informants… they all drink there. In the Sump, whispers are worth more than gold. But be warned,” she added, her voice hardening. “It is not a place for the weak. You survived the Regent’s prison. That is nothing. Survive one night in the Sump, and you might actually have a chance.”

She straightened up, her manner becoming dismissive. Their transaction was complete. “Now get out of my shop. I don’t know you. I never saw you. The falcon is dead.”

Valerius nodded, clutching the small parcel of food. He turned and walked to the door, his new, ill fitting clothes feeling strange against his skin. He did not look back. He stepped out into the alley, and the city swallowed him whole. He was no longer Valerius of House Arcanthia. He was a ghost, a rumor, another nameless soul in the filth and the fog. He had a crust of bread, a place to sleep, and a destination. The Sump. It sounded like the end of the world. For him, it might be the only place to find a new beginning. The hunt was far from over, but the prey had learned a new and terrible trick. It had learned how to disappear.

The alley was a throat, and it had swallowed him. Valerius moved with a purpose he did not feel, his feet finding purchase on slick, uneven stones. The fog was a shroud, muffling sound and turning the weak glow of distant lanterns into hazy, jaundiced eyes. Every shadow seemed to hold a threat, every drip of water from an overhanging eave was the footstep of an Iron Gauntlet. He clutched the oilcloth bundle, the rough texture a grounding reality in a world that had become a nightmare. The bread and cheese were not just food; they were a weight in his hand, proof that he was still alive, still a part of the world of the living.

He had to find the flop, Joric’s flop. Elara’s directions had been simple, a series of turns spoken in a low, clipped tone. Left at the Tanner’s Yard, right at the broken fountain, look for the sign of the three blind rats. The names meant nothing to him. His city had been one of marble plazas and grand avenues, of districts named for noble houses and historic victories. This was a different geography altogether, a map drawn in filth and desperation.

He forced himself to walk not with the measured stride of a lord, but with the shuffling, head down gait of the defeated. He kept his eyes low, scanning the ground ahead, avoiding eye contact as if the very act of meeting a gaze was a challenge he could not afford. The Rookery was a living thing, and its language was one of postures and glances. He saw it in the way a hunched figure scurrying past flattened himself against a wall to let a trio of swaggering dockhands pass. He saw it in the quick, predatory flicker of a money lender’s eyes from a darkened doorway. He was learning, his senses raw and screaming with the effort of it.

The smell of the Tanner’s Yard hit him like a physical blow, a wall of chemical stench and rot that made his empty stomach clench. He gritted his teeth and made the turn. The broken fountain was little more than a pile of rubble in a small, muddy square, its basin choked with refuse. A pack of skeletal dogs watched him pass with unnerving stillness. He counted his paces, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. He was exposed here, away from the tight press of the alleys.

Then he saw it. A splintered piece of wood hanging from a single rusted hinge over a low, soot stained doorway. Carved crudely into its surface were three shapes that could, with a generous imagination, be identified as rats. Their eyes had been gouged out, leaving three empty holes. He hesitated for only a second, then pushed the warped door open and slipped inside.

The air was thick and hot, heavy with the smell of unwashed bodies, stale sweat, and cheap, throat stripping gin. A low ceiling crisscrossed with dark, heavy beams seemed to press down on the room. The only light came from a single, sputtering tallow lamp on a crude wooden table. In its weak, flickering orbit, he could make out shapes lying on the floor. Dozens of them. Men, and a few women, packed together on thin straw pallets, wrapped in rags, their breathing a chorus of snores, coughs, and troubled mutters.

A mountain of a man sat at the table, his bulk blocking most of the lamplight. He was bald, his head and face a roadmap of scars, and he was methodically sharpening a long, wicked looking knife on a whetstone. The rhythmic scrape of steel on stone was the only clear sound in the room. This had to be Joric.

Valerius approached the table, his new boots making no sound on the dirt floor. The man did not look up, but the motion of the knife paused.

“What?” The voice was a low rumble, like rocks grinding together.

“Elara sent me.” The words felt foreign in his own mouth, rough and clipped. “She said you’d have a spot.”

Joric finally lifted his head. His eyes were small and dark, chips of obsidian buried in a fleshy face. They swept over Valerius, taking in the ragged clothes, the gaunt features, the way he held himself. It was a look that stripped away all pretense, a butcher’s appraisal of a piece of meat. Valerius held out the tarnished copper coin.

Joric’s massive hand engulfed the coin without a word. He gestured with the knife toward the far wall, where a small, empty space lay between a snoring drunkard and a pile of what looked like discarded fishing nets. “There. No noise. No trouble. You cause trouble, I use this.” He patted the knife’s blade with his thumb, a gesture that was both casual and deeply menacing. “You’re gone by sunrise.”

Valerius nodded, not trusting himself to speak. He retreated to the designated space, the eyes of the giant on his back the entire way. He sat down, his back against the damp, cold stone of the wall. The straw was thin and smelled faintly of mildew. The man next to him groaned in his sleep and rolled over, his breath a foul wave of soured ale.

For the first time in what felt like a lifetime, he was not in a cell. He was not being watched by a guard. He was anonymous, just another piece of human flotsam. He unwrapped the oilcloth. The bread was dark and dense, the cheese hard and salty. He broke off a piece of bread and chewed it slowly, deliberately. It tasted of earth and grain and survival. In his old life, he had dined on roasted pheasant with spiced wine, on delicate pastries and fruits from the southern continent. He had taken it all for granted, the endless parade of flavors and textures. This simple, coarse fare was more satisfying than any of those forgotten feasts. It was real. It was earned. Each bite was a victory, a small act of defiance against the Regent and the fate he had planned for him.

He ate half the bread and cheese, wrapping the rest carefully and tucking it inside his shirt. He lay down, pulling the thin, scratchy tunic tighter around himself. Sleep would not come easily. The air was too foul, the symphony of misery too loud. But he could rest. He closed his eyes and let the darkness of the flop house merge with the darkness behind his eyelids. He did not dream of his palace, or his titles, or the woman he had been forced to leave behind. He dreamed only of shadows, of a river of information, of a place called the Sump.

He woke before the sun, a gray, watery light just beginning to filter through the grime on the single high window. The flop was already stirring, a place of groans and coughs as its inhabitants prepared to face another day. Valerius did not wait for the sun. He rose, every muscle aching from the hard floor, and slipped out of the door as silently as he had entered. Joric was still at his table, his head resting on his massive arms, the knife lying beside his hand. He did not stir.

The morning fog was even thicker, clinging to the stone and muffling the world. The Rookery was waking up, and the sounds were different now. The clang of a smith’s hammer, the rumble of a cart’s wheels on the cobbles, the shouted curses of vendors setting up their stalls in some unseen market. The city was a machine, and its gears were beginning to grind.

He ate the rest of his bread and cheese as he walked, forcing himself to move with a purpose. He had a destination. According to Elara, the Sump was at the Rookery’s lowest point, the place where the warren of streets and alleys finally gave up and bled into the river. He just had to keep going down.

It was a slow descent into a deeper hell. With every street he crossed, the buildings seemed to lean closer together, blocking out more of the sky. The air grew heavier, thick with the smell of stagnant water and coal smoke. Canals of green, scummy water replaced some of an alley’s cobblestones, crossed by slick, treacherous wooden planks. Rats, bold and fat, skittered in the open, unconcerned by his presence.

He saw the Iron Gauntlets twice. The first time, it was a patrol of four men, their polished steel breastplates and helms a jarring sight in the pervasive decay. They moved with arrogance, their gauntleted hands resting on the pommels of their swords, their eyes scanning the faces of the wretches who scurried out of their way. Valerius ducked into a doorway reeking of rotten fish, turning his face to the wall and making himself small until they passed. Their casual brutality was a cold weight in his gut. They were not just soldiers; they were an occupation force in their own city.

The second time was more dangerous. He was navigating a narrow, winding lane when he heard raised voices from around the bend. He pressed himself into a shallow alcove behind a pile of crates. Peeking through a crack, he saw two Gauntlets pinning a small, weaselly man against a wall.

“We know you hear things, Ratkin,” one of the guards snarled, his voice echoing in the confined space. “A nobleman. Tall, with the look of a falcon. Have you seen him?”

The man, Ratkin, was trembling, his eyes wide with terror. “No, ser. Honest. I seen nothing. I hear nothing. The guilds, they said to keep our mouths shut.”

The guard backhanded him across the face. The sound was a sharp, wet crack. “The guilds don’t pay us. The Regent does. We are looking for a traitor. Hiding him is the same as treason. Think on that.” They let him go, and he slumped to the ground, sobbing. The Gauntlets shared a look of disgust and continued on their way.

Valerius waited until their footsteps faded completely. His heart was hammering. They were close. They were methodical. And they were using fear as their primary weapon. He understood now why Elara had warned him away from the usual channels. The Regent’s grip was a fist, and it was squeezing the Rookery hard. The Sump was his only hope.

He continued his descent. The ground became permanently damp, the stone walls slick with a fine, greasy moss. The air tasted of salt and mud. He could hear the low, sluggish lap of water now, the sound of the great river that bordered the city. He passed under a massive, arching sewer outlet, a torrent of the city’s filth pouring into a churning brown basin below. Elara had said the tavern was where the old city drains into the river. This had to be the place.

He found it in an alley that was more of a stone-lined trench. It ended in a solid wall of rock, but set into it was a single, heavy door of dark, waterlogged wood, bound with pitted iron. There was no sign, no name, only a single, hooded lantern hanging from a hook, its flame sputtering and casting long, dancing shadows. A low, continuous murmur emanated from behind the door, a hive-like hum of voices. This was the Sump.

He took a deep breath, the foul air filling his lungs, and pushed the door. It was heavy, resisting him for a moment before swinging inward with a low groan. He stepped across the threshold and into another world.

The inside of the Sump was a cavern. The ceiling was low, supported by thick, rough-hewn pillars that looked like petrified trees. The air was a thick soup of pipe smoke, roasting meat, and the sweet, cloying scent of something he couldn't identify. It was crowded, a sea of bodies packed around small, rickety tables. Torches in iron sconces provided a guttering, smoky light that made everything seem to shift and writhe. A huge, roaring fire pit dominated the far wall, spitting sparks and casting a hellish red glow on the faces of those gathered near it.

It was a menagerie of the city’s forgotten and forbidden. Dockworkers with arms as thick as tree limbs drank alongside lithe, shadowy figures who never showed their faces. A group of sailors from a southern vessel, their skin dark and covered in intricate tattoos, were laughing loudly in a corner. He saw a disgraced alchemist with stained fingers arguing with a river smuggler whose face was a mask of scars. A woman with eyes as hard as flint sat alone, calmly sharpening a pair of daggers on a whetstone, ignoring everyone around her. No one wore the livery of a guild. No one displayed the crest of a house. Here, identity was a cloak to be worn or discarded at will.

And no one paid him any mind. He was just another shadow slipping into a room full of shadows. His ragged clothes, which had felt like a costume, were a uniform here. He was invisible. The feeling was both terrifying and liberating.

He moved carefully through the press of bodies, his senses on high alert. This place was a predator’s den, and he was new meat. He needed to find a corner, a place to observe without being observed. He spotted an empty stool at the very end of a long, battered bar, tucked into a nook near a dripping section of the stone wall. He made his way there, keeping his shoulders hunched and his eyes down.

The bartender was a massive woman with a stony face and arms corded with muscle. She was missing an eye, the empty socket covered by a worn leather patch. She glanced at him, her one good eye appraising him in an instant. “What?” she grunted.

“Ale,” Valerius said, his voice a low rasp. He slid one of his few remaining coppers onto the wet, sticky wood of the bar.

She drew a mug of something dark and frothy from a large cask, slapped it down in front of him, and swept the coin away in one fluid motion. The transaction took less than three seconds.

He took a tentative sip. The ale was thin, bitter, and tasted vaguely of the river. It was foul, but it was a prop. It gave him a reason to be there. He wrapped his hands around the crude earthenware mug, feeling its slight warmth, and he began to listen.

Information is a river, Elara had said. In the Sump, it was a raging flood, a chaotic mess of currents and eddies. He heard dozens of conversations at once, a tapestry of whispers, boasts, and complaints.

"...the price of Glimmer Dust is through the roof since the Gauntlets cracked down on the Wharf Rats..."

"...he tried to cheat me at cards. Tried. He’ll be feeding the crabs by morning..."

"...the Sea Serpent is sailing with the tide, but her captain is looking for discreet cargo. Heavy cargo..."

It was all noise, the everyday commerce of the underworld. He filtered it out, listening for something else. A mention of the Regent, of the Iron Gauntlets, of escape. He took another sip of the terrible ale, his eyes scanning the room, trying to match voices to faces. He learned to ignore the loud, boisterous groups. The real secrets were in the quiet corners, in the words spoken from behind a raised hand, in the conversations that stopped abruptly when someone new drew near.

An hour passed. Then another. The smoke and the noise were a physical weight, pressing down on him. His initial fear had subsided, replaced by a cold, calculating patience. This was not so different from a royal court, he realized. Both were full of predators and prey, of shifting alliances and whispered secrets. The currency was different, gold and favors instead of coppers and threats, but the game was the same.

And then he heard it. A conversation from a nearby table, occupied by two men hunched over their drinks. One was a wiry man with nervous, darting eyes. The other was older, his face a web of wrinkles, a pipe clenched between his teeth.

"The Gauntlets are crawling all over the ports," the nervous man whispered, his voice tight with anxiety. "Every ship is being searched stem to stern. They turned my cousin's boat inside out looking for something. Or someone."

The older man took a long draw from his pipe, exhaling a cloud of fragrant smoke. "They look in the light," he rasped, his voice like dry leaves skittering across pavement. "They always look in the light. They forget the city is older than they are. Deeper."

"What do you mean?"

The old man leaned in closer, his voice dropping even lower. Valerius had to strain to hear him. "They control the gates and the docks. They do not control the tunnels. They do not control the dark." He took another puff of his pipe. "The Regent's father, in his wisdom, tried to seal the Old Cistern Tunnels. Said they were unsafe. But you can't seal a wound that deep. The Eel knows. He still uses them. From the undercroft of the abandoned textile mill, all the way to the Graywater Estuary, clear past the sea forts. A straight run to the open water, if you don't mind the dark and the smell. And if you can afford his price."

The Eel. The name hung in the air like the pipe smoke. A route out. A path the Iron Gauntlets did not know. Valerius felt a jolt, a spark of electricity in the cold dread of his existence. This was it. This was the new path the river of information had taken.

He kept his face impassive, his eyes fixed on his mug of ale. He took a slow, deliberate drink, forcing the bitter liquid down his throat. He did not look at the men. He did not react. In the Sump, showing interest was a weakness, an invitation for betrayal. The information was his now, a tiny, precious seed of hope.

He had survived the night. He had a name. He had a destination. Finding a man called the Eel in the labyrinth of the Rookery would be a new kind of hunt, more dangerous than anything he had faced before. But for the first time since the prison gates had slammed shut behind him, Valerius of House Arcanthia felt something other than despair. He felt the thrill of the chase. The prey had found a scent to follow, a path out of the trap. He finished his ale, set the empty mug down on the bar, and melted back into the shadows of the city, his hunt for the Eel about to begin.
