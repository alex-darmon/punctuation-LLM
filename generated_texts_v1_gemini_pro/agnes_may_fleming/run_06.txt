With the chill of the dungeon still clinging to his bones like a lover’s unwelcome ghost, Master Gideon could be forgiven for thinking that his life’s great work in arcane philosophy had led him to a singularly unimpressive end. A stone floor, a jeering crowd, and a judge whose wig was so preposterously large it seemed to possess a gravitational pull of its own; it was a farce of the highest order, and yet one for which the price of admission was his own head. He felt a strange, feverish desire for the entire affair to conclude, for the judgment to be passed and the axe to be sharpened, so that he might at last have some peace from the relentless pomp of it all.

Nor did the proceedings disappoint in their alacrity; they had barely arranged their robes and cleared their throats before the Chief Proctor, a man whose face was a perpetual sneer carved in sour lard, rose to his feet. The Duchess, for her part, lounged upon her high backed chair, a creature of ivory and apathy, fanning herself with a fan of black swan feathers. Beside her, the Lord Confessor, a gaunt man robed in shadows, watched Gideon with eyes that burned like cold blue coals, a faint, reptilian smile playing about his thin lips. It was this smile, more than the baying of the crowd or the stern face of the Proctor, that sealed Gideon’s certainty of his doom.

“The prisoner will state his name and his crime,” the Proctor boomed, his voice echoing in the vaulted chamber with a self importance that grated on the ear.

“My name is Gideon Fell,” the scholar replied, his own voice quiet but carrying with a scholar’s precision. “As for my crime, I must confess my ignorance. You have dragged me from my library on some charge of which I have not yet been properly informed. Perhaps your excellency could enlighten me, and we could both be the wiser for it.”

A ripple of amusement went through the assembled nobles, and the Proctor’s face darkened to a shade of mottled plum. “Insolence will not serve you here, sorcerer! You stand accused of consorting with unholy powers and plotting against the security of Her Grace, the Duchess Callista!”

“Indeed! Then I can only say Her Grace’s security must be a fragile thing, if it can be threatened by a man whose only companions are books and whose most potent conspiracy is the formulation of a new ink,” Gideon retorted, his gaze flicking to the Duchess herself. For a moment, he thought he saw a flicker of interest behind the bored mask, but it was gone as quickly as it appeared, and she merely stifled a delicate yawn.

“He confesses his treasonous craft!” hissed the Lord Confessor, leaning forward so that his face emerged from the gloom of his cowl. “He speaks of his inks and his letters, the very tools he used to pen his pacts with the netherworld!”

“My lord, if every man who makes ink is a sorcerer, then every clerk in this city is a demonologist, and your own scribes are a veritable legion of hell,” said Gideon coolly. “I regret only that I did not think to turn one of them into a toad before my arrest; it would have at least lent some credence to this theatrical display.”

The Lord Confessor recoiled as if struck. “Blasphemy! He mocks the holy office and this court! He is condemned by his own tongue!”

“I quite agree,” said the Proctor, seizing his moment and snatching up a scroll from the table before him. “The evidence is overwhelming, the confession plain. Gideon Fell, you are found guilty of high treason and the practice of forbidden arts. Have you any final words before sentence is pronounced?”

“Only that I am astonished at the speed with which you dispense with justice in this city. It is a model of efficiency, if not of sense,” Gideon said, giving a slight, mocking bow.

“It is a speed you will soon appreciate,” the Proctor snarled, and he passed the scroll to a page, who carried it with trembling hands to the Duchess. She took it without looking, her expression one of utter disinterest, and reached for a quill. The feather tip hovered over the parchment, a breath away from tracing the signature that would end his life. Gideon watched that hand, slender and white, and felt a strange calm settle over him.

But the quill did not fall. The Duchess paused, her head tilted, and a new light entered her eyes. It was not kindness, nor was it mercy; it was the sharp, predatory gleam of a cat that has suddenly noticed a mouse it had previously overlooked.

“One moment, Proctor,” she said, her voice a low, musical chime that cut through the stuffy air of the court. “You say he is a sorcerer?”

The Proctor, startled by this unexpected interruption, bowed low. “Indeed, Your Grace. A most foul and dangerous practitioner of dark magic.”

“And he can, for instance, turn a man into a toad?” she asked, a slow smile spreading across her perfect lips as she turned her gaze not on Gideon, but on the Lord Confessor, whose own reptilian smile had frozen on his face.

“So his impudent boast would suggest, Your Grace!” the Proctor affirmed.

“How very fascinating,” the Duchess murmured, setting down the quill and tapping a finger on the death warrant. “We are bored with simple beheadings. They are so dreadfully crude. Let the prisoner be taken back to the Tower. If by sunrise tomorrow he can transform the Lord Confessor into a toad, he shall have his freedom. If he fails, he will be burned. It will be a far more entertaining spectacle for the court. See that it is done.”

The Proctor’s mouth hung open, a perfect little ‘o’ of astonishment. The Lord Confessor, however, was a study in apoplectic rage. His face, already sallow, mottled into a patchwork of livid purple and waxy white. He took a staggering step forward, one hand clutching the holy symbol at his neck as if to ward off the Duchess’s very words.

“Your Grace! This is… this is a mockery! A circus! The man is a convicted traitor, a warlock! He should be purified by fire, not indulged in his satanic boasts!”

The Duchess did not even grant him a glance. Her eyes remained on Gideon, a glimmer of profound and terrible amusement in their depths. She waved a dismissive hand, the gesture as final as a headsman’s axe. “I am the law in this city, Lord Confessor. And I am bored. Your objections are noted and, like so many other tedious things today, they are ignored. The court is adjourned.”

Two guards, their faces impassive beneath their steel helmets, moved to flank Gideon. One of them nudged him roughly in the small of his back with the butt of a halberd. “Move, sorcerer.”

Gideon offered the Duchess a second, deeper bow, a gesture of pure, unadulterated irony. “I am Your Grace’s most humble and, for the moment, most motivated servant. I shall endeavor to provide a memorable morning’s entertainment.”

He was marched from the hall, the buzz of the stunned courtiers following him like a swarm of bees. He could feel the Lord Confessor’s hateful glare burning into his back, a heat more palpable than any pyre. As they descended the winding stairs back toward the dungeons, the sounds of the court faded, replaced by the damp chill of stone and the rhythmic clank of the guards’ armor. Gideon walked with his head held high, his mind a whirlwind. He had stared into the abyss of a swift, predictable death, and a bored aristocrat had thrown him into a different abyss entirely, one that was deeper, darker, and infinitely more absurd.

Freedom if he could turn a man into a toad. It was the sort of bargain a desperate fool would make with a crossroads demon, not a sentence handed down by a civilized court. But then, Gideon had long suspected that civilization was merely a thin, gilded veneer over the same old cruelties. The Duchess had just proved him right. She did not want justice; she wanted a show. And he was now the star performer, the lead actor in a farce where the only prop was a funeral pyre.

His cell was as he had left it: a cube of sweating stone, furnished with a pile of straw that smelled of mold and despair. The heavy door slammed shut behind him, the sound of the bolt shooting home a final, echoing punctuation mark. He was alone. The single, high window showed only a sliver of darkening sky. He had until sunrise.

For a long moment, Gideon simply stood in the center of the small space, listening to the frantic beating of his own heart. The calm he had felt in the courtroom, the intellectual detachment of a man observing his own demise, had evaporated. In its place was a cold, sharp terror. He was no sorcerer. He was a scholar, a man of letters and chemistry. He had spent his life among books and alembics, studying the properties of pigments and the composition of acids. His secret work, the work that had led him here, was in creating inks that could not be faded by light or washed away by water, inks that could carry messages unseen within their very fabric. It was a kind of magic, he supposed, the magic of knowledge, but it would not allow him to violate the fundamental laws of nature.

He sank down onto the straw, running his hands through his hair. He could not perform a miracle. He could not transform flesh and bone. He would fail. And he would burn. The image of the flames, of the agony, licked at the edges of his mind. He pushed it away. Panic was a luxury he could not afford. The Duchess had given him a stage. She had given him an audience. And she had given him his chief accuser as a co-star. There had to be a way. A performance did not need to be real; it only needed to be convincing.

He began to think. Not as a condemned man, but as he always had: as a scholar presented with an impossible problem. The problem was not magic. The problem was belief. The entire court, from the Proctor to the lowest page, already believed him to be a sorcerer. The Lord Confessor screamed it from the rooftops. They had done half the work for him. All he needed to do was provide the climax.

What were the properties of a toad? It was amphibious, cold-blooded, with warty skin. It often had a swollen, bloated appearance. Its tongue was long and darting. He could not make the Lord Confessor a toad. But could he make him *look* like one?

His mind raced through the pharmacopoeia he kept in his memory, the vast catalogue of herbs, minerals, and animal extracts he had studied. He needed something to induce paralysis, something to cause discoloration of the skin, something to make the tongue swell and protrude. He needed a poison. A very specific, non-lethal poison that would mimic the traits he required.

There was the venom of the Five-Spotted Puffer, which could numb the limbs and make the face slack. But he did not have a fish. There was Foxglove, which could disrupt the heart and turn the skin a bluish hue, but he had no access to an apothecary’s garden. He had stone, straw, a wooden bucket for waste, a clay jug of brackish water, and the coarse woolen tunic on his back. It was nothing.

Hours passed. The sliver of sky in the window turned from deep indigo to black, pricked with a few cold stars. He paced the cell, a caged animal, his mind sifting through forgotten lore and obscure chemical treatises. And then he stopped. He remembered a passage in an old text, a footnote in a chapter on fungal growths. It described a particular type of lichen, the pale, crusty sort that grew on damp, sunless stone. *Petram Ranam*, the alchemists had called it. Toadstone. It was not magical. The name was a fancy, derived from the warty appearance of the fungus. But the text claimed that when powdered and mixed with a weak acid, like soured wine or vinegar, it produced a substance that could cause temporary numbness on contact and, if ingested in a small dose, severe cramping, bloating, and a pronounced, greenish pallor to the skin.

He knelt, his fingers tracing the cold floor. In the corner, where the damp was thickest, he felt it. A rough, scaly patch of growth. It was a desperate chance, a flimsy thread of hope, but it was all he had. Using a sharp edge of stone he flaked from the wall, he began to painstakingly scrape the lichen from the floor, gathering the pale grey powder in a fold of his tunic.

He was deep in his work when he heard the scrape of the bolt. The door creaked open, and a pool of torchlight spilled into his cell. Standing there, silhouetted against the flame, was the Lord Confessor. He was alone, having dismissed the guards.

“The witch at his work,” the Confessor sneered, his voice dripping with venomous satisfaction. His reptilian smile was back in place, wider and more cruel than ever. “Making your pathetic potions? Calling on your pathetic demons? They will not help you. At sunrise, your soul will be forfeit, and your flesh will be ash.”

Gideon rose slowly, carefully concealing the gathered powder. He adopted an air of weary power, as if this interruption were a great and tiresome inconvenience. “You should not have come here, Confessor. The air in this place is thin. It is difficult to breathe when one is filling the space with spirits.”

The Confessor scoffed, though he took an involuntary step back. “Your tricks are at an end. The Duchess plays her games, but God’s judgment is absolute. I came to offer you one final chance at salvation. Confess your sins, renounce your master, and I will pray for your soul as you burn. You may even be granted the mercy of a quick strangulation before the flames touch you.”

Gideon smiled, a slow, chilling expression. This was the moment. He needed a catalyst, an acid. And the Lord Confessor, in his righteous fury, might just provide it. “Your mercy is as thin as your faith, my lord. You did not come here to save my soul. You came to gloat. You came because you are afraid. You are afraid that I can do it.”

“Afraid?” The Confessor’s laugh was a dry rattle. “I am a shield of the Almighty! I am afraid of nothing, least of all a charlatan in a dirty shirt.”

“Then you will not object to a small condition for tomorrow’s… ceremony,” Gideon said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “The transformation requires a conduit. A sympathetic link between the practitioner and the subject. The subject must offer something of himself to the spell, willingly.”

The Confessor’s eyes narrowed. “What sort of devil’s bargain is this?”

“No bargain,” Gideon said, waving a dismissive hand. “A simple component. To ensure the magic is focused, the subject must partake of a sacramental libation. With me. A sharing of wine, to create the bond.” He watched the Confessor’s face carefully. “Of course, if you are afraid the wine might be poisoned, you can bring it yourself. Your own vintage, from your own cellars. Poured from a single bottle into two cups, by your own hand. That way, you can be certain there is no mortal trickery involved. You will drink one, and I the other. Then the true magic can begin.”

He had laid the trap. By suggesting poison himself, he made the idea seem like a common ruse he was disdainfully casting aside. He was appealing to the Confessor’s arrogance, to his belief that Gideon’s power was supernatural, not chemical. A man obsessed with demons would never suspect a trick involving simple fungus.

The Lord Confessor stared at him, his mind clearly turning the proposal over. The chance to participate, to prove his fearlessness before the entire court while watching Gideon seal his own doom, was powerfully tempting. To drink with the warlock, from the same bottle, would be the ultimate act of defiance against the powers of darkness.

“You think to play games with me, sorcerer?” he hissed.

“I am simply explaining the requirements of the art,” Gideon replied coolly. “If you lack the courage, I am sure the Proctor would be willing to stand in your place. The spell requires a subject, not necessarily *you*. Though the Duchess specifically requested you. It would be a shame to disappoint her.”

That was the final, perfect barb. The thought of being replaced, of looking a coward in the eyes of the Duchess, was intolerable to the Lord Confessor. His vanity was a greater force than his caution.

“I do not lack courage,” he spat. “Fine. I will bring the wine. A deep Arbor Red, consecrated by my own chaplain. We will drink your devil’s toast. And when you fail, I will personally light the kindling beneath your feet.”

He turned on his heel and stormed out, slamming the door behind him. The bolt crashed home. Gideon leaned against the cold stone wall, letting out a breath he did not realize he had been holding. He had the lichen. And now, he had his acid. Wine was not ideal, but a good, strong red would be acidic enough. He had the delivery method. And he had a subject who would willingly, even eagerly, drink the dose.

He spent the next hour working in the near darkness. He used the sharp stone to grind the lichen powder against the floor until it was as fine as dust. He had no cup, so he would have to use his hands. The plan was risky. A speck of powder too much, and he might actually kill the man. A speck too little, and nothing would happen. The dosage had to be perfect. He measured it out by eye, a tiny mound of grey dust that represented his only chance at life.

As the first hint of grey began to soften the black square of the window, he heard the guards returning. The door opened. It was time.

The courtyard was already crowded. The city’s nobility had turned out in their finest silks and velvets, their faces alight with morbid curiosity. It was a festival of death. At the center of the yard stood the pyre, a brutish cone of dry timber stacked high. To one side, on a raised dais draped in purple, sat the Duchess. Her expression was one of placid, elegant boredom, but her eyes, fixed on Gideon as he was led out, shone with predatory anticipation.

The Proctor stood beside her, looking nervous. And near the pyre, waiting, was the Lord Confessor. He held a silver tray. On it sat a dark green bottle of wine and two simple pewter cups. His smile was one of absolute triumph.

Gideon was halted before the dais. “Well, Master Fell,” the Duchess’s musical voice floated down. “The sun is climbing. The stage is set. Are you prepared to give us our show?”

“I am, Your Grace,” Gideon said, his voice steady. He turned to the Lord Confessor. “Is the sacramental wine prepared as we discussed?”

“It is,” the Confessor said loudly, for the benefit of the crowd. “One bottle of pure, blessed wine. Poured by my own hand, into two identical cups. So that all may see there is no poison, no trickery. Only your magic against the power of my faith.”

He poured the dark red liquid into the cups. The crowd murmured. This was high drama. The sorcerer and the holy man, drinking from a shared bottle before the final contest. The Confessor picked up one cup and held it out to Gideon. He took the other for himself.

Gideon took the cup. His hands were steady. As he raised it, in a gesture that looked like a toast, he curled the fingers of his other hand, the one in which he had held the tiny pinch of lichen powder concealed in his palm. In a single, fluid motion, as he brought the cup toward his lips, he let the powder fall from his thumb into the Confessor’s cup. It was a sleight of hand worthy of a market cutpurse, executed under the gaze of a hundred nobles. The dark red of the wine hid the powder instantly.

“A toast,” Gideon said, his voice ringing across the silent courtyard. “To transformations.”

The Lord Confessor sneered. “To justice.”

He drained his cup in one defiant gulp. Gideon barely touched his own to his lips before lowering it. All eyes were on the Confessor. He stood tall, chest puffed out, a triumphant smirk on his face as he set the empty cup down. He looked at Gideon, then at the Duchess, then at the crowd. He opened his mouth to declare the sorcerer’s failure.

But no words came out.

A look of confusion crossed his face. He blinked. He swallowed hard. His smirk faltered. He touched a hand to his throat, his eyes widening in sudden panic. A low, gurgling sound escaped his lips. The crowd leaned forward as one.

The Lord Confessor’s face began to change. His already sallow skin took on a strange, greenish tint, like old bronze. His jowls seemed to swell, his neck puffing out. He staggered back, his hands clawing at his collar, his breath coming in ragged, croaking gasps. His legs, suddenly weak, buckled beneath him, and he fell to his knees, his body seized by violent cramps that made him hunch over, his back bowed and lumpy.

He looked up, his eyes wide with a terror so profound it was almost sublime. His mouth opened in a silent scream, and his tongue, swollen and dark, lolled out. A string of spittle connected it to his chin. He collapsed onto his stomach, his limbs twitching feebly, his whole body bloated and still. From the crowd, a woman shrieked. Then another. The Lord Confessor, the righteous shield of the Almighty, lay on the cold stones of the courtyard, looking for all the world like a great, green, expiring toad.

Gideon stood perfectly still, the picture of a sorcerer who had just expended a great deal of energy. He let the silence stretch, letting the horror and astonishment of the crowd solidify into belief.

Then he turned to the dais. The Proctor was as white as a sheet, his jaw slack. The guards were frozen, their hands gripping their halberds, looking from the twitching form on the ground to Gideon as if he were a demon made flesh.

But the Duchess was smiling. It was no longer the smile of a cat with a mouse. It was the radiant, utterly delighted smile of a connoisseur who has just witnessed a masterpiece. She began to applaud, her gloved hands making a soft, rhythmic patter in the stunned silence.

“Bravo,” she said, her voice filled with genuine admiration. “Bravo, Master Fell. A far more entertaining spectacle than we had hoped for. You have met the terms of the bargain.” She rose to her feet. “The prisoner is to be released. Immediately. Give him his purse and see him to the city gates. His services are no longer required.”

The Proctor, jolted into action, stammered, “But, Your Grace… the Lord Confessor…”

The Duchess glanced down at the pathetic, green-tinged man spasming on the cobblestones. “Have the physicians attend to him,” she said with a wave of her hand. “Perhaps they can find a large lily pad for him to rest on. It seems his inner nature has finally been made manifest.” Her eyes met Gideon’s one last time, and in them he saw not a hint of belief in his magic, but a profound respect for his artistry. She knew it was a trick. And she did not care. The show was all that mattered.

Two guards, their faces a mixture of fear and awe, approached Gideon. One untied his hands. The other pressed a small, heavy purse into his palm. Without a word, they escorted him from the courtyard, past the gawking nobles and through the terrified ranks of the city watch. No one met his eye.

As he walked through the great iron-strapped gates of the city, a free man, the morning sun was warm on his face. He did not look back. He had walked into the city a scholar of inks and letters and walked out a legend, a sorcerer of terrifying power. He fingered the purse in his pocket. The Lord Confessor would likely recover in a day or two, with a splitting headache and a case of profound humiliation. The city, however, would talk about the day the warlock Gideon Fell turned the Lord Confessor into a toad for a hundred years. Justice, he mused, was a matter of perspective. But a good story, he now knew, was a far more powerful thing than magic itself.

The road out of the city was dusty and rutted, a far less grand affair than the paved avenues within its walls. Gideon put a league between himself and the gate before he dared to slow his pace. He did not feel like a legend. He felt like a man who had just prodded a hornet’s nest with a very short stick and was now walking away, pretending not to hear the buzzing. The purse was a reassuring weight against his hip, a solid, tangible fact in a morning that had dissolved into pure theatre. He found a small copse of silver birch trees just off the road, their slender white trunks offering a meager illusion of privacy, and sat on a mossy stone.

With trembling fingers, he untied the leather drawstring. Gold. Not the silver he had expected, or the copper of a beggar’s dole. Ten heavy gold sovereigns, each bearing the profile of the Duchess’s late husband, a man with a perpetually sour expression. It was a fortune. It was more than payment for services rendered; it was a silent acknowledgment of the performance, a patron’s gift to her favoured artist. He ran a thumb over the cold metal. The Duchess had not bought a sorcerer. She had bought a story, and she was a woman who clearly believed in paying for quality.

He had won. He was free, he was wealthy beyond his immediate needs, and he had humiliated a man who represented the very sort of pious, self-serving authority he had always despised. Yet the victory felt hollow, brittle. He had not vanquished the Lord Confessor with righteous power or superior intellect. He had done it with a carefully measured dose of Veridian Canker, a powdered mold that grew only on the undersides of stones in the Blackwater Fen. Mixed with a stabilizing agent derived from ground beetle shells, it was harmless. But when introduced to the specific acidic compound found in the bitter lemon water the Lord Confessor drank each morning for his humors, the reaction was spectacular.

It began with a tingling of the extremities, followed by a violent cramping of the facial muscles, pulling the skin taut and wide over the bones. The true artistry, however, lay in the secondary component. A fine, almost invisible dust of a peculiar lichen, which Gideon had paid a serving boy a whole silver piece to coat the inside of the Lord Confessor’s silver chalice with. This dust, when it met the sweat produced by the Canker’s initial shock to the system, created a vibrant, sickening green stain on the skin. It would be gone by morning, but for a few crucial minutes, it was horrifyingly effective. The final touch, the spasmodic twitching, was simply the Canker running its course through the man’s nervous system. Unpleasant, certainly, but no more lethal than a bee sting.

His incantation had been gibberish, a few lines of a pre-Imperial epic poem about the mating habits of sea slugs, delivered with as much gravitas as he could muster. The crowd, primed for magic, saw magic. The Duchess, primed for entertainment, saw a master at his craft. And Gideon, the scholar of obscure texts and chemical reactions, was now, to the world, a warlock. The title sat upon his shoulders like an ill fitting, and dangerously flammable, cloak.

He tucked the purse deep into his coat and started walking again, heading north, towards the coast. He had a vague notion of taking a ship, of leaving the duchy and its politics far behind. He could go to the Free Cities, resume his old life, perhaps open a small shop selling rare inks and pigments. The name Gideon Fell would mean nothing there. He could be anonymous again. The thought was as intoxicating as any wine.

By late afternoon, the sun beating down on him, he came upon a roadside inn called The Weary Pilgrim. It was a squat, timber framed building with a thatched roof that looked as though it were slowly melting. Smoke curled from its chimney in a lazy grey ribbon. It was exactly the sort of unremarkable, forgettable place he was looking for. He pushed open the heavy oak door and stepped inside.

The common room was dim and smelled of stale beer, woodsmoke, and damp wool. A few patrons, farmers by the look of their calloused hands and sun weathered faces, sat hunched over wooden mugs at a long table. They glanced up as he entered, their expressions dull and incurious. The innkeeper, a heavy set man with a beard like a spade, was wiping the bar with a stained cloth.

“A room for the night,” Gideon said, his voice a little hoarse. “And a meal.”

The innkeeper grunted, his eyes flicking over Gideon’s dusty but well made clothes. “That’ll be three coppers for the room. Supper’s stew. Another copper.”

Gideon nodded, relieved at the man’s complete lack of interest. He was just another traveler. He placed the coins on the bar, and the innkeeper gave him a key attached to a block of wood. As Gideon turned to head for the stairs, a voice piped up from the corner table.

“You come from the city, traveler?”

Gideon paused. It was one of the farmers, a younger man with an eager, sharp featured face.

“I passed through it,” Gideon said carefully.

“Then you must have heard! They say a warlock was brought before the Duchess this very morning. They say he struck down the Lord Confessor with a single curse!”

The common room, which had been filled with a low murmur, fell silent. All eyes were on Gideon. He felt a familiar cold dread prickle his skin. His new life, his legend, had outpaced him.

“News travels fast on these roads,” Gideon said, trying for a noncommittal tone.

“They say,” another farmer chimed in, his voice low with awe, “that the Lord Confessor turned green as pond scum and swelled up like a toad full of marsh gas. Right there in the courtyard.”

A woman, who Gideon presumed was the innkeeper’s wife, made a sign against evil. “The Almighty preserve us.”

The eager young farmer was on his feet now, his eyes shining. “And the Duchess, she just applauded! Applauded the demon’s work and set him free! Can you imagine the power of such a man?”

Gideon felt the weight of their stares. He was a man of medium height, with the ink stained fingers of a scribe and the slightly stooped shoulders of someone who spent too much time bent over books. He did not look like their image of a demon. He looked, he knew, profoundly ordinary. Which was, perhaps, even more terrifying to them. What if evil could look so unremarkable?

“I heard something of it,” Gideon said, his mouth dry. He turned to go.

“Wait,” the young farmer said, taking a step closer. “My cousin is in the city watch. He said the warlock was a scholar. A man of books. Looked a bit like you, he did. Tallish, with dark hair.”

Gideon’s heart hammered against his ribs. He forced himself to meet the man’s gaze. “Many men have dark hair,” he said quietly. “It was a strange tale, to be sure. Now, if you’ll excuse me, the road has been long.”

He did not wait for a reply, but walked up the creaking wooden stairs to his room. He locked the door behind him and leaned against it, his breath coming in short, sharp bursts. It was one thing to be a legend in a city a day’s walk away. It was another to have the legend sitting with you in a tavern, drinking ale and speculating about your appearance. His anonymity was already a ghost.

His room was small and spare, containing only a narrow bed with a straw mattress and a single stool. A grimy window looked out onto a pigsty. It was perfect. He lay on the bed, not bothering to remove his boots, and stared at the water stained ceiling. He had created a story so powerful that it had taken on a life of its own. It was a rampaging beast, and he was running just a few steps ahead of it, hoping not to be trampled. He had thought the story was his tool, but he was quickly becoming its servant, forced to play a role he had not auditioned for.

Supper was brought to his room by a young girl with wide, fearful eyes, who set the tray down just inside the door and scurried away as if the floorboards were hot. The stew was thin and greasy, but Gideon ate it mechanically. He needed his strength. He would leave before dawn, push on, and hope to find a place where the story had not yet arrived.

Sleep did not come easily. Every creak of the inn, every shout from the common room below, sent a jolt of anxiety through him. He imagined the farmers downstairs, their whispers growing louder, their suspicions solidifying into certainty. He saw them coming up the stairs with cudgels and torches, ready to mete out their own rough justice to a warlock.

It was near midnight when a soft, hesitant knock came at his door.

Gideon froze, his hand instinctively going to the small paring knife he kept for sharpening quills. It was a pathetic weapon.

“Who is it?” he called, his voice tight.

“It is I, sir. Elara.” The voice was soft, female. He did not recognize the name. “The innkeeper’s daughter. I… I beg a moment of your time.”

He hesitated. A trap? Unlikely. A woman’s voice was a poor lure for a cornered man. Cautiously, he went to the door and slid the bolt back.

The girl from earlier stood there, holding a flickering candle that cast long, dancing shadows down the hallway. She was young, perhaps seventeen, with a pale, earnest face and dark hair tied back in a simple braid. The fear he had seen in her eyes before was still there, but it was overshadowed by a desperate, pleading hope.

“What do you want?” Gideon asked, keeping his hand on the door.

She curtsied, a nervous, jerky motion. “Forgive me for disturbing your rest, Master Fell.”

She knew his name. The blood drained from his face. The young farmer must have convinced the others.

Seeing his expression, she rushed to speak. “No, please, don’t be alarmed. I mean you no harm. I overheard them in the common room. I know who you are. What you can do.” She took a deep breath, her knuckles white where she gripped the candlestick. “My brother, my little brother Tom, he is sick. He has the wasting fever. The physician has bled him twice and given him poultices of mustard and rue, but he only grows weaker. He coughs blood in the mornings.”

Tears welled in her eyes, shimmering in the candlelight. “They say you command life and death. That you can make a man’s very nature manifest upon his skin. Please, sir. I know your arts are not for common folk, but I have saved a little money. It is not much, but it is all I have. Please, can you help him?”

Gideon stared at her, a wave of profound weariness washing over him. Here it was. The inevitable consequence of his grand performance. He had not smote a villain with righteous fury; he had played a clever trick on a pompous fool. But in the telling, the story had been polished and sharpened into a weapon of miraculous power. And now this desperate girl wanted him to wield it to save her brother.

He felt a pang of something he had not felt all day: guilt. His spectacle, his bid for freedom, had given this poor girl a sliver of false hope that was about to be cruelly extinguished.

“Child,” he said, his voice softer than he intended. “My… abilities… are not what you think. They are not for healing.”

“But they are powerful,” she insisted, her voice dropping to a desperate whisper. “You humbled the Lord Confessor. The most powerful man of the cloth in the duchy. Surely a simple fever is within your grasp. I am not asking for a miracle for free. I can pay.” She fumbled in her apron pocket and pulled out a few copper coins, holding them out on her trembling palm.

Gideon looked at the pitiful offering, and then back at her tear streaked face. He could tell her the truth. He could explain the precise chemical interactions of fen mold and citrus acid. He could describe the way he had manipulated a disgruntled acolyte. He could shatter her hope with the mundane, disappointing facts of his deception. And what would that achieve? It would crush her, and it would put his own life in jeopardy. A confessed charlatan was far more likely to be lynched than a feared warlock.

He had to play the part. It was his only protection.

“The forces I deal with are volatile,” he said, choosing his words as if they were stones to build a wall. “They are not instruments of mending. To turn them upon a sickness… the results could be catastrophic.” It sounded suitably arcane and self important.

Her face fell. The light in her eyes flickered and died. “Oh,” she whispered, her hand dropping to her side. The coins clattered softly on the wooden floor. “I… I see. I am sorry to have disturbed you, master.”

She turned to leave, her shoulders slumped in defeat. Gideon felt like the lowest kind of monster. He had sold this girl a grand illusion, and when she came to him with real, heartbreaking need, all he could offer her was more empty words.

As she took her first step away, a new sound reached them from outside. The thudding of horses’ hooves, many of them, moving at a fast, purposeful pace. They were not the slow plodding of a farmer’s cart. They were the urgent, rhythmic beats of trained riders. They stopped abruptly in the inn’s courtyard below.

Elara looked back at Gideon, her eyes wide with a new kind of fear. “Who is that at this hour?”

A moment later, the inn’s main door slammed open downstairs. A man’s voice, sharp and imperious, cut through the night. “The innkeeper! Show yourself! We are looking for a traveler. A scholar, by the name of Gideon Fell.”

Gideon’s blood ran cold. It could be the Proctor, his brief courage having failed, now sending men to rectify his mistake. It could be the Lord Confessor’s own men, sent to exact a far more permanent and painful revenge. He backed into his room, his mind racing. The window was too high, the pigsty below too uninviting. There was nowhere to run.

Heavy, booted footsteps pounded up the stairs. Elara flattened herself against the wall, her face ashen. Two men appeared at the top of the landing. They were not city watch, nor were they the Confessor’s black robed acolytes. They wore fine leather jerkins over dark green tunics, and the crest embroidered on their chests was unmistakable: the silver falcon of the Duchess.

The leader, a man with a scarred face and cold, intelligent eyes, scanned the hallway. His gaze fell on Gideon, standing framed in the doorway of his room. The man’s expression was unreadable. He gave a slight, almost imperceptible nod.

“Gideon Fell,” he said. It was not a question.

Gideon said nothing, his throat too tight to form words.

The man took a step forward, his hand resting on the pommel of his sword. He paid no mind to the terrified girl beside him. “Her Grace, the Duchess, sends her compliments on your morning’s performance.” He reached inside his jerkin and produced a sealed letter, the silver falcon impressed into the dark green wax. “And she has an encore in mind.”

Gideon’s mind, a moment before a frantic whirlwind of panicked escape routes, became a sudden, deathly still void. The silver falcon on the wax seal seemed to stare at him, a predator’s eye promising a swift and merciless end. An encore. The word was a mockery, a silken cord tightening around his neck. His morning’s performance had been a desperate gambit for a handful of coins and a hot meal. It had been cheap theater, smoke and mirrors built on a foundation of lies. Now, the most powerful woman in the province had bought a ticket and was demanding a private show, one where the consequences of failure would be infinitely more severe than a few thrown vegetables.

The scarred man, whose name Gideon did not know and did not wish to, held the letter out. His posture was one of patient impatience. He was a weapon waiting to be aimed, and his target was Gideon.

Gideon’s hand felt impossibly heavy as he reached out and took the letter. The wax was cool and smooth beneath his thumb. He did not break the seal. There was no need. The message was already perfectly clear. This was not an invitation. It was a summons.

He looked past the man’s shoulder at Elara. She was a ghost against the hallway wall, her earlier heartbreak now dwarfed by this new, more immediate terror. Her fear was for him, he realized with a jolt of sour shame. She still believed in the warlock. She saw these dangerous men and assumed they had come for him because of his power, not because of his fraud.

“You will come with us,” the scarred man said, his voice a low rumble of gravel. He gestured with his chin down the stairs. “Now.”

Gideon swallowed, the sound loud in his own ears. He had to say something, to maintain the fiction. A true warlock would not be so easily herded. A true warlock would have an aura of menace, of untouchable power. All Gideon had was a pounding heart and the sweat trickling down his back.

“Her Grace is not one for waiting,” he managed, forcing a measure of coldness into his tone. He let his gaze drift over the man as if assessing a mildly interesting insect. “I am aware.”

It was a pathetic attempt, but the soldier’s expression did not change. He was not here to be impressed. He was here to follow orders.

Gideon turned his head slightly, his eyes finding Elara again. The small pouch of coins he had earned that morning was still on his belt. It felt like a lead weight, a physical manifestation of his guilt. He had taken this money and offered her nothing. Now he was being taken away, perhaps to his death, by the very power he had pretended to wield. It was a grimly perfect irony.

He untied the pouch, the leather soft and worn. He took a step towards her. The second guard, who had been silent until now, shifted his weight, his hand moving to his sword. The scarred leader held up a hand, a minute gesture that stopped his subordinate instantly.

Gideon held the pouch out to Elara. “For your brother,” he said, his voice softer than he intended, the warlock’s facade cracking under the strain. “Find a proper physician. A real one. From the city. This will be enough.”

Her eyes, wide and luminous with tears, darted from the pouch to his face. She did not understand. She saw a powerful sorcerer, hounded by the Duchess’s guard, taking a moment to show her a kindness he had previously denied. The truth, that this was the desperate act of a guilty fraud, was blessedly hidden from her.

She shook her head. “No, master. I cannot. You…”

“Take it,” Gideon insisted, his voice regaining a sliver of its false authority. “Consider it a… down payment. For a task I may yet undertake. Now go. This is not a place for you.”

He pressed the coins into her hand and closed her trembling fingers around the pouch. He did not wait for a reply. He turned his back on her, on the last person in this miserable town who saw him as something more than he was, and faced the Duchess’s men. He squared his shoulders, lifted his chin, and descended the stairs as if he were walking to a throne and not, in all likelihood, a scaffold.

The ride was a blur of cold night air and rhythmic dread. Gideon was placed on a fine, spirited mare that seemed to sense his fear and danced nervously beneath him. The scarred man, who Gideon learned was named Torvin, rode to his left, while the other guard, a younger, harder man named Luc, rode to his right. Two more guards rode behind, their presence a constant pressure. They moved through the sleeping town like a phantom troop, their hoofbeats muffled by the dirt roads, the only sound the creak of leather and the jingle of harness.

Once they were clear of the town and onto the king’s road, the pace increased. The moon was a sliver of bone in a sky of black velvet, offering little light. The world was reduced to shadows and silhouettes, the dark mass of the forest on one side, the faint gleam of the river on the other.

Gideon tried to gather his thoughts, to build the walls of his deception higher and stronger than ever before. He had survived the Proctor. He had survived the Lord Confessor’s men. This was just another, more elaborate, stage. But the audience was far more discerning.

“Does Her Grace often send armed escorts for her evening entertainment?” Gideon asked, pitching his voice to carry over the wind and hooves.

Torvin turned his head. The moonlight caught the jagged path of the scar that cut from his temple to his jaw. “Her Grace is interested in results, not entertainment.”

“And what result does she expect from me?”

Torvin was silent for a long moment. “The kind that cannot be bought from physicians or prayed for from priests.”

The words confirmed Gideon’s deepest fear. This was not a request for some arcane consultation, some scholarly translation of a forgotten text. This was a demand for a miracle. A sickness. A curse. Just as he had feared, just as Elara had begged for. The universe had a cruel sense of humor.

“The forces I command are not trivial things,” Gideon said, reciting the lines he had used on Elara, testing them on this new, harder audience. “They are not to be summoned lightly. The cost can be great.”

“Her Grace is prepared to pay any cost,” Torvin said flatly, and the simple finality of the statement chilled Gideon more than the night air.

They rode on in silence. Gideon’s mind raced, sifting through the fragments of lore he had accumulated over years of study. Not the real, dangerous magic he had once sought and failed to find, but the stories about it. He knew the rituals, the gestures, the arcane vocabulary. He knew the superstitions and beliefs of the nobles, their terror of curses, their faith in bloodlines and omens. He had to use that. He had to build a performance so convincing, so layered with the authentic details of the charlatan’s art, that it would appear real even to the desperate and the powerful. His life depended on his ability to seem, in every way, like the one thing he was not.

After what felt like an eternity, the road began to climb. The trees thinned, replaced by high, manicured hedges. Through a set of magnificent iron gates wrought in the shape of swooping falcons, they entered the ducal domain. The castle was not the grim, martial fortress Gideon had expected. It was a sprawling residence of pale stone and dark slate, its towers and turrets reaching for the stars like graceful fingers. Lights glowed in a hundred windows, a beacon of sleepless anxiety in the vast, dark grounds.

They dismounted in a silent, torchlit courtyard where grooms appeared from the shadows to take their horses. The air here was different, cleaner, scented with cut grass and night blooming flowers instead of the town’s miasma of woodsmoke and refuse. But the silence was heavy, oppressive. It was the silence of a house holding its breath.

Torvin led him inside, not through the grand main entrance but a smaller, private door. They walked through corridors hung with rich tapestries depicting ancient hunts and forgotten battles. The floors were polished marble, their footsteps echoing in the cavernous spaces. Servants flattened themselves against the walls as they passed, their faces pale and their eyes downcast. There was no doubt that the entire household was caught in the grip of whatever crisis had prompted Gideon’s summons.

Finally, they stopped before a pair of tall, carved oak doors. Torvin knocked once, a sharp, precise rap. A voice from within, clear and female, bade them enter.

Torvin opened one of the doors and stood aside, gesturing for Gideon to proceed. “Her Grace, the Duchess Elena,” he murmured, his voice for the first time holding a note of something other than stern command. It was reverence.

Gideon stepped into the room. It was a solar, a private study, warmly lit by a large fire and a dozen beeswax candles. Books lined the walls from floor to ceiling, not for show, but the well worn tools of a keen mind. A massive desk of dark wood was covered in maps and documents. And standing by the tall, leaded glass window, staring out into the night, was the Duchess.

She turned as he entered, and Gideon felt his carefully constructed composure begin to fail. He had expected an older woman, perhaps, hardened by politics or softened by grief. The Duchess Elena was young, scarcely older than himself, with a cascade of dark hair pinned back with silver clasps and eyes the color of a stormy sea. She was beautiful, but it was a severe, intelligent beauty, and the expression on her face was one of immense strain, held in check only by a will of iron. She wore a simple gown of dark green velvet, the color of her house, unadorned with jewelry save for a single silver ring on her finger, carved into the shape of a falcon.

“Gideon Fell,” she said. Her voice was the same one that had called them in, a calm, musical alto that held no hint of warmth. She surveyed him from head to toe, her gaze sharp and analytical. “You are less… imposing than your reputation in the town square this morning might suggest.”

Gideon bowed his head slightly, a gesture he hoped looked more like arcane respect than abject terror. “Appearances, Your Grace, are often the cheapest of illusions.”

A faint smile touched her lips, but it did not reach her eyes. “Indeed. I have had my fill of illusions. My court is filled with them. The illusion of loyalty. The illusion of competence from my physicians. The illusion of solace from my priests. I have come to you because I am told you deal in something more substantial.”

“What I deal with is dangerous, Your Grace,” Gideon said, falling back on his practiced script. “It is not to be trifled with.”

“My son is dying,” she said, and the words cut through all pretense, all the games of power and perception. They were a raw, brutal truth. The iron control in her voice wavered for a fraction of a second, a hairline crack in a marble statue. “The physicians say it is a wasting fever. They bleed him and give him foul tasting tinctures and he only grows weaker. The priests pray over him, chanting their litanies to the gods, and his light continues to fade. This is no fever. I know it. It is a curse.”

She walked from the window to her desk, her movements fluid and purposeful. She picked up a small, exquisitely carved wooden bird. “A gift,” she said, her voice laced with venom. “From my dear cousin, the Baron Von Hess, on the occasion of my son’s tenth birthday last month. Two days later, the sickness began.”

Gideon’s mind seized on the detail. A gift. A potential vector. A starting point for his investigation, if he could even call it that.

“You believe the Baron has cursed your son,” Gideon stated, keeping his tone neutral, observational.

“I believe the Baron would see his own bloodline rule this duchy,” she corrected him, her eyes flashing. “He has always coveted what is not his. He dabbles in the old ways. He keeps strange company. It is no great leap of logic.” She placed the bird back on the desk with a sharp click. “I did not summon you here for a lesson in politics, Master Fell. I summoned you to undo what has been done. Name your price. Gold, land, titles. They are all yours. Save my son.”

This was it. The moment of truth. The precipice. He could confess now, tell her he was a fraud, a simple scholar with a talent for theatrics, and throw himself on her mercy. The thought was tempting. It would be a relief, a release of the crushing pressure. And it would almost certainly get him executed on the spot for wasting her time, for giving her a moment of false hope only to snatch it away.

No. The only path was forward. The only way to live was to lie.

Gideon drew himself up, letting a slow, deliberate silence hang in the air. He made himself look into her desperate, intelligent eyes. “The price is not something that can be measured in gold or land, Your Grace. The price is paid in risk. To untangle a curse is to touch the threads of its weaving. The weaver may notice. To turn back such a power… it could turn on the one who attempts it. Or it could rebound, strengthened, upon the victim.”

He was speaking nonsense, but it was educated nonsense, woven from the tales and legends he knew so well. It sounded authentic. He saw a flicker of something in her eyes. Not belief, perhaps, but a willingness to believe. She was out of other options.

“What must you do?” she asked.

“I must see the boy,” Gideon said. “I must see the space where he sleeps. I must feel the echo of the malice that has been directed at him. Only then can I know the nature of the curse. And only then can I know if it can be undone.”

The Duchess stared at him for a long, heart-stopping moment. He felt like a mouse being examined by the falcon on her ring. She was weighing him, judging him, and his entire future rested on her conclusion.

Finally, she gave a single, sharp nod. “Very well. Torvin will take you.” She turned her back on him then, facing the window once more, a dismissal as clear as any words. The audience was over. The performance, however, was just beginning.

Gideon followed Torvin out of the solar, his robes whispering against the marble floor. They walked down another corridor, this one quieter, more personal. The air grew heavier, thick with the cloying scent of medicinal herbs and something else, the faint, sweetish smell of sickness.

They stopped at a door identical to the others, but Gideon could feel the misery emanating from it. Torvin pushed it open gently and stepped aside. Gideon entered the sickroom.

It was a large, airy chamber, but the heavy velvet curtains were drawn tight, plunging it into a perpetual twilight lit only by a few flickering candles. A fire burned low in the hearth, making the room uncomfortably warm. A severe looking woman in a nurse’s gray dress sat in a chair by the bed, her hands folded in her lap. She looked up, her expression tired and hopeless.

But Gideon’s attention was fixed on the bed. It was a massive, four poster bed, draped in green and silver silks, fit for a prince. And in the center of it, lost in the expanse of pillows and blankets, was a small, still form.

The Duchess’s son. Julian.

Gideon approached the bed slowly, his every movement calculated to appear solemn and mystical. He could feel Torvin’s eyes on his back, the nurse’s weary gaze on his face. He looked down at the boy.

The Duchess was right. This was no simple fever. The boy’s skin was pale, almost translucent, stretched tight over his small bones. His hair, a dark echo of his mother’s, was damp with sweat and plastered to his forehead. His breathing was shallow, a faint flutter in his chest. But it was his eyes that were the most disturbing. They were open, but they were not focused. They stared up at the canopy of the bed, glassy and vacant, seeing nothing. He was fading, a candle flame guttering in a draft.

Gideon extended a hand, letting it hover over the boy’s forehead. He was not trying to sense a magical aura; he was feeling for heat. The boy’s skin was cool, clammy. Not the burning heat of a fever. The physicians were wrong.

He let his eyes drift around the room, taking in every detail. The table beside the bed held a clutter of vials and cups. A silver basin. A Bible lay open on a small stand. His gaze fell upon a small vase on the windowsill, almost hidden by the heavy curtain. In it was a single, beautiful flower. It had a delicate, star shaped bloom of the purest white, and its leaves were a deep, waxy green. Gideon did not recognize the species. It was exotic, rare. And out of place.

He walked over to it, feigning a sudden interest, as if drawn by some unseen force. He leaned closer, pretending to study its arcane properties. He was, in fact, trying to identify it, dredging his memory of the botanical texts he had studied. It was called something… Moonpetal? No. Ghost Orchid? No.

He inhaled deeply, catching its scent. It was faint, but sweet, with an undercurrent of something else. Something… metallic. Like bitter almonds.

And then, his blood ran cold for the second time that night, but this was a different kind of cold. It was not the cold of fear, but the chilling certainty of discovery. He knew the flower. He had seen it in an old, forbidden herbalist’s tome. *Olenna’s Kiss*. Beautiful, rare, and lethally poisonous. Not instantly fatal, but a slow, creeping poison, ingested in small doses, perhaps as a powder sprinkled on food or steeped in a drink. It produced a wasting sickness. It mimicked a fever but without the heat. It clouded the mind, stole the strength, and slowly, inexorably, extinguished the life of its victim.

Gideon looked from the flower to the fading boy in the bed. This was not a curse woven from malice and moonlight. This was a murder, cold and calculated, being committed with a flower. The Baron’s gift had not been a cursed totem. It had been a delivery system.

He had his answer. He had a way out. He could save the boy. But he could not simply point to the flower and declare it to be poison. He was not a physician. He was a warlock. His solution had to be one of magic. His cure had to be a ritual. The lie had to be maintained, now more than ever. For if he succeeded, his reputation would be sealed. He would be the Duchess’s sorcerer. A fraud, trapped in a cage of his own making, but a living one.

He turned from the window, his expression a mask of grim gravity. He looked at Torvin, at the nurse, and let the silence stretch until it was almost unbearable.

“The malice is strong in this room,” he said, his voice a low, resonant whisper. “It is a clinging thing. A shadow with a sweet scent.” He looked directly at Torvin. “The curse is not in the boy. It is tethered to an object. An anchor. It feeds on his life, drawing it into itself. To save him, I must first break the anchor.”

Torvin’s scarred face was unreadable, but his eyes were fixed on Gideon, waiting.

Gideon raised a single, dramatic finger and pointed to the small vase on the windowsill.

“It is there.”
