The dust motes danced in the solitary shaft of light that pierced the gloom of Professor Volkov’s study, illuminating the intricate lines of an ancient celestial globe, its brass meridian tarnished by decades of contemplation. I had sought him out, as many had before me, drawn by the rumour of his singular wisdom, his profound, if often obscure, pronouncements on the intractable difficulties of existence. He sat hunched over a vast, unfurled parchment, his spectacles perched precariously on the end of a nose that seemed chiselled from the very granite of his unwavering will, a fine pen held motionless between his gnarled fingers.

“Ah, young Aris,” he murmured, his voice a low rumble, as if emerging from the subterranean chambers of time itself, “You have come, no doubt, seeking an answer to that question which vexes all of us, the fundamental riddle of our being.” He did not raise his head, but his eyes, sharp and piercing even in their aged setting, lifted slowly from the map to rest upon my face, an expectant, almost melancholic, gaze.

"Indeed, Professor," I managed, feeling the familiar weight of my own uncertainties press down upon me, "I find myself adrift, charting a course without a fixed star, and I recalled your words, your unique perspective on the currents that carry us."

He sighed, a long, drawn-out sound like the wind across a desolate plain. “Currents, yes. And eddies, and undertows, and the sudden squalls that rip the sails from the mast. I know them well, my boy, I know them all. There was a time, long past now, when I believed I had found the very heart of the ocean, the one unchanging point from which all else might be measured.” He paused, his gaze drifting to the globe, then to an old, leather-bound atlas on a nearby stand. “It was not a thing of flesh and blood, Aris, not a woman nor a child, nor the fickle favour of men. No, it was a land, a continent, a chimera born of ancient texts and fleeting observations, a place rumoured but never truly touched by the hand of man. He tapped the unfurled map gently with his pen. “I had, for years, dreamt of its jagged coastlines, its ice-capped peaks, its hidden rivers that flowed into forgotten seas. His voice, which had grown vibrant with the recounting, suddenly softened, dropping to a near whisper. “So halt’ ich’s endlich denn in meinen Handen, Und nenn’ es in gewissem Sinne mein.” He pronounced the German with a careful, almost tender articulation, lingering on the final word, his eyes now closed, lost in the echo of that distant triumph. He lit a small, ancient oil lamp that stood beside him, its flame flaring suddenly, chasing the shadows from the corner of the room. “To tell you the truth, Professor,” I said, with an effort that surprised me, a surge of my own anxieties bubbling to the surface, “I came here to understand the maps you’ve drawn, not of continents, but of souls.”

Here you had him as he was; he knew how to be so generously encouraging as to make a scrupulous man hesitate on the brink of confidence; but if I did hesitate, it was not for long.
