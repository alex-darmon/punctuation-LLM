The sun was beatin' down like a blacksmith's hammer on the little town of Miller's Bend, and the dust was so thick from all the wagons that you couldn't hardly see across the main street. It was fair day, and folks was swarmin' in from all the outlying farms and shacks, some of 'em ridin' on mules so old they looked like they'd seen the great flood, and some just a-trudgin' along, kickin' up more dust with every step they took. There was a feller with a trained bear that danced a jig when he played his fiddle, and another one sellin' shiny trinkets that you could swear was solid gold but was probably just brass all shined up. I was sittin' on an old apple crate by the General Store, watchin' it all, when a big red wagon with painted signs all over it come rattlin' down the street, pulled by two bony horses that looked like they'd walked all the way from Kansas.

The wagon was covered in fancy letters, tellin' everybody about “Doc Grub’s Miraculous Elixir, Cure-All for Every Ailment Known to Man!” and on top of it was a skinny man in a high hat and a striped suit, yellin' and waving his arms like a scarecrow in a hurricane. He jumped off his wagon right in the middle of the street, and a little feller with a drum started beatin' out a noisy tune, and pretty soon a crowd was gatherin' around, all gawkin' at the Doc.

“Folks!” he sung out, his voice like a rusty hinge, “I see before me a population riddled with aches and pains, a-sufferin' from the ague and the consumption, and no doubt troubled by the melancholia of the spirit!” He waved a bottle of dark liquid over his head. “But fear not, for Doc Grub has journeyed far and wide, from the peaks of the Rockies to the swamps of Louisiana, to bring you the one true salvation, the potion that will turn your sorrows into smiles, your coughs into chuckles, and your tired old bones into the spryest jig-dancin' limbs in the whole territory!”

The crowd looked mighty impressed, and some folks was already feelin' in their pockets for coin. But then old Man Hemlock, who was standin' right at the front with his arms crossed and a face like a dried-up prune, he spits a stream of tobacco juice on the ground and says, “What kind o’ salvation is that, Doc, if it smells like rye whiskey and pond water mixed together?”

Doc Grub, he stopped his speech right quick and looked at Old Man Hemlock like he was a pesky fly. “My good sir,” he says, smooth as a river stone, “that, my good sir, is the aroma of pure, unadulterated goodness, distilled from the finest herbs and spirits known to man. It is a flavor that speaks of health, of vigor, of eternal youth!”

Old Man Hemlock just snorted. “Eternal youth, you say? Why, my grandma’s old cough syrup tasted better, and it only ever cured a sore throat. Yours, I reckon, will just make a fella forget he’s sick for a spell, till the morning sun starts beatin' on his head and he remembers what a fool he was.”

A few folks in the crowd snickered, and Doc Grub’s face got a little redder under his big hat. “Sir, you impugn my good name and my honest trade!” he cried, puffin' out his chest. “I have testimonials, sir, from governors and generals, from ladies of high society and sturdy pioneers, all a-singin’ the praises of Doc Grub’s Miraculous Elixir!” He pulled out a roll of papers tied with a ribbon, but Old Man Hemlock wasn't buyin' it.

“Testimonials ain’t worth the paper they’re writ on if the stuff’s no good, Doc,” Hemlock says, and he reached out his hand slow and steady and took the bottle right out of Doc Grub’s grasp. He uncorked it with his thumb, sniffed it real hard, and then, quick as a snake, he took a big swallow. The crowd was silent, all eyes on him, waitin' to see what would happen.

Old Man Hemlock’s eyes watered, and he coughed, a real deep, rumblin' cough that sounded like a wagon axle breakin'. He spat out a mouthful, then wiped his beard with the back of his hand. “Rye whiskey and pond water, just like I thought,” he says, his voice raspy. “But with a generous dash of turpentine, if I ain’t mistaken. That’s for cleanin’ paint brushes, and makin’ a man sick enough to wish he was dead.”

The crowd started to buzz, a low angry hum. Doc Grub looked around, his smile gone, and he started edgin' back towards his wagon, mumblin' somethin' about ungrateful ruffians. But the buzz got louder, and a big fella with a red beard stepped forward and says, “So you was tryin’ to poison us, was you, Doc? Doc Grub tried to climb onto his wagon, but a couple of fellas grabbed his leg and pulled him back down. The crowd surged forward, and I seen a couple of men grab Doc Grub’s fancy hat and throw it in the dust. He squawked and tried to fight ‘em off, but there was too many. They started shakin' him, and somebody shouted, “Let’s give him a taste of his own medicine!”

Sometimes a crowd like that, when it gets stirred up, ain’t got no sense at all; and I figured it was best to be somewhere else when the sense got kicked out of 'em good and proper. The water was a muddy brown, not clear like the mountain streams I'd seen in other parts, but it had a power to it. I walked along the bank, kickin' at pebbles, thinkin' about Doc Grub and his turpentine tonic. Always amazes me how easily folks get taken in. They want to believe in magic, I reckon, somethin' to make their troubles vanish with a swallow and a cough. And when they find out it's just snake oil, well, they get madder than a wet hen. Righteous indignation, they call it, but it mostly looks like plain old fury to me.

I found a smooth rock by the water's edge and sat myself down. The sun was startin' to dip low, paintin' the sky in purples and oranges. A man needs his calm at the end of the day, especially after witnessin' the folly of others. As I struck a match against the rock, the scent of the pipe smoke mingling with the river air, I saw somethin' bobbin' in the water about twenty feet out. It wasn't a log, not exactly. I ain't one to go pokin' my nose into things that ain't mine, but somethin' about it tugged at my curiosity. It looked heavy, for one thing, pullin' that branch down.

I watched it for a spell, the pipe clenched in my teeth, my gaze narrowin'. Then, a patch of light-colored fabric appeared for a moment, then disappeared again under the water. It looked a sight like a sleeve. And then, a hand. A pale, still hand, briefly visible before the current sucked it back under. My pipe nearly dropped from my mouth. That weren't no drowned sack. That was a person.

Now, I ain't no hero. Never claimed to be. But I ain't one to stand by and watch a soul get carried off by the river neither. My heart started thumpin' a little harder against my ribs. I stood up, knocked the dottle out of my pipe, and started to walk along the bank, keepin' my eyes on the spot where I'd seen the hand. The current was slow, but steady, and that body was driftin' downstream, tanglin' and untanglin' with the branch.

The bank was muddy and slick in places, and I had to watch my footing. The sun was sinkin' faster now, turnin' the river into a streak of fire and shadow. I could hear the faint cries of a night hawk somewhere up above, and the buzz of mosquitoes started to pick up. I sped up my pace, breakin' into a jog. I needed to get closer, find a spot where the bank was less steep. I wasn't lookin' forward to a swim in that murky water, but it looked like I might not have much choice.

A little further down, the bank sloped gentler, and a patch of sandy ground offered a better foothold. The body, still entangled with the branch, had drifted closer to this spot. I could see more of it now. It was a man, dressed in dark, worn clothes. His head was mostly submerged, but I could make out the dark hair matted against his skull. He didn't look like a local, not with those city-cut britches and fine, albeit water-logged, coat.

I shucked off my boots and my outer jacket, tossin' them onto the sand. The water felt cold when I waded in, grabbin' at my ankles. It was murkier than it looked from the bank, and my feet sank into the soft riverbed. I pushed forward, the water risin' to my waist, then my chest. The current was stronger than it appeared, tuggin' at my legs, tryin' to pull me under. I fought against it, usin' my arms to paddle and push myself towards the branch.

Reachin' the man, I grabbed for his arm. He was heavier than he looked, completely limp. It took a good bit of grunt and pullin' to get him free from the snagged branch. His skin was cold to the touch, and I felt a prickle of fear. Was he alive? I didn't know. But I had to try. I wrapped one arm around his chest, tryin' to keep his head above water, and began the slow, strenuous haul back to the bank.

Each step was a struggle. The muddy bottom wanted to swallow my feet, and the current kept tryin' to spin me around. My lungs burned, and my muscles ached with the effort. I thought of Doc Grub gettin' his comeuppance, and a bitter laugh bubbled up in my throat. From one mess right into another, that's my luck. But I kept pullin', kept pushin', until finally, my feet found firm sand. I dragged him out of the water, a dead weight, and collapsed beside him on the bank, breathin' hard and sputterin' water.

He was a young man, barely out of his teens, by the look of him. His face was pale, his lips blue. I turned him onto his side, and a gush of water coughed out of his mouth. Then, another gush, and a terrible, gurgling cough that racked his whole body. He was alive. A shaky breath escaped his lips, then another, ragged and weak. I rolled him gently onto his back and started patting his chest, talkin' to him in a low, steady voice. "That's it, son. Breathe. Come on, breathe for me."

He coughed again, a wet, rattling sound, and his eyes fluttered open. They were a startling blue, wide and confused, starin' up at the twilight sky. He looked around, then at me, then back at the water, his brow furrowed. "Wha... what happened?" he rasped, his voice barely a whisper.

"You took a swim, son," I said, feelin' a strange relief wash over me. "A rather deep one. You're lucky I was passin' by."

He tried to sit up, but his limbs were weak, and he collapsed back onto the sand. He looked thoroughly miserable, shiverin' despite the warm evening air. "The satchel," he choked out. "My satchel. Is it... is it here?" His eyes darted frantically around, searchin' the bank.

I glanced around. "Didn't see no satchel," I told him, shruggin'. "Just you. You were tangled up with a branch."

His blue eyes filled with a desperate light. "It must have gone in the river. It must have. Oh, no. That's everything. Everything." He tried to push himself up again, this time with more determination, but he was still too weak. He fell back, a frustrated groan escaping him.

"Hold on there, steady," I said, puttin' a hand on his shoulder. "You ain't goin' anywhere right now. You just swallowed half the river. Tell me what was in this satchel that's got you so riled up."

He lay there, chest heaving, his gaze fixed on the dark water. "It was... my father's ledger. And maps. Maps to the... to the claims." His voice was barely audible. "He died followin' them. I was supposed to continue. To find it. And now... now it's gone." A single tear traced a path down his muddy cheek.

My eyes narrowed. "Claims, you say? What kind of claims?" I'd heard stories of gold and silver in these parts, but mostly they were just that, stories. Wild goose chases for fools.

"Copper. Silver. A vein of gold, my father believed," he said, his voice stronger now, a spark of hope mingled with despair. "He spent his life lookin' for it. Said it was hidden deep, not in the usual places. Needed these maps, these calculations from his ledger to find it."

I looked at him, truly looked at him. He was young, probably no more than twenty. Clothes were fine once, but now torn and mud-stained. He had the look of a scholar or an educated man, not a prospector. His hands were soft. "So, you're a city boy, then. Never held a pickaxe in your life, I reckon."

He flushed, but didn't deny it. "I studied geology. My father wanted me to use my head, not just my back. He did all the groundwork, the surveying. I was meant to interpret his notes, to pinpoint the exact location." He winced, a fresh cough tearin' through him. "But I slipped. On the bank. My horse got spooked by a snake, I think. He shook his head. "Ran off, I suppose. I don't remember much after hittin' the water. Just cold... and dark."

"Well," I said, gettin' to my feet. My clothes felt clammy and heavy. "No use cryin' over spilled milk or drowned satchels. You need to get warm. And dry. And get some food in you. Ain't gonna find no claims lookin' like a half-drowned rat."

He tried to make small talk, askin' about the area, but his voice was weak, and I mostly just grunted in response. My mind was busy wonderin' what kind of trouble I'd just walked into. Claims of gold and silver were a dime a dozen, and usually led to naught but empty pockets and broken dreams. But the look in his eyes, that desperation, it seemed real enough.

A flat patch of ground, sheltered by a thick wall of willows, with the river bendin' gently around it. I dropped my pack and started gatherin' dry branches and twigs. Thomas, bless his heart, tried to help, but he was clumsy, and his hands were too cold to be much use. "Just sit down, son," I told him, "you're gonna fall over and crack your head open. He wasn't tryin' to sell me anything; he was just layin' bare his soul.

He lay down by the fire, wrapped in his damp, heavy coat. Soon, his breathing evened out, and he was fast asleep, probably exhausted from the ordeal. I stayed awake for a while, tendin' the fire, my mind churnin'. What if his father's claims were real? What if this Thomas Abernathy wasn't just another dreamer? Gold and silver. Those were powerful words, able to twist a man's heart and turn his head. I'd seen it happen. But I'd also seen men find it, against all odds.

We set off after a quick breakfast of hardtack and coffee. The river stretched before us, glintin' in the mornin' sun. The banks were mostly overgrown with willows and reeds, makin' the walk slow and tedious. We had to push through thickets, step over fallen logs, and navigate muddy patches. Thomas, still not quite himself, stumbled frequently.

"You said your father was a surveyor," I said, tryin' to keep him distracted from his misery. "Did he ever tell you anything about where these claims were, specifically?"

"Only that they were in the northern foothills, near an old prospector's trail that led to a forgotten pass," Thomas replied, battin' away a fly. "He described a particular rock formation, a 'sleeping giant' made of red stone. I chewed on that. Red stone formations weren't uncommon in the foothills, and neither were springs. But a "sleeping giant" and a "weeping spring" were specific enough to maybe jog a memory, if a man had spent enough time in those mountains. I hadn't, not much, but I knew fellas who had.

We walked for hours, our eyes scourin' the water and the banks. We saw discarded cans, old boots, pieces of driftwood, but no sign of a leather satchel. Thomas grew quieter as the day wore on, his hope dimmin' with every mile. I could see the despair creepin' back into his eyes.

"It's no use, is it?" he said finally, sittin' down on a log, his head in his hands. "It's gone. Lost forever. All of his work. All of his sacrifice."

"It ain't over till it's over," I said, though I was startin' to feel the same way. The river was wide, and anything could have happened to that satchel. It could be buried under a sandbar, torn apart by the current, or carried a hundred miles downstream. "But we ain't gonna find it sittin' here feelin' sorry for ourselves. We can keep lookin' a while longer, or we can head back to town, and you can try to piece together what you remember of his notes."

He lifted his head, his face streaked with dirt and dejection. "I don't remember enough. Only fragments. The calculations were complex. And the maps were coded. He had his own symbols, his own way of marking things."

Just as he spoke, I heard a faint squawk from the other side of the river. A hawk, maybe. But then, it came again, louder, and distinctly not a bird. It was a man's voice, yellin'.

I held up a hand. "Hear that?"

Thomas listened, his eyes wide. "What is it?"

"Sounds like trouble," I said, already movin' towards a break in the trees that offered a clearer view of the opposite bank. "And trouble generally finds me, no matter how far I try to run from it."

Pushin' through the thicket, I peered across the river. The opposite bank was steeper here, a rocky bluff risin' above the water. And there, scramblin' frantically up the bluff, was a man. He was small and wiry, dressed in buckskins, and he was shoutin' and pointin' back down the river. His face was contorted in fear.

"What's he lookin' at?" Thomas whispered, comin' up beside me.

Then I saw it. Roundin' a bend in the river, comin' downstream fast, was a small, rickety raft. And on the raft, standin' tall and menacing, were two figures. They were big men, even from this distance, wearin' tattered coats and wide-brimmed hats. And one of them was holdin' a rifle.

"Looks like our troubles just got themselves a raft," I muttered. "And they're headin' our way."

The man on the bluff saw us, and he pointed, his mouth movin' in a silent scream, tryin' to warn us. But it was too late. The men on the raft had already spotted him, and then us. The one with the rifle raised it to his shoulder.

"Get down!" I yelled, grabbin' Thomas and pullin' him behind a thick willow trunk. A moment later, the crack of a rifle shot echoed across the water, followed by the whizz of a bullet tearin' through the leaves above our heads.

"They're shooting at us!" Thomas gasped, his face pale with terror.

"Well, they ain't shootin' at the squirrels, are they?" I said, peerin' cautiously around the trunk. The raft was closer now, movin' with the current, and the men were lookin' right at us. They weren't just passing by. They were lookin' for somethin', or someone.

The man on the bluff had vanished, probably found a crevice to hide in. Smart move. Now it was just us.

"Who are they?" Thomas asked, his voice tremblin'.

"Dunno," I said, "but they ain't friendly. And they're comin' for somethin'. Or someone." My gaze fell on Thomas, and a cold suspicion started to form. "You got any enemies, Thomas Abernathy? Any fellas who might want your father's ledger, or his claims, or maybe just to shut you up?"

He shook his head, his eyes wide. "No! I... I don't think so. My father kept his work private. Very private."

Another shot rang out, closer this time, splattering mud near our feet. The men on the raft were guidin' it towards our side of the river. They were gonna land here.

"We gotta move," I said, gettin' up and pullin' Thomas along. "And fast. Through the trees. Head away from the river."

We plunged into the dense thicket of willows and river brush, pushin' our way through the tangled branches. The leaves whipped at our faces, and the thorny bushes snagged our clothes. Thomas, despite his fear, kept up, driven by the immediate threat. I could hear the shouts of the men from the river, growin' closer. They were on the bank now, I reckoned, comin' after us.

"Faster!" I urged, pushin' through a particularly dense patch of brambles. My mind was racin'. These weren't just common bandits. They were organized, and they were huntin'. And if they were after Thomas, it meant his father's claims might be more than just a madman's dream. It meant there might really be something worth killin' for.
