Arthur, though stung by the public assertion of Ashworth's favored position, swallowed his pride. He turned to Lady Eleanor, offering his arm. "Lady Eleanor, if you would do me the honor."

"With pleasure, Mr. Penhaligon," she replied, her eyes still holding that curious, discerning gleam. As she took his arm, her fingers, though gloved, conveyed a surprising firmness, and for a fleeting moment, he felt a peculiar sense of alliance, as if she, too, was an observer of a rather predictable play.

The dining room was a cavernous space, dominated by a long mahogany table polished to a mirror sheen. Candles flickered in elaborate candelabras, casting a warm, dancing light over the ancestral portraits that lined the walls. The air was rich with the smell of roasting meats and delicate sauces. Lord Harrington took his place at the head of the table, with Clara to his right and Ashworth to her right. Lady Eleanor sat opposite Clara, and Arthur found himself seated next to Lady Eleanor, opposite Ashworth, a strategic positioning that allowed him to observe the entire dynamic.

The first courses were served: a delicate consommé, followed by poached salmon with asparagus. The conversation began, as it always did at formal dinners, with polite inquiries about travel and the current state of affairs in London. Lord Harrington, with an almost indecent eagerness, steered the conversation towards Ashworth's estates, his recent business ventures, and his political aspirations.

"Lord Ashworth, your insights into the new railway lines are always most enlightening," Lord Harrington declared, beaming. "Such foresight, such an understanding of the nation's burgeoning infrastructure. Truly commendable."

Ashworth accepted the praise with a modest nod, though his eyes subtly scanned Clara's face for her reaction. "One endeavors to remain abreast of progress, Lord Harrington. Opportunity, after all, does not wait for the hesitant." He then turned his attention to Clara, a charming smile playing on his lips. "Lady Clara, I recall your father mentioning your keen interest in the improvements to the local parish school. I have recently endowed a new wing to one of my own estate schools in Kent. Perhaps you would be interested in discussing the architectural plans? They incorporate some rather novel concepts for ventilation and lighting, I assure you."

Clara offered a gracious reply. "That is most kind of you, Lord Ashworth. I would indeed be interested. Education, particularly for the less fortunate, is a subject close to my heart." Her words were perfectly composed, but Arthur, watching her across the table, noticed a slight tension in her shoulders, a subtle rigidity that betrayed her discomfort. She avoided Ashworth's direct gaze, focusing instead on her plate.

Arthur felt a slow burn of indignation. Ashworth was not merely making polite conversation; he was subtly parading his wealth and influence, laying claim to common interests with Clara, attempting to establish a connection based on his own perceived generosity. He was a master of the subtle power play, and it was clear that Lord Harrington was an eager accomplice.

Lady Eleanor, however, seemed to find the display rather amusing. She caught Arthur’s eye and offered a faint, sardonic smile before turning to him. "Tell me, Mr. Penhaligon, is it not exhausting to be constantly surrounded by such earnest conversation regarding investments and estates? Sometimes I confess I long for a discussion of poetry, or perhaps the latest scientific discoveries. My brother, bless his ambitious heart, rarely indulges in such frivolous pursuits." Her tone was light, but there was an unmistakable undercurrent of irony.

Arthur found himself unexpectedly charmed by her candor. "Indeed, Lady Eleanor. While I respect the necessity of managing one's affairs, a broader scope of intellectual engagement can be most refreshing. I confess to a particular fondness for the Romantic poets myself."

"Ah, the Romantics," she mused, her eyes twinkling. "Full of passion and unbridled emotion. So unlike the meticulous calculations of land acquisition, would you not agree?" She glanced pointedly at her brother, who was now regaling Clara with a detailed account of his tenant farmers' productivity rates.

Arthur suppressed a smile. "A stark contrast, certainly. Though perhaps there is a certain romance in the careful cultivation of one's resources, if one is inclined to view it so." He was careful to avoid outright agreement with her implied criticism, maintaining his decorum.

The conversation between Arthur and Lady Eleanor provided a welcome, if brief, respite from the main current of the dinner party. He found her to be quick-witted and surprisingly perceptive, her intelligence a sharp contrast to her brother's more ponderous, self-satisfied pronouncements. She asked him about his work with Lord Harrington, his background, and his interests, listening with an attentiveness that felt genuine.

Meanwhile, Lord Harrington continued to steer the conversation back to Ashworth. "Mr. Penhaligon is a most diligent and capable man, Lord Ashworth," Lord Harrington interjected, seemingly catching a lull in the adjacent conversation. "A protégé of mine, as it were. He has a keen head for figures, though perhaps less of an eye for the larger sweep of things, the broader canvas of societal influence, if you understand my meaning." The compliment was backhanded, a clear attempt to place Arthur in his subordinate role, to diminish any perceived threat.

Arthur felt his jaw tighten, but he maintained a calm façade. Ashworth merely offered a polite, noncommittal murmur, clearly uninterested in Arthur's capabilities unless they directly served his own interests.

The main courses arrived: roasted pheasant with wild mushrooms, a saddle of lamb, and an array of perfectly cooked vegetables. Fine wines flowed freely, though Arthur drank sparingly, keenly aware that his wits must remain sharp. He observed Clara, watching the careful mask she wore. She responded to Ashworth's inquiries with polite, measured phrases, her smiles practiced, her gaze often drifting to the flickering candles or the ornate ceiling. There was a quiet defiance in her composure, a refusal to be overtly charmed or won over, that only someone who knew her as intimately as Arthur could discern.

Ashworth, oblivious to the subtle resistance, pressed on with his overtures. "Lady Clara, I am planning a summer sojourn to my country estate in Derbyshire. We have a rather excellent pack of hounds, and the riding trails are unparalleled. Perhaps you would do us the honor of joining us for a week or two? Eleanor, I am sure, would be delighted by your company." He gestured grandly to his sister.

Lady Eleanor, caught off guard, raised an eyebrow, a faint frown creasing her brow for a moment before she composed herself. "Indeed, Lady Clara. It would be most pleasant." Her enthusiasm, however, seemed somewhat forced.

Clara hesitated for a fraction of a second, her eyes flicking towards her father, who was practically beaming. "That is a most generous invitation, Lord Ashworth. I shall, of course, consult with my father regarding such an extensive visit." Her response was deferential, yet it skillfully avoided direct acceptance.

Lord Harrington, however, was quick to interject. "Nonsense, my dear! An excellent idea. A change of scenery would do you a world of good. And Lord Ashworth's estate, I can assure you, is one of the finest in the country. We shall discuss the arrangements, by all means." He spoke as if Clara's acceptance was a foregone conclusion, her personal wishes entirely secondary.

Arthur felt a cold dread settle in his stomach. A summer sojourn. Weeks under Ashworth's roof, with Lord Harrington's implicit blessing. It was a clear maneuver to accelerate the courtship, to isolate Clara and pressure her into an engagement. His hands, resting beneath the table, clenched into fists. He had sworn to fight for her, and this was precisely the kind of subtle yet relentless pressure he had anticipated.

He caught Clara's eye once more. This time, her look was desperate, a silent plea for understanding, for rescue. Arthur offered her the barest, almost imperceptible nod, a gesture intended to convey his unwavering support, his promise that he would indeed stand by her.

The conversation moved on to other topics, but the underlying current of the evening had shifted. The pleasantry had faded, replaced by a tangible sense of purpose from Lord Harrington and Lord Ashworth, and a growing unease from Clara and Arthur.

As the desserts were served,  delicate fruit tarts and rich syllabubs,  Lord Harrington raised his glass. "To our esteemed guests, Lord Ashworth and Lady Eleanor. May their visit be as delightful to them as it is to us. And may the bonds of friendship and, perhaps, something more, flourish between our families." He looked pointedly at Clara and Ashworth as he spoke, his meaning unmistakable.

Ashworth raised his glass, his eyes gleaming with satisfaction, clearly interpreting Harrington's words as a further endorsement of his pursuit. Clara, however, merely touched her glass to her lips, her gaze remaining fixed on the table before her, her expression unreadable.

Arthur, though compelled to raise his own glass in polite acknowledgement, felt a surge of rebellious resolve. "Something more," Harrington had said. Indeed. But the "something more" he envisioned was diametrically opposed to the Earl's wishes.

The dinner eventually drew to a close, and the company retired once more to the drawing room for coffee and liqueurs. The atmosphere was lighter now, the formalities of the meal having been observed. Lord Harrington and Lord Ashworth engaged in a more relaxed discussion of political matters, while Lady Eleanor, with a shrewd glance at Clara, moved to engage her in conversation about fashion and charitable endeavors.

Arthur, for his part, found a quiet corner near a bookshelf, ostensibly perusing the spines of various volumes, but in truth, his attention was keenly focused on Clara. He watched her animated conversation with Lady Eleanor, noting the genuine smiles she offered her. Perhaps, he mused, Eleanor might prove to be an unexpected ally, or at least a neutral party. She did not seem to share her brother's overtly ambitious nature.

After a respectable period, Lord Ashworth approached Clara, interrupting her conversation with his sister. "Lady Clara, I must confess, the evening has been most illuminating. I find our shared interests to be more numerous than I had first anticipated." He offered her a confident smile, clearly believing he had made significant progress.

Clara returned his smile with a polite, if somewhat weary, grace. "Indeed, Lord Ashworth. It has been a most interesting discussion."

"I shall look forward to continuing it tomorrow, over breakfast, perhaps?" he pressed, his gaze intense.

Clara, under her father's watchful eye, could hardly refuse. "That would be most agreeable, Lord Ashworth."

Arthur’s heart sank a little further. Breakfast. Another opportunity for Ashworth to press his suit. This was going to be a battle of attrition, a slow siege against Clara's resolve. He recognized that his position was precarious. He was a mere secretary, reliant on Lord Harrington’s patronage. Any overt challenge to Ashworth, or indeed to Lord Harrington’s plans, could result in his immediate dismissal, and that would sever his access to Clara entirely. He had to be strategic, subtle, and utterly unwavering in his conviction.

As the hour grew late, Lord Ashworth and Lady Eleanor prepared to depart, their carriage waiting outside. Lord Harrington bade them a warm farewell, expressing his profound pleasure at their visit and reiterating his hope for future, more frequent, interactions. Ashworth, before stepping into the carriage, turned to Clara once more, a possessive glint in his eye. "Until tomorrow, Lady Clara."

Clara offered a slight nod, her expression veiled.

Once the guests had departed, Lord Harrington turned to Clara, his face beaming. "An excellent evening, my dear! Ashworth is precisely the kind of man I had hoped for. Well-connected, prosperous, with a clear vision for the future. And so taken with you! Did you not observe it?"

Clara forced a small smile. "He is certainly... attentive, Father."

Lord Harrington clapped his hands together. "Attentive indeed! And rightly so. He represents a most advantageous match, Clara. One that will secure your position, and more importantly, the standing of this family, for generations to come. I have every confidence that you will come to appreciate his many qualities. Indeed, I would be most surprised if he does not present a formal offer before the week is out."

Clara's face, for a fleeting moment, paled. She looked utterly trapped, a beautiful bird ensnared in a gilded cage. She met Arthur’s gaze across the almost empty drawing room. Her eyes were wide with a silent desperation, a stark contrast to the composed woman she had been moments before. Arthur offered her a look of reassurance, a promise held in his steady, unwavering stare. He would not allow it. He would not stand by and watch her be forced into a union she did not desire.

"Good night, Father," Clara said, her voice barely a whisper. "I am quite fatigued." She curtsied quickly and retreated from the room, leaving Lord Harrington to contemplate his perceived success.

Arthur, feeling the weight of the evening pressing down on him, waited until Lord Harrington had retired to his study, no doubt to tally the evening's triumphs. The house was now quiet, save for the distant sounds of servants extinguishing lamps. He remained in the drawing room for a few moments, the scent of sherry and extinguished candles hanging in the air. His mind raced, analyzing every interaction, every subtle glance, every unspoken word.

Ashworth's confidence, Harrington's eagerness, Clara's quiet distress. The pieces of the puzzle formed a bleak picture. An engagement could be imminent. He had very little time.

He thought of Clara's despairing gaze, and the memory fueled a fierce, protective determination within him. He had promised her he would not stand by. He would not. But how to act? An open declaration of his affection would be ruinous, for both of them. It would paint Clara as disobedient, perhaps even scandalous, and himself as an opportunistic schemer, surely leading to his immediate banishment. He needed a strategy, a way to subtly undermine Ashworth's pretensions, to expose the superficiality that Clara sensed beneath his polished exterior, without revealing his own hand too soon.

He considered Lady Eleanor. She seemed intelligent, perhaps even sympathetic. Her subtle digs at her brother had not gone unnoticed. Could she be an unexpected ally, or at least a source of information? It was a possibility worth exploring.

But ultimately, the burden rested on him. He had to prove to Lord Harrington that Ashworth was not the "perfect choice." He had to reveal that true virtue lay elsewhere, not in titles or land. He had to show them all that the "something more" Clara desired, the "something more" that he represented, was infinitely more valuable than any worldly acquisition. This would require courage, cunning, and an unwavering belief in the power of their love.

Arthur walked slowly up the grand staircase, the silence of the house amplifying the beating of his own heart. The battle had been joined tonight. It was no longer a theoretical threat, but a living, breathing challenge. As he reached his own chamber, he looked out the window. The stars, which had witnessed his solemn vows with Clara in the garden, now glittered like watchful eyes in the vast, indifferent expanse of the night. He would not fail her. He would not fail them. The fight for Clara Harrington had only just begun. And he, Arthur Penhaligon, would fight it with every fiber of his being. The conviction was absolute, unshakeable. He had faced down hardship before, had clawed his way from obscurity to a position of trust, however limited. This was a different kind of fight, one for the heart, for a future that seemed impossibly distant yet utterly essential. He would find a way. He had to. For Clara. For them. The thought of Lord Ashworth's confident smirk, the casual way he had laid claim to Clara's attention, only served to sharpen his resolve. The game was on.

The first grey light of dawn, tentative and cool, filtered through Arthur's chamber window. He had slept little, his mind a relentless churn of possibilities and perils, strategies and pitfalls. The absolute conviction he had felt the previous night had not diminished with the coming day. Instead, it had solidified into a cold, hard resolve. He rose, the silence of the large house still pervasive, broken only by the chirping of early birds. He dressed with an uncharacteristic meticulousness, every button, every fold, a small act of defiant preparation. He was not merely a secretary now; he was a combatant, albeit one whose weapons must be intellect and discretion.

He reviewed his position. Lord Harrington held the power, Clara's father, a man driven by societal expectations and the desire for a prestigious lineage. Lord Ashworth possessed the pedigree, the land, the superficial charm that so easily impressed men like Harrington. Clara, caught between filial duty and a yearning for something deeper, was the prize, and the casualty, of this impending conflict. Arthur himself, lacking title or significant fortune, had only his mind, his loyalty, and the silent understanding he shared with Clara. To openly challenge Ashworth was suicide; to allow events to unfold passively was equally unthinkable. He needed a third path, a subtle erosion of Ashworth's appeal, a quiet demonstration of his own intrinsic worth.

His first objective was observation. He needed to understand Ashworth's routines, his habits, his vulnerabilities. Where did his polished facade thin? What topics bored him, or revealed his shallow understanding? He also needed to identify opportunities to showcase Ashworth's deficiencies without appearing to do so deliberately. This required patience and a keen eye. And then there was Lady Eleanor. She was his most promising, if unpredictable, avenue for assistance. Her quick wit and discerning gaze suggested an intelligence that might see beyond her brother's superficial glitter. He would need to approach her carefully, perhaps through a shared interest, a casual conversation that could lead to a deeper understanding. He would not ask for her help directly, not yet, but rather seek to cultivate her sympathy for Clara's situation.

The breakfast table was a scene of subdued activity when Arthur descended. Lord Harrington was already seated, immersed in a stack of correspondence. Clara sat opposite him, her eyes shadowed, picking at a piece of toast. Ashworth, however, was already in full flow, regaling anyone within earshot with an anecdote about a recent fox hunt. He was loud, expansive, and utterly oblivious to the subtle despair emanating from Clara. Lady Eleanor, positioned next to Clara, listened with an amused, almost detached air, occasionally raising an eyebrow at her brother's pronouncements.

Arthur took his usual seat, a discreet distance from the main conversation. He served himself a modest plate and listened. Ashworth spoke of the glorious chase, the prowess of his hounds, the fine condition of his horses. He used terminology designed to impress, to convey an air of aristocratic ease and expertise. Yet, Arthur noticed, he offered no real insight, no reflection on the beauty of the countryside, no genuine affection for the animals, only the thrill of the kill and the superiority of his equipment. It was a performance, polished but hollow.

"It sounds as though you had quite the exhilarating morning, Ashworth," Lord Harrington remarked, finally looking up from his letters, a hint of paternal pride in his tone.

"Indeed, sir. Nothing quite like it to clear the cobwebs and sharpen the senses. One needs a sturdy constitution for it, I assure you. Not every gentleman is suited to the rigours of the field." Ashworth cast a dismissive glance towards Arthur, a barely perceptible flicker of contempt.

Arthur met his gaze impassively. He had no interest in hunting, finding it a barbaric pursuit, but he knew better than to voice such an opinion in this company. He would not be drawn into direct conflict on such trivialities.

"Some prefer the rigour of the mind," Lady Eleanor interjected smoothly, her voice a cool counterpoint to her brother's bluster. She looked directly at Arthur, a knowing glint in her eye. "Mr Penhaligon, I believe you were perusing the latest edition of the Royal Society's proceedings yesterday evening? Did anything of note catch your eye?"

Arthur felt a small surge of gratitude towards Eleanor. She had thrown him a lifeline, an opportunity to demonstrate a different kind of "rigour." "Indeed, Lady Eleanor. There was a most fascinating article on the advancements in textile machinery. The potential for efficiency and improved working conditions is quite remarkable, though the societal impact on cottage industries would, of course, require careful consideration." He spoke thoughtfully, not boastfully, genuinely engaged with the subject.

Harrington, usually indifferent to such matters, perked an ear. "Textile machinery, you say? Hmm. Always interested in industrial progress. Are these designs practical, Penhaligon, or mere theoretical musings?"

"They appear eminently practical, my lord, particularly the new loom design. It promises to reduce manual labour while increasing output, which could benefit not only the manufacturers but potentially lower costs for consumers, provided the transition is managed with foresight."

Ashworth, who had been attempting to engage Clara in a whispered conversation about a forthcoming ball, cleared his throat loudly. "Oh, the tedium of factory talk! Always such a dismal affair, Penhaligon. Leave the grimy details to the grubby merchants. A gentleman's concern lies with the land, his tenants, and the preservation of our noble traditions. Innovation, while perhaps necessary, should never overshadow the beauty of the established order." He delivered this pronouncement with an air of absolute authority, as if shutting down a frivolous debate.

Arthur merely inclined his head, a faint, almost imperceptible smile playing on his lips. Ashworth had just revealed precisely the sort of superficiality Arthur hoped to expose. He dismissed an entire field of human endeavour, one crucial to the nation's prosperity, as "tedium." He valued "noble traditions" over progress, and by extension, over the welfare of ordinary people affected by these changes.

Lady Eleanor caught Arthur's eye again, her own expression a mix of amusement and something more complex, perhaps understanding. Clara, who had been listening to Arthur, subtly straightened in her chair, a faint flush colouring her cheeks. For a brief moment, Arthur saw a spark of hope in her eyes, a silent acknowledgement of his victory in that small exchange.

After breakfast, the gentlemen retired to the library for morning papers and further discussion. Ashworth, predictably, gravitated towards the hunting journals and sporting news. Lord Harrington reviewed financial reports, occasionally calling upon Arthur for clarification or assistance. Arthur, meanwhile, used the opportunity to observe Ashworth more closely. He noticed that while Ashworth spoke confidently on a range of topics, his knowledge rarely extended beyond the most superficial understanding. He had opinions, strongly held ones, but they seemed to be inherited, not earned through diligent inquiry.

A while later, Ashworth, bored with the financial discussions, wandered towards the shelves. "Remarkable collection, Lord Harrington. So many books. Do you truly read them all?" he asked, a hint of genuine bewilderment in his voice.

Lord Harrington chuckled. "Not every single one, my boy, but a good many. A well stocked library is a sign of a well stocked mind, or so I like to believe."

"Indeed. I suppose. Though I find life itself to be the best teacher. Experience, rather than dusty tomes, is where true wisdom resides. And who has more experience than a man of property, managing his estates, engaging with the world?" Ashworth puffed out his chest slightly.

Arthur, from his desk, merely continued his work, but his mind registered the casual dismissal of learning. He recalled Clara's intellectual curiosity, her love for literature and history, her yearning for stimulating conversation. Ashworth's worldview offered none of that. This was a crucial point of divergence, a chasm between Ashworth's values and Clara's.

Later that morning, Arthur sought out Lady Eleanor. He found her in the conservatory, attending to some exotic orchids, her delicate fingers carefully misting the leaves. It was a tranquil setting, far removed from the bluster of the breakfast table.

"Lady Eleanor," Arthur began, his voice soft so as not to startle her, "those orchids are quite magnificent. They require such a delicate touch."

She turned, a small smile gracing her lips. "Mr Penhaligon. Indeed they do. Unlike some individuals, they respond well to gentle persuasion rather than brute force. They remind me of certain delicate sensibilities, easily bruised by an unthinking hand." Her gaze was pointed, though her tone remained light.

Arthur took a step closer, maintaining a respectful distance. "I confess I know little of horticulture, but I admire the dedication. I find myself often observing the natural world, seeking its patterns and lessons. Much like life, it presents its challenges, doesn't it?"

"Oh, it does," she agreed, sighing faintly. "Life, and the expectations that accompany it, can be a most thorny bush indeed. Particularly for young women, burdened by the weight of family honour and societal expectation." She glanced at him, a flicker of something close to sympathy in her eyes. "You seem to possess a keen understanding of such pressures, Mr Penhaligon. Perhaps it is your own journey, from more modest beginnings, that grants you such insight."

Arthur chose his words carefully. "I have indeed experienced my share of life's rougher edges, Lady Eleanor. And I have learned that true worth lies not in inherited circumstance, but in character, in compassion, and in the courage to choose one's own path, even when it is the more difficult one." He paused, letting his words hang in the air. "I observe Miss Harrington, and I see a young woman of extraordinary intellect and sensitivity, a spirit that yearns for more than mere gilded cages."

Lady Eleanor put down her misting bottle. She looked at Arthur directly, her expression serious now. "You speak with unusual frankness, Mr Penhaligon. And with a perception I rarely encounter in this household, save perhaps in Clara herself." A small, cynical smile played on her lips. "My brother, I fear, views the world through a very particular lens. One that values surface over substance, and convenience over genuine feeling. He sees Clara as a most suitable ornament for his estate, a necessary acquisition. He will never understand her 'yearning for more.'"

"And you, Lady Eleanor? Do you understand it?" Arthur asked, a silent plea in his tone.

She turned back to an orchid, tracing a delicate petal. "I understand enough to know that Clara deserves better than a life of elegant boredom, bound to a man whose greatest intellectual achievement is memorising the breeding lines of horses. My father, alas, is blind to such nuances. He sees only the title, the land, the respectable connection. He believes himself to be acting in Clara's best interests."

"But at what cost to her happiness?" Arthur pressed gently.

"Ah, happiness," she murmured. "An elusive butterfly, often crushed by the weight of good intentions. What would you have me do, Mr Penhaligon? My voice is but a whisper against my father's pronouncements."

"I would ask nothing of you directly, Lady Eleanor. Only that you observe. Observe my lord Ashworth, truly observe him. And perhaps, if the opportunity arises, allow others to observe him too. The truth, I believe, has a way of revealing itself, particularly when exposed to the light of genuine scrutiny."

She straightened, a thoughtful expression on her face. "You believe my brother's 'truth' will be sufficiently... unappealing?"

"I believe Miss Harrington deserves a partner who appreciates her depth, who can engage her mind, and who possesses a genuine heart, not merely a polished exterior," Arthur responded, his voice firm. "And I believe Lord Ashworth will struggle to demonstrate these qualities under even modest pressure."

Lady Eleanor picked up a small pair of shears, examining them. "My brother is not unintelligent, Mr Penhaligon. Merely uncurious. And rather proud. He has never been truly challenged. Perhaps a gentle nudge, a well placed question, might prove enlightening for all parties." She paused, then met his gaze with a faint, knowing smile. "Consider me... observing, Mr Penhaligon. And perhaps, occasionally, providing the most subtle of nudges."

Arthur felt a significant weight lift from him. He had found an ally, a subtle but intelligent one. "Thank you, Lady Eleanor. That is more than I could have hoped for."

As the day progressed, Arthur continued his observations. Ashworth spent a considerable portion of the afternoon supervising the conditioning of some horses in the stable yard, then returned to the drawing room, bored. He seemed to have no intellectual pursuits, no artistic inclinations, no genuine interest in the estate beyond its capacity to provide revenue and social standing. His conversation with Clara during afternoon tea was dominated by his own plans for expanding his hunting grounds and his disdain for the current government's agricultural policies, which he pronounced "entirely misinformed" without offering any detailed arguments or alternative solutions. Clara listened politely, her eyes distant.

Arthur made a point of discussing with Lord Harrington a new drainage system proposed for a distant field, a practical and somewhat complex engineering matter. He explained the merits and potential pitfalls with clarity and precision, drawing on his own past experiences and extensive reading. Ashworth, overhearing parts of the conversation, scoffed. "More tinkering with the earth, Penhaligon? Surely the land knows best how to manage its own moisture. We are gentlemen, not engineers." Lord Harrington, however, listened intently to Arthur, nodding his head at various points. The seed of doubt, Arthur hoped, had been planted.

The next morning brought an unexpected opportunity. Over breakfast, Lord Harrington was in a foul mood. "Confound it all!" he grumbled, slapping a letter down on the table. "Another dispute with the Craddocks and the Millers. Old Man Miller insists young Craddock's sheep are encroaching on his pasture, damaging his crop. Craddock claims the fence is ancient and always been thus. Petty squabbles, but they always escalate if left unattended. I haven't the patience for it this morning."

Ashworth, eager to impress, immediately puffed up. "Leave it to me, sir! A man of property understands how to handle tenants. A firm hand, that's what's needed. I shall ride out there immediately and set them straight. A clear pronouncement of the rules, a reminder of their duties to their landlord, and all will be resolved."

Lord Harrington looked at Ashworth, a flicker of uncertainty in his eyes. "They are both old families, Ashworth. Stubborn as mules, both of them. It's not always a simple matter of 'setting them straight.' There are often long standing grudges, historical claims. It requires a delicate touch sometimes."

"Nonsense, sir," Ashworth waved a dismissive hand. "Sentimentality has no place in estate management. They need to understand who holds the authority. I shall make it clear." He pushed back his chair, ready to depart.

Arthur, who had been listening intently, chose his moment carefully. "My lord, if I may. Perhaps it is not merely authority that is needed, but understanding. If the fence is indeed ancient, as young Craddock claims, it might not be a simple matter of encroachment, but a question of historical usage and undefined boundaries. There may be no single 'right' answer, only an equitable solution."

Lord Harrington looked at Arthur, a thoughtful expression on his face. "Equitable, you say? And how would one ascertain such a thing?"

"Perhaps by listening to both sides without prejudice, my lord. By examining any old maps or agreements, by understanding the livelihood of each family, and seeking a compromise that benefits both, rather than dictating terms from a position of power. Sometimes, a long-term solution is better achieved through conciliation than confrontation." Arthur spoke calmly, his eyes meeting Harrington's. He was not challenging Ashworth directly, but offering an alternative approach, one that subtly highlighted Ashworth's lack of nuanced understanding.

Ashworth scoffed. "Conciliation! With farmers? They'll take an inch and demand a mile, Penhaligon. One must maintain order. Any weakness is exploited."

"Perhaps, Lord Ashworth," Arthur countered gently, "but a judicious application of understanding can often lead to greater long-term loyalty and productivity. A happy tenant is a productive tenant."

Lord Harrington drummed his fingers on the table. He looked from Ashworth's confident, almost arrogant face, to Arthur's calm, reasoned one. He knew Ashworth represented what he desired for Clara's future status. But he also knew Arthur, and had seen his quiet competence time and again.

"Perhaps, Ashworth," Lord Harrington said slowly, "Mr Penhaligon has a point. It is a tricky situation. Why don't you ride out with Mr Penhaligon? Listen to his approach. Observe how he handles them. It might be... instructive."

Ashworth's face fell, but he quickly composed himself. To refuse would be to openly defy Lord Harrington, and to suggest he could not learn from a mere secretary. "Very well, sir. If you insist. Though I doubt there is much to be learned from such... diplomacy." He shot Arthur a venomous look.

Clara, who had been following the exchange with bated breath, now allowed a faint, almost imperceptible smile to touch her lips. Lady Eleanor, across the table, gave Arthur a subtle, approving nod.

The ride to the Craddock and Miller farms was largely silent. Ashworth, clearly irritated by the imposed collaboration, rode ahead, occasionally casting impatient glances back at Arthur. Arthur, for his part, used the time to mentally prepare. He had dealt with similar disputes in his earlier life, navigating the complexities of human relationships and grievances. He knew that beneath the surface of most arguments lay fear, pride, and a deep desire to be heard.

Upon arrival, the scene was exactly as Lord Harrington had described. Old Mr. Miller, a wiry man with a face like weathered leather, stood beside his field, gesticulating angrily towards a section of dilapidated fence. Young Mr. Craddock, sturdier and equally incensed, stood protectively near his flock of sheep.

Ashworth immediately took charge. "Now, then! What is all this nonsense? Lord Harrington has sent me to sort this out. Miller, you claim encroachment. Craddock, you dispute it. This ends now. I will have no bickering on Lord Harrington's land." He spoke loudly, his tone authoritative and dismissive, instantly alienating both men.

Miller, already agitated, puffed out his chest. "Nonsense, is it, m'lord? My winter wheat is bein' trampled! And my father's father farmed this land, and that fence has always marked my boundary!"

Craddock retorted, "That fence is a century old, sir! It stands where it always stood. His sheep wandered, not mine!"

The argument immediately escalated, both men shouting over Ashworth, who in turn attempted to shout them down. It was a chaotic and unproductive scene. Ashworth, clearly frustrated, threw up his hands. "This is precisely the sort of unreasonable behaviour I expect! This is hopeless!"

Arthur stepped forward, placing a gentle hand on Ashworth's arm. "My lord, perhaps we might take a slightly different approach." He then turned to the two farmers, his voice calm and clear, yet respectful. "Gentlemen, please. Lord Ashworth and I are here to help resolve this, not to exacerbate it. But we cannot do so if we cannot hear what each of you has to say." He paused, allowing his words to sink in. "Mr. Miller, could you show us exactly where you believe the encroachment is occurring, and explain the damage?"

Miller, surprised by the calm tone, hesitated, then grudgingly began to point out the trampled wheat. Arthur listened patiently, nodding occasionally, asking clarifying questions. He walked the disputed line of the fence, examining the ground, the plants, the evidence. He then turned to Craddock. "And Mr. Craddock, could you explain the history of this fence, as you understand it, and tell us about your sheep's grazing patterns?"

Craddock, initially wary, relaxed somewhat under Arthur's attentive gaze. He spoke of his family's history on the land, the traditional ways, and how his flock had always grazed in that particular area.

Arthur spent nearly an hour listening, observing, and asking questions. He discovered that the original fence line had indeed been built decades ago, before much of the land was properly surveyed. Over time, erosion and natural shifting of the earth had caused the boundary to become unclear in places. Miller, feeling his livelihood threatened, saw trespass. Craddock, feeling his family's long-standing rights infringed, saw injustice. It was not malicious intent, but a conflict born of ambiguity and perceived threat.

Ashworth, after his initial outburst, had stood by, arms crossed, growing increasingly bored and impatient. He interjected occasionally with blustering pronouncements about ownership rights, which only served to make the farmers more defensive.

Finally, Arthur had a clear picture. "Gentlemen," he began, addressing both farmers, "it seems this is not a simple case of one man being entirely in the wrong. The boundary itself, through no fault of either of you, has become unclear. Mr. Miller's crop is indeed suffering. Mr. Craddock's sheep are indeed grazing in an area they have always traditionally used. The solution, I believe, lies in clarifying that boundary for good."

He then proposed a solution. "We could measure out a precise, agreed-upon line, perhaps even with a surveyor, and rebuild this section of fence to be indisputable. In the interim, to compensate Mr. Miller for his damaged crop, Mr. Craddock could assist him with a day's labour on another part of his farm, or perhaps provide a small quantity of wool at a reduced rate from his next shearing. In return, Mr. Miller would agree to respect the newly demarcated boundary and cease his complaints, understanding that Mr. Craddock acted without malice."

The farmers looked at each other, then at Arthur. There was a pause, a weighty silence. Then, old Mr. Miller nodded slowly. "A day's labour, you say? Or some wool? Hmm. And a proper fence? That sounds... fair, young man. Much fairer than just bein' told we're wrong."

Craddock, relief washing over his face, agreed. "Aye, a new fence, that's what's needed. And I'd be glad to help out, Mr. Miller. No harm meant, you know."

A truce had been brokered. The agreement was sealed with handshakes.

Ashworth, who had witnessed the entire process, looked utterly bewildered. "Well, I'll be damned," he muttered under his breath. "They actually listened to him. I was ready to ride away and let them stew."

Arthur merely smiled faintly. "Patience, my lord, and a willingness to understand, can often achieve more than dictates."

On the ride back, Ashworth was uncharacteristically subdued. He still looked annoyed, but a flicker of grudging respect, or at least surprise, might have been visible in his eyes. He remained silent for most of the journey, deep in thought.

When they returned to Harrington Hall, Lord Harrington was waiting. "Well? What is the outcome?" he asked, looking expectantly at Ashworth.

Ashworth hesitated. "They... they reached an agreement, sir. A rather... unconventional one. Mr. Penhaligon had a proposal. It seems to have satisfied them for now." He spoke grudgingly, clearly discomfited by the unexpected success of Arthur's approach.

Arthur then provided a more detailed, yet modest, account of the resolution, emphasizing the farmers' willingness to compromise once they felt heard. "It appears, my lord, that a clearer boundary and a gesture of good will were all that was truly needed."

Lord Harrington listened, his expression growing increasingly thoughtful. He looked at Arthur, then at Ashworth. "So, Penhaligon, you managed to resolve a dispute that has festered for weeks, simply by... listening?"

"And proposing an equitable solution, my lord. It is often the simplest approach that proves most effective, once the true nature of the problem is understood."

Lord Harrington nodded slowly. "Indeed. Indeed. Well done, Penhaligon. Very well done." There was a genuine note of approval in his voice, a hint of something more than mere pleasantry. He then turned to Ashworth. "Perhaps there is indeed something to be learned from such diplomacy, my boy. A firm hand is useful, but a wise one is invaluable."

Ashworth merely grunted, his face a mask of annoyance. He knew he had been outmanoeuvred, not through cunning or trickery, but through genuine competence and a deeper understanding of human nature.

Arthur, though externally calm, felt a surge of quiet triumph. He had not only resolved a pressing estate matter, but he had done so in a way that subtly highlighted Ashworth's shortcomings and his own strengths, all under the direct observation of Lord Harrington. He had shown that "the something more" Clara desired was not a weakness, but a profound strength.

Later that evening, as Arthur prepared for dinner, he caught a glimpse of Clara in the corridor. Her eyes, no longer shadowed with despair, held a gentle light. She offered him a small, almost imperceptible smile, one that spoke volumes. It was a smile of gratitude, of recognition, and perhaps, of rekindled hope. He had taken a significant step. The battle for Clara's future was far from over, but he had proven that he was a formidable opponent, not just for Lord Ashworth, but for the entrenched expectations of a society that valued title over true worth. He had just demonstrated a different kind of power, one rooted in empathy and intellect. And that, he knew, was a power that Ashworth, for all his inherited status, could never truly possess. The fight had moved from being a theoretical challenge to a tangible, ongoing engagement. Arthur had drawn first blood, not with a sword, but with understanding. He had shown them a glimpse of the man he was, and the man Ashworth was not. The conviction he felt was now laced with a potent sense of hope. He would not fail them. He would not. The game was most certainly on.

The aroma of roast pheasant and claret filled the dining hall that evening, a rich tapestry of scents that usually heralded an atmosphere of jovial, if formal, ease. Tonight, however, an undercurrent of tension, subtle yet palpable, permeated the otherwise splendid setting. Lord Harrington presided at the head of the table, his gaze often drifting to Arthur, who sat a few places down. There was a contemplative air about the old lord, a quiet assessment in his eyes that made Arthur feel both scrutinized and, paradoxically, seen. Ashworth, seated opposite Arthur, maintained a stiff posture. His fork clinked against his plate with slightly more force than necessary, and his eyes, when they met Arthur's, held a cold, impenetrable glint. He said little throughout the meal, offering only terse, monosyllabic responses when directly addressed. The casual banter that often punctuated these dinners was noticeably absent.

Arthur, though aware of the scrutiny and the simmering resentment, focused on his meal, contributing to the occasional polite conversation initiated by Lady Harrington, who, though oblivious to the deeper undercurrents, was adept at maintaining social graces. Yet, even as he spoke of the weather or the recent harvest, a part of his mind remained attuned to the subtle shifts in the room. He felt the weight of Lord Harrington’s gaze, not as a burden, but as an opportunity. He knew this was not merely a victory in a single skirmish, but the beginning of a prolonged campaign.

Midway through the main course, Arthur’s eyes, almost by instinct, sought out Clara. She sat further down the table, her presence usually as muted as her soft gowns, but tonight there was a faint luminescence about her. Her eyes, which had for so long held a resigned sorrow, now possessed a quiet spark. When their gazes briefly met, a fleeting, almost imperceptible smile touched her lips, a delicate affirmation that transcended words. It was a gesture of profound gratitude, a silent acknowledgment of the strength he had displayed. It fortified him, reminding him of the true stakes of this unspoken game. He offered a slight, equally discreet nod in return, a promise that he would not waver. The sight of her renewed hope was a powerful elixir, dispelling any lingering doubts he might have harbored about the path he had chosen.

After dinner, as the gentlemen retired to the smoking room, Lord Harrington motioned for Arthur to join him near the large fireplace. Ashworth, who had been making his way towards the decanters, paused, a flicker of irritation crossing his face before he continued on, settling into a chair furthest from them.

"Penhaligon," Lord Harrington began, his voice a low rumble, "that business with the farmers. It was handled...unconventionally, as Ashworth so begrudgingly put it." A dry chuckle escaped him. "But effectively. Most effectively. Tell me, what was your initial impression of the situation? What led you to believe a different approach was needed?"

Arthur chose his words carefully. "My lord, the reports indicated a long standing grievance, compounded by a lack of direct communication. The previous attempts, I gathered, focused on enforcing rules rather than understanding the root of the farmers' distress. People, my lord, often respond better to understanding than to dictates, especially when their livelihoods are at stake."

"And your solution? This boundary adjustment, this 'gesture of goodwill'?" Lord Harrington prodded, his eyes sharp.

"It was a practical resolution, my lord. The land in question, though legally yours, provided little benefit to the estate directly, while its proper use was vital to the farmers. By formalizing a slight adjustment, with a corresponding, albeit small, symbolic payment from them as acknowledgement of your lordship's generosity and proprietary right, we achieved both a practical solution and a restoration of trust. They gained clarity and security; the estate gained a loyal, cooperative tenant base. It was a trade of a small, disputed parcel for goodwill and stability."

Lord Harrington steepled his fingers, his gaze fixed on the dancing flames. "Goodwill and stability. Two commodities often undervalued in these matters. You see things differently, Penhaligon. You look beyond the immediate letter of the law, to the spirit of cooperation. A valuable trait, indeed." He paused, then continued, "I have been considering a number of matters on the estate. The new drainage system for the western fields, for instance. Ashworth has been tasked with overseeing it, but progress has been... slow. And the estimates keep rising. He insists on bringing in experts from London, citing local incompetence. But I confess, I wonder if a more localized, perhaps less costly, solution might exist."

Arthur understood the implicit invitation. "My lord, if you wish, I would be happy to review the plans and offer any insights I might have. I have some familiarity with agricultural engineering from my studies, though I am no expert."

"Modest, as ever," Lord Harrington said, a faint smile touching his lips. "But your insights thus far have proven remarkably perspicacious. Yes, Penhaligon, I would like you to look at the plans for the western fields. Confer with the foreman, Mr. Briggs. See what he has to say. He is a good man, Briggs, knows the land intimately, but is often ignored. Give me your assessment by the end of the week."

It was a clear delegation, a public vote of confidence, delivered with polite formality but immense significance. Ashworth, from his distant chair, stiffened perceptibly. The very air seemed to crackle with his silent fury. Arthur, however, merely bowed his head. "As you wish, my lord. I will apply myself to it at once."

The next morning, Arthur wasted no time. After a light breakfast, he sought out Mr. Briggs, the estate foreman, a man whose weather beaten face and gnarled hands spoke of decades spent toiling on the land. Briggs was initially wary, accustomed to being dismissed by the younger gentry. Arthur, however, approached him with genuine respect and an open ear.

"Mr. Briggs," Arthur began, "Lord Harrington has asked me to look into the western field drainage project. He specifically mentioned your deep understanding of the land. I would be grateful for your insights."

Briggs eyed him, a flicker of surprise in his rheumy eyes. "My insights, eh? Not many lords or their agents care for an old man's ramblings."

"Lord Harrington certainly does," Arthur assured him. "And so do I. We need your practical knowledge to ensure this project is both effective and efficient."

Over the next few hours, Arthur walked the western fields with Briggs, listening intently as the foreman explained the nuances of the terrain, the seasonal water flow, the historical challenges of the boggy patches, and the most sensible, often traditional, methods of drainage. Briggs pointed out areas where Ashworth's 'London experts' plans were impractical or overly complex, suggesting simpler, more robust solutions using local materials and techniques. He spoke of natural culverts, careful ditching, and even specific types of native plants that could aid in water absorption.

"Master Ashworth, he just looks at drawings," Briggs grumbled, spitting to the side. "Don't walk the land. Don't feel the squish of it under his boots. Wants to spend a fortune on pipes that'll likely clog in a few years anyway. We've always managed this land with a bit of common sense and hard graft, not with fancy city notions."

Arthur absorbed every word, asking probing questions, drawing sketches in his notebook, comparing Briggs's practical wisdom with the often abstract plans he had been given. It became clear that Ashworth's reliance on external 'experts' was not only more expensive but also less tailored to the specific challenges of Lord Harrington's estate. The London plans were generic, ill suited to the particular soil composition and microclimate. Briggs, on the other hand, offered solutions that were rooted

in the specific ecology of Harrington Hall.

Arthur spent the remainder of that day and much of the next immersed in the western fields. He walked every inch, sometimes sinking an inch or two into the softer ground, feeling the soil beneath his boots exactly as Briggs had described. He carried a small surveying kit, taking levels, noting gradients, and measuring the flow of existing streams and rivulets. Briggs, initially hesitant to leave his usual duties, found himself drawn into Arthur's methodical process. He saw that Arthur was not merely paying lip service to his wisdom but was genuinely trying to understand.

"See here, Master Penhaligon," Briggs would point with a calloused finger, "this dip, just by the old willow? That's where the water always collects after a heavy rain. No amount of buried pipe will properly draw it off if the main channel isn't cleared first, and that channel needs to follow the natural fall of the land, not some straight line drawn on a map."

Arthur would nod, sketching rapidly. "And what if we were to widen this existing natural culvert, perhaps line it with local stone to prevent erosion, and then supplement it with a series of smaller, shallower feeder ditches that gently direct the water towards it?"

Briggs’s eyes, usually clouded with resignation, brightened. "Aye, now you're talking sense. That's how we've always done it, when we were allowed to. Before the gentry started thinking cleverness meant complication." He paused, rubbing his chin. "And those sedges we spoke of, plant them along the banks. Their roots bind the soil, soak up the excess, and keep the banks from crumbling."

Their collaboration became a seamless dance of practical observation and scientific understanding. Arthur realised that agricultural engineering, at its core, was the formalisation and optimisation of centuries of empirical knowledge. Briggs’s methods were often intuitively perfect solutions to complex hydrological challenges, even if he did not use terms like "hydrostatic pressure" or "soil permeability." Arthur's role was to articulate the 'why' behind Briggs's 'how,' and to present it in a way that resonated with Lord Harrington's desire for efficiency and longevity.

He spent evenings poring over his notes, comparing the London plans to his own observations and Briggs’s recommendations. The London plans, he discovered, involved significant excavation to lay large diameter clay pipes at uniform depths. This approach ignored the varied soil strata, the presence of impermeable clay layers at certain depths, and the fluctuating water table. It was a one size fits all solution, poorly suited to the undulating, diverse terrain of the western fields. The cost estimates for the London plan were indeed astronomical, accounting for imported materials, extensive labour, and the machinery required for deep trenching.

Briggs's approach, which Arthur now enthusiastically endorsed, focused on working *with* the land rather than imposing upon it. It involved:
1.  **Clearing and widening existing natural watercourses:** Utilising the land's inherent drainage paths.
2.  **Shallow open ditches:** Carefully graded to direct surface water to the main channels, designed to be easily maintained by estate labour.
3.  **Strategic planting of water absorbing vegetation:** Sedge grasses, reeds, and certain native willow species along ditch banks and boggy margins.
4.  **Local materials:** Using fieldstone for culvert lining, timber for small sluices, all sourced from the estate or nearby.
5.  **Estate labour:** Employing the existing workforce, providing them with useful, practical work rather than relying on external contractors.

Arthur began drafting his report, carefully structuring his arguments. He included detailed sketches, cross sections of proposed ditches, and a clear comparison of costings. He emphasised the long term sustainability and the ecological benefits of Briggs's methods, arguing that they would not only drain the land effectively but also improve soil health and support local wildlife. He noted that the 'London experts' plans, while theoretically sound for a uniform, flat agricultural plain, were entirely inappropriate for Harrington Hall's specific topography and soil composition. He was careful to couch his criticisms in objective, factual terms, avoiding personal attacks on Ashworth, though the implicit critique was undeniable.

By Friday afternoon, Arthur had his report ready. It was a thick dossier, bound neatly, with precise diagrams and carefully tabulated figures. He had worked diligently, often late into the night, fueled by strong tea and a growing sense of purpose. He felt a quiet confidence in his findings.

Lord Harrington received Arthur in his study promptly at four o'clock. Ashworth was already present, seated stiffly in an armchair by the fireplace, a copy of the London plans conspicuously unfurled on a side table. His gaze, as Arthur entered, was a mixture of disdain and apprehension.

"Ah, Penhaligon," Lord Harrington greeted, indicating a chair opposite his desk. "Have you had a productive week with Mr. Briggs?"

"Indeed, my lord," Arthur replied, placing his report on the desk. "I believe I have a comprehensive assessment of the western fields drainage proposal, incorporating Mr. Briggs's invaluable practical knowledge."

Lord Harrington gestured for him to proceed. Arthur opened his report, his voice calm and measured. "My lord, after extensive consultation with Mr. Briggs and personal inspection of the western fields, I have come to the conclusion that the existing London plans, while professionally drafted, are not optimally suited for Harrington Hall's unique ecological and geological conditions."

Ashworth bristled. "And pray tell, Mr. Penhaligon, what makes you, with your rather limited experience, more qualified to judge the work of esteemed metropolitan engineers?" His tone was sharp, laced with condescension.

Arthur met his gaze evenly. "My qualifications, Mr. Ashworth, are in agricultural engineering. My assessment is based on the specific application of those principles to the observed conditions of the land, informed by Mr. Briggs's unparalleled local knowledge. The London plans propose a uniform system of deep, buried clay pipe drainage. This approach, while effective in some contexts, is highly inefficient and indeed problematic for the western fields."

He turned to Lord Harrington. "Specifically, my lord, the western fields possess varying soil strata, including shallow clay pans that impede vertical drainage, and numerous natural depressions and historical watercourses. The proposed deep piping system would necessitate extensive excavation, disrupting the soil structure, and would likely struggle with the fluctuating water table. Furthermore, the material costs for imported clay pipes and the labour for such deep trenching are substantial."

He then laid out a series of his sketches. "Mr. Briggs and I have developed an alternative proposal. It focuses on enhancing the estate's natural drainage capabilities. We propose to clear and strategically widen existing natural culverts, which follow the natural gradient of the land, rather than forcing straight lines through uneven terrain. These would be supplemented by a network of shallower, open ditches." He pointed to a detailed diagram. "These ditches would gently guide surface water into the main channels. Their open nature allows for easy maintenance by estate workers and facilitates natural aeration of the soil."

"Open ditches?" Ashworth scoffed. "My dear Penhaligon, are we to return to the dark ages of drainage? Buried pipes are the mark of modern agriculture, preventing evaporation and maintaining aesthetic appeal."

"Aesthetic appeal is secondary to functionality in agricultural land, Mr. Ashworth," Arthur countered politely but firmly. "And as for evaporation, these ditches are designed for water removal, not retention. Moreover, the London plans did not account for the rapid clogging potential of the buried pipes given the specific silts and organic matter present in these fields. Open ditches, as Mr. Briggs has demonstrated over decades, are easily cleaned and maintainable."

He continued, "We also propose the strategic planting of native water absorbing vegetation along ditch banks and in marshier areas. Plants such as certain sedges and rushes have extensive root systems that bind soil, prevent erosion, and actively absorb excess moisture, contributing to a healthier ecosystem. All materials,  stone for culvert lining, timber for small sluices,  can be sourced from the estate itself, or locally, dramatically reducing material costs and transport overheads."

Arthur then presented a comparative cost analysis. "My lord, the London plan, as estimated, would cost approximately two thousand pounds for materials and external labour, with an estimated completion time of nine to twelve months due to specialized equipment and contractor scheduling. Our alternative, utilising estate labour and locally sourced materials, is estimated at no more than four hundred pounds, with a projected completion time of four to six months, and continuous maintenance can be integrated into the existing estate work schedule."

Lord Harrington had been listening intently, occasionally stroking his chin. He picked up Arthur's report, thumbing through the pages of sketches and calculations. Ashworth, meanwhile, was visibly seething, his face a tight mask of indignation.

"Four hundred pounds versus two thousand," Lord Harrington murmured, his eyes fixed on the figures. "A considerable difference, Penhaligon. And you are confident in these estimates?"

"Absolutely, my lord," Arthur affirmed. "Mr. Briggs assisted directly with the material sourcing and labour hour estimations. He has a precise understanding of what is required and what the estate workforce is capable of achieving."

"But these are mere ditches, my lord," Ashworth interjected, his voice strained. "Common, unsophisticated work. One cannot expect modern results from such antiquated methods. The metropolitan engineers employed by myself have studied at the finest institutions. Their methods are scientifically proven, not just the ramblings of an old foreman."

"Mr. Ashworth," Lord Harrington said, his voice quiet but firm, "common sense, when applied to the land, often proves more reliable than abstract theory. And Mr. Briggs's 'ramblings,' as you call them, have sustained this estate for generations. Penhaligon's report is not merely a summary of Mr. Briggs's views, but a detailed engineering proposal that integrates practical wisdom with scientific principles. He has provided objective analysis, complete with verifiable costings and environmental benefits."

He looked from Ashworth to Arthur, then back to the report. "Penhaligon, your proposal is compelling. The cost savings alone are significant, but your emphasis on sustainability and working with the land's natural features is particularly astute. This aligns with my own long-term vision for the estate. I confess, the London plans always struck me as rather... grand, for simple drainage."

Ashworth spluttered, "But my lord, I commissioned those plans in good faith! They represent the cutting edge of agricultural innovation!"

"And I appreciate your diligence, Ashworth," Lord Harrington said, a slight edge now in his voice. "But innovation must be appropriate. A state of the art plough is useless if it cannot turn our soil. Penhaligon, I want you to proceed with your plan for the western fields. Work closely with Mr. Briggs. I want to see this implemented without delay."

Arthur bowed his head. "As you wish, my lord. I will oversee it personally."

"And Ashworth," Lord Harrington continued, turning to his steward, "while I value your initiatives, I expect you to respect my decisions. This is not a slight against you, but a practical choice for the good of the estate."

Ashworth, though outwardly composed, emanated a furious energy. "Of course, my lord. Your will is paramount." But his eyes, as they flickered to Arthur, held a chilling promise of future retribution. Arthur sensed it, a cold prickle on his skin. He had won this skirmish, but the war for Lord Harrington's trust and the estate's future was far from over.

The next Monday morning, the western fields buzzed with activity. Under Arthur's quiet direction and Briggs's experienced supervision, estate workers began the task of clearing and deepening the natural watercourses. Arthur was present every day, often alongside Briggs, directing the placement of stones, checking gradients with his surveying instruments, and explaining the purpose of each step to the men. He found a deep satisfaction in the physical work, in seeing his plans translate directly into tangible progress.

The estate workers, initially cautious of the new 'gentleman engineer,' quickly came to respect Arthur's hands on approach and his genuine interest in their work. He listened to their suggestions, incorporated their knowledge of specific tricky spots, and even shared a flask of tea with them during breaks. Briggs, in particular, flourished under Arthur's trust. His gruff exterior softened, and he took immense pride in seeing his lifelong observations finally put into practice with the full backing of the estate.

"This is how it should always have been," Briggs grumbled contentedly one afternoon, watching a line of men carefully shaping a ditch bank. "Working with the land, not fighting it. That Master Ashworth, he'd have had us digging trenches deep enough for a coffin, and for what? To bury good money and get poor results."

Arthur smiled. "Sometimes, Mr. Briggs, the simplest solutions are the most elegant."

Over the following weeks, the transformation of the western fields was remarkable. The boggy patches began to recede, the soil, though still damp in places, felt firmer, and the newly cleared ditches channeled water efficiently towards the main river. The strategically planted sedges began to take root, their green blades promising future stability. The cost remained precisely within Arthur's estimates, and the work was completed well within the projected timeframe.

Lord Harrington made several visits to the fields, always accompanied by Arthur and Briggs. He observed the progress with evident satisfaction. "Penhaligon, this is truly excellent work. The fields look healthier, and the water management is visibly superior. And all at a fraction of the cost. You have proven your worth beyond doubt."

His commendation, delivered with genuine warmth, was a balm to Arthur's spirit. He felt a profound sense of accomplishment, not just for the success of the project, but for having earned the trust of Lord Harrington and the respect of the estate workers.

Ashworth, however, was a constant, brooding presence. He would sometimes ride past the western fields, his eyes scanning the work with a critical, almost hostile glare. He never stopped to speak to Arthur or Briggs, but his disdain was palpable. He rarely missed an opportunity to make a subtly disparaging remark in Lord Harrington's presence, though these attempts were now largely ignored.

"I do hope these open ditches do not attract an undue number of vermin, my lord," Ashworth might say during dinner, casting a sidelong glance at Arthur. Or, "One trusts the aesthetic integrity of the estate will not be permanently compromised by such... rustic alterations."

Lord Harrington would usually respond with a dismissive wave of his hand. "The integrity of the estate, Ashworth, rests on its productivity and health, not on some artificial notion of beauty that hinders its function. And I have yet to see a single rat drawn by a clear, flowing ditch."

Despite Lord Harrington's unwavering support, Arthur was acutely aware that Ashworth's animosity was a persistent threat. Ashworth was too deeply entrenched in the estate's administration, too proud, and too accustomed to unchallenged authority to simply retreat. Arthur knew this victory would only serve to harden Ashworth's resolve to undermine him.

His position, while strengthened, also made him a target. He had effectively discredited Ashworth in an area where the steward had asserted expertise. This was a direct challenge to Ashworth's authority and status, and such slights, Arthur knew, were rarely forgiven in the intricate hierarchy of an aristocratic household.

One evening, as Arthur reviewed the accounts for the western fields project, ensuring every last nail and shovelful of gravel was accounted for, he heard a soft knock at his door. It was Mr. Davies, Lord Harrington's private secretary, a quiet, efficient man whom Arthur had come to respect.

"Mr. Penhaligon," Davies began, "Lord Harrington sends his compliments regarding the completion of the western fields project. He is most impressed."

"My thanks, Mr. Davies," Arthur replied. "It was a team effort, largely thanks to Mr. Briggs."

Davies offered a rare, slight smile. "Indeed. But your leadership was instrumental. Lord Harrington has asked me to convey a new task. He would like you to investigate the rotation of crops in the northern fields. He feels the yields have been declining over the past few seasons, and has heard, perhaps from Mr. Briggs, that you possess knowledge in this area as well."

Arthur felt a surge of gratification. This was another significant delegation, further cementing his role. "I would be delighted, Mr. Davies. I have indeed studied crop rotation methods, soil enrichment, and modern agricultural practices. I will commence my investigation immediately."

Davies nodded. "Excellent. Lord Harrington mentioned that Mr. Ashworth has, for some years, been advocating a consistent rotation of wheat and barley, citing market demand. However, the land itself seems to be suffering for it." He paused, lowering his voice slightly. "He also indicated that Mr. Ashworth has expressed strong views against the introduction of any 'unconventional' crops or methods."

Arthur understood the subtle warning. He was once again being pitted against Ashworth, this time in the very heart of the estate's agricultural productivity. Ashworth's 'market demand' argument likely masked a desire for simplicity and control, perhaps a reluctance to invest in new seed or educate himself on different cultivation practices.

"I understand, Mr. Davies," Arthur said. "I will approach the matter with due diligence and provide Lord Harrington with a comprehensive assessment of the options."

As Davies departed, Arthur leaned back in his chair, a thoughtful expression on his face. The northern fields. Another challenge. Another opportunity to prove himself, and another potential confrontation with Ashworth. He knew that the steward would likely view any deviation from his established crop rotation as a direct attack on his judgment.

He began to formulate his approach. He would need to study the soil composition of the northern fields, analyze historical yield data, and research various crop rotation systems, including the integration of legumes for nitrogen fixation and fallow periods for soil recovery. He would again seek out Mr. Briggs, whose practical understanding of the estate's soil history and microclimates would be invaluable.

Arthur felt a growing sense of belonging at Harrington Hall. He was no longer just an educated guest, but an active participant in the estate's management, a contributor to its prosperity. This gave him a purpose, a structure to his days, and a fulfillment he hadn't known since his university studies. He was an engineer of the land, applying his intellect and knowledge to real world problems.

Yet, the undercurrent of tension with Ashworth remained a constant, nagging presence. Ashworth controlled the estate finances, managed the larger contracts, and had Lord Harrington's ear on many matters. Arthur knew he could not afford to be complacent. Ashworth's attacks might be subtle, but they were persistent, and a misstep by Arthur could be disastrous. The steward would be waiting for such a moment, ready to exploit any perceived failure.

Arthur opened a fresh notebook, titled "Northern Fields Crop Rotation." He dipped his pen in ink, a quiet resolve settling over him. He would not just study crop rotation; he would present a plan that was not only economically sound but environmentally beneficial, one that would secure the long-term health and productivity of Lord Harrington's cherished estate. This was not merely about winning arguments; it was about ensuring the future of Harrington Hall. And in that, he found a profound and meaningful cause. The path ahead was clear, though fraught with political challenges. Arthur was ready. He felt a quiet excitement for the work to come, even as he braced himself for the inevitable resistance. The story of Harrington Hall, and his place within it, was just beginning to unfold. He had found his calling, and he intended to pursue it with every fibre of his being. The legacy of his education, the practical wisdom of the land, and the expectations of Lord Harrington now rested on his shoulders. He would not disappoint. He felt a quiet strength welling within him, a determination to navigate the complexities of estate management and human ambition, all for the sake of the land he was growing to love. The western fields were merely the first step. The northern fields beckoned, and with them, another chapter in the intricate tapestry of Harrington Hall.in the specific ecology of Harrington Hall.

Arthur spent the remainder of that day and much of the next immersed in the western fields. He walked every inch, sometimes sinking an inch or two into the softer ground, feeling the soil beneath his boots exactly as Briggs had described. He carried a small surveying kit, taking levels, noting gradients, and measuring the flow of existing streams and rivulets. Briggs, initially hesitant to leave his usual duties, found himself drawn into Arthur's methodical process. He saw that Arthur was not merely paying lip service to his wisdom but was genuinely trying to understand.

"See here, Master Penhaligon," Briggs would point with a calloused finger, "this dip, just by the old willow? That's where the water always collects after a heavy rain. No amount of buried pipe will properly draw it off if the main channel isn't cleared first, and that channel needs to follow the natural fall of the land, not some straight line drawn on a map."

Arthur would nod, sketching rapidly. "And what if we were to widen this existing natural culvert, perhaps line it with local stone to prevent erosion, and then supplement it with a series of smaller, shallower feeder ditches that gently direct the water towards it?"

Briggs’s eyes, usually clouded with resignation, brightened. "Aye, now you're talking sense. That's how we've always done it, when we were allowed to. Before the gentry started thinking cleverness meant complication." He paused, rubbing his chin. "And those sedges we spoke of, plant them along the banks. Their roots bind the soil, soak up the excess, and keep the banks from crumbling."

Their collaboration became a seamless dance of practical observation and scientific understanding. Arthur realised that agricultural engineering, at its core, was the formalisation and optimisation of centuries of empirical knowledge. Briggs’s methods were often intuitively perfect solutions to complex hydrological challenges, even if he did not use terms like "hydrostatic pressure" or "soil permeability." Arthur's role was to articulate the 'why' behind Briggs's 'how,' and to present it in a way that resonated with Lord Harrington's desire for efficiency and longevity.

He spent evenings poring over his notes, comparing the London plans to his own observations and Briggs’s recommendations. The London plans, he discovered, involved significant excavation to lay large diameter clay pipes at uniform depths. This approach ignored the varied soil strata, the presence of impermeable clay layers at certain depths, and the fluctuating water table. It was a one size fits all solution, poorly suited to the undulating, diverse terrain of the western fields. The cost estimates for the London plan were indeed astronomical, accounting for imported materials, extensive labour, and the machinery required for deep trenching.

Briggs's approach, which Arthur now enthusiastically endorsed, focused on working *with* the land rather than imposing upon it. It involved:
1. Clearing and widening existing natural watercourses: Utilising the land's inherent drainage paths.
2. Shallow open ditches: Carefully graded to direct surface water to the main channels, designed to be easily maintained by estate labour.
3. Strategic planting of water absorbing vegetation: Sedge grasses, reeds, and certain native willow species along ditch banks and boggy margins.
4. Local materials: Using fieldstone for culvert lining, timber for small sluices, all sourced from the estate or nearby.
5. Estate labour: Employing the existing workforce, providing them with useful, practical work rather than relying on external contractors.

Arthur began drafting his report, carefully structuring his arguments. He included detailed sketches, cross sections of proposed ditches, and a clear comparison of costings. He emphasised the long term sustainability and the ecological benefits of Briggs's methods, arguing that they would not only drain the land effectively but also improve soil health and support local wildlife. He noted that the 'London experts' plans, while theoretically sound for a uniform, flat agricultural plain, were entirely inappropriate for Harrington Hall's specific topography and soil composition. He was careful to couch his criticisms in objective, factual terms, avoiding personal attacks on Ashworth, though the implicit critique was undeniable.

By Friday afternoon, Arthur had his report ready. It was a thick dossier, bound neatly, with precise diagrams and carefully tabulated figures. He had worked diligently, often late into the night, fueled by strong tea and a growing sense of purpose. He felt a quiet confidence in his findings.

Lord Harrington received Arthur in his study promptly at four o'clock. Ashworth was already present, seated stiffly in an armchair by the fireplace, a copy of the London plans conspicuously unfurled on a side table. His gaze, as Arthur entered, was a mixture of disdain and apprehension.

"Ah, Penhaligon," Lord Harrington greeted, indicating a chair opposite his desk. "Have you had a productive week with Mr. Briggs?"

"Indeed, my lord," Arthur replied, placing his report on the desk. "I believe I have a comprehensive assessment of the western fields drainage proposal, incorporating Mr. Briggs's invaluable practical knowledge."

Lord Harrington gestured for him to proceed. Arthur opened his report, his voice calm and measured. "My lord, after extensive consultation with Mr. Briggs and personal inspection of the western fields, I have come to the conclusion that the existing London plans, while professionally drafted, are not optimally suited for Harrington Hall's unique ecological and geological conditions."

Ashworth bristled. "And pray tell, Mr. Penhaligon, what makes you, with your rather limited experience, more qualified to judge the work of esteemed metropolitan engineers?" His tone was sharp, laced with condescension.

Arthur met his gaze evenly. "My qualifications, Mr. Ashworth, are in agricultural engineering. My assessment is based on the specific application of those principles to the observed conditions of the land, informed by Mr. Briggs's unparalleled local knowledge. The London plans propose a uniform system of deep, buried clay pipe drainage. This approach, while effective in some contexts, is highly inefficient and indeed problematic for the western fields."

He turned to Lord Harrington. "Specifically, my lord, the western fields possess varying soil strata, including shallow clay pans that impede vertical drainage, and numerous natural depressions and historical watercourses. The proposed deep piping system would necessitate extensive excavation, disrupting the soil structure, and would likely struggle with the fluctuating water table. Furthermore, the material costs for imported clay pipes and the labour for such deep trenching are substantial."

He then laid out a series of his sketches. "Mr. Briggs and I have developed an alternative proposal. It focuses on enhancing the estate's natural drainage capabilities. We propose to clear and strategically widen existing natural culverts, which follow the natural gradient of the land, rather than forcing straight lines through uneven terrain. These would be supplemented by a network of shallower, open ditches." He pointed to a detailed diagram. "These ditches would gently guide surface water into the main channels. Their open nature allows for easy maintenance by estate workers and facilitates natural aeration of the soil."

"Open ditches?" Ashworth scoffed. "My dear Penhaligon, are we to return to the dark ages of drainage? Buried pipes are the mark of modern agriculture, preventing evaporation and maintaining aesthetic appeal."

"Aesthetic appeal is secondary to functionality in agricultural land, Mr. Ashworth," Arthur countered politely but firmly. "And as for evaporation, these ditches are designed for water removal, not retention. Moreover, the London plans did not account for the rapid clogging potential of the buried pipes given the specific silts and organic matter present in these fields. Open ditches, as Mr. Briggs has demonstrated over decades, are easily cleaned and maintainable."

He continued, "We also propose the strategic planting of native water absorbing vegetation along ditch banks and in marshier areas. Plants such as certain sedges and rushes have extensive root systems that bind soil, prevent erosion, and actively absorb excess moisture, contributing to a healthier ecosystem. All materials,  stone for culvert lining, timber for small sluices,  can be sourced from the estate itself, or locally, dramatically reducing material costs and transport overheads."

Arthur then presented a comparative cost analysis. "My lord, the London plan, as estimated, would cost approximately two thousand pounds for materials and external labour, with an estimated completion time of nine to twelve months due to specialized equipment and contractor scheduling. Our alternative, utilising estate labour and locally sourced materials, is estimated at no more than four hundred pounds, with a projected completion time of four to six months, and continuous maintenance can be integrated into the existing estate work schedule."

Lord Harrington had been listening intently, occasionally stroking his chin. He picked up Arthur's report, thumbing through the pages of sketches and calculations. Ashworth, meanwhile, was visibly seething, his face a tight mask of indignation.

"Four hundred pounds versus two thousand," Lord Harrington murmured, his eyes fixed on the figures. "A considerable difference, Penhaligon. And you are confident in these estimates?"

"Absolutely, my lord," Arthur affirmed. "Mr. Briggs assisted directly with the material sourcing and labour hour estimations. He has a precise understanding of what is required and what the estate workforce is capable of achieving."

"But these are mere ditches, my lord," Ashworth interjected, his voice strained. "Common, unsophisticated work. One cannot expect modern results from such antiquated methods. The metropolitan engineers employed by myself have studied at the finest institutions. Their methods are scientifically proven, not just the ramblings of an old foreman."

"Mr. Ashworth," Lord Harrington said, his voice quiet but firm, "common sense, when applied to the land, often proves more reliable than abstract theory. And Mr. Briggs's 'ramblings,' as you call them, have sustained this estate for generations. Penhaligon's report is not merely a summary of Mr. Briggs's views, but a detailed engineering proposal that integrates practical wisdom with scientific principles. He has provided objective analysis, complete with verifiable costings and environmental benefits."

He looked from Ashworth to Arthur, then back to the report. "Penhaligon, your proposal is compelling. The cost savings alone are significant, but your emphasis on sustainability and working with the land's natural features is particularly astute. This aligns with my own long-term vision for the estate. I confess, the London plans always struck me as rather... grand, for simple drainage."

Ashworth spluttered, "But my lord, I commissioned those plans in good faith! They represent the cutting edge of agricultural innovation!"

"And I appreciate your diligence, Ashworth," Lord Harrington said, a slight edge now in his voice. "But innovation must be appropriate. A state of the art plough is useless if it cannot turn our soil. Penhaligon, I want you to proceed with your plan for the western fields. Work closely with Mr. Briggs. I want to see this implemented without delay."

Arthur bowed his head. "As you wish, my lord. I will oversee it personally."

"And Ashworth," Lord Harrington continued, turning to his steward, "while I value your initiatives, I expect you to respect my decisions. This is not a slight against you, but a practical choice for the good of the estate."

Ashworth, though outwardly composed, emanated a furious energy. "Of course, my lord. Your will is paramount." But his eyes, as they flickered to Arthur, held a chilling promise of future retribution. Arthur sensed it, a cold prickle on his skin. He had won this skirmish, but the war for Lord Harrington's trust and the estate's future was far from over.

The next Monday morning, the western fields buzzed with activity. Under Arthur's quiet direction and Briggs's experienced supervision, estate workers began the task of clearing and deepening the natural watercourses. Arthur was present every day, often alongside Briggs, directing the placement of stones, checking gradients with his surveying instruments, and explaining the purpose of each step to the men. He found a deep satisfaction in the physical work, in seeing his plans translate directly into tangible progress.

The estate workers, initially cautious of the new 'gentleman engineer,' quickly came to respect Arthur's hands on approach and his genuine interest in their work. He listened to their suggestions, incorporated their knowledge of specific tricky spots, and even shared a flask of tea with them during breaks. Briggs, in particular, flourished under Arthur's trust. His gruff exterior softened, and he took immense pride in seeing his lifelong observations finally put into practice with the full backing of the estate.

"This is how it should always have been," Briggs grumbled contentedly one afternoon, watching a line of men carefully shaping a ditch bank. "Working with the land, not fighting it. That Master Ashworth, he'd have had us digging trenches deep

and blast great big channels through solid rock, not caring a whit for the spring lines or the flow of the land." He spat a stream of tobacco juice precisely at a small, glistening beetle. "Never mind the expense or the waste. Just brute force, that was his way."

Arthur nodded, watching the men. "He had a different vision, Mr. Briggs. One rooted in efficiency of a different kind. He saw the land as something to be conquered, reshaped to fit an ideal of production."

Briggs snorted. "And ideal built in an office, mind you, not out here where the mud sucks at your boots and the wind bites at your ears. This land, she has her own will. You listen to her, she'll give you plenty. You fight her, she'll fight you back, and she'll always win in the end. Look at these channels. We're not digging new ones. We're just remembering the old ones, finding where the water always wanted to go, and helping it on its way. Clearing the choked arteries, you might say."

Arthur had indeed observed this. Briggs possessed an almost uncanny ability to read the terrain. He would walk slowly, pausing, sometimes kneeling to feel the soil, sometimes tracing a line in the air with his gnarled finger, explaining to Arthur how a subtle dip indicated an ancient watercourse, how a change in vegetation marked a spring, how the 'ribs of the land,' as he called the underlying geological formations, dictated the natural flow. Arthur, with his surveying instruments, would then verify Briggs's instincts, often finding them remarkably accurate. His scientific training had given him precision, but Briggs offered intuition born of a lifetime's observation. The synergy was powerful.

The estate workers, initially suspicious of Penhaligon and his strange metal instruments, had indeed warmed to him. He was a gentleman, yes, but he worked harder than many of them, often foregoing lunch to pore over his plans in the field, or to lend a hand with a particularly heavy stone. He treated them with respect, asking their opinions, listening to their insights, and often incorporating their practical suggestions into the plan. He learned their names, their families, and their particular strengths. When young Thomas, usually nimble as a goat, twisted an ankle on a slippery bank, Arthur was the first to offer aid, helping him back to the estate house and arranging for the doctor. Such actions fostered a quiet loyalty, a sense that Penhaligon was truly on their side.

Ashworth, however, was not idle. His presence in the western fields became a rare and unsettling event. He would ride through, typically in the late afternoon, his eyes scanning the workers, his gaze lingering on Arthur. He never dismounted, never spoke directly to Penhaligon or Briggs, but his silence was more eloquent than any criticism. He seemed to be collecting data, cataloging perceived inefficiencies, perhaps, or simply reinforcing his own authority by making his supervisory rounds. Arthur felt his gaze like a cold breath on his neck.

The official reports Ashworth submitted to Lord Harrington, however, were another matter. While outwardly complying with his Lordship's directive, they were subtly laced with misgivings. "Work on the western fields proceeds, my Lord, under Mr. Penhaligon's direction. While progress is steady, the methods employed are unconventional and appear to be significantly more labor intensive than the proposals submitted by Messrs. Davies and Sons. I have noted a considerable expenditure on sundry local materials, such as river stones and timber for bank reinforcement, rather than the more uniform and predictable manufactured conduits. While Mr. Penhaligon assures me these are cost effective in the long run, their immediate impact on the estate's quarterly expenditure will require careful scrutiny."

Lord Harrington, however, was not easily swayed. He had made his decision, and he had learned to trust Arthur's judgment. He would often ride out to the western fields himself, usually unannounced. He preferred to observe, to listen, to feel the pulse of his land and his people. He saw Arthur knee deep in mud, discussing the angle of a weir with Briggs. He saw the workers, not merely toiling, but engaged, taking pride in their craft. He noted the natural beauty of the slowly emerging system, the way the water flowed smoothly, gently, almost imperceptibly, rather than being forced down straight, rigid lines. He observed the renewed growth of marsh grass and reeds in areas that had been barren, the return of waterfowl to newly formed pools. These subtle signs pleased him far more than any perfectly symmetrical, geometrically precise channel proposed by the London engineers.

One blustry Tuesday, a week after a particularly heavy rain, Lord Harrington found Arthur and Briggs surveying a section where a newly cleared channel had unexpectedly eroded a small bank. The ground had been softer than anticipated, a pocket of unstable clay hidden beneath a thin layer of topsoil.

"A minor setback, my Lord," Arthur explained, pointing to his survey notes. "We've found a particularly recalcitrant seam of clay here. The initial flow after the downpour was a little too vigorous for this section. We'll need to reinforce this bank more substantially than originally planned. Mr. Briggs suggests a 'living wall' of willow cuttings, interwoven with larger stones. It will take time to establish, but it will be stronger in the long run, and attract beneficial insects."

Briggs, wiping mud from his hands, added, "Aye, my Lord. Clay's a tricky mistress. You think you know her, then she shifts on you. But the willows, they'll root deep. They'll bind her up proper. And they won't cost much more than the labor to put them in, seeing as we've got plenty of cuttings on the estate."

Lord Harrington looked at the small, slumped section of bank. "And how would the London engineers have handled this, do you think, Ashworth?" he asked, not to Arthur or Briggs, but to the steward who had, coincidentally or not, appeared on horseback behind them, his face impassive.

Ashworth cleared his throat. "My Lord, Messrs. Davies and Sons would undoubtedly have specified a concrete retaining wall, or perhaps a series of galvanized steel pilings. A more permanent, though admittedly more costly, solution. It would prevent any future erosion of this nature."

"Permanent, perhaps," Lord Harrington mused, looking from Ashworth to the willow cuttings Briggs held in his hand, "but would it be... natural? Would it adapt? Or would it simply be a rigid barrier against the land's will?" He turned back to Arthur and Briggs. "Proceed with the willows, Mr. Penhaligon. And thank you, Mr. Briggs, for your counsel. I trust your judgment in these matters."

Ashworth's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly, but he remained silent. As Lord Harrington rode off, a faint smile played on Arthur's lips. Ashworth's eyes, however, held a deeper, colder glint. The 'minor setback' with the clay bank, Arthur suspected, would not be forgotten by the steward.

Indeed, Ashworth began a campaign of insidious undermining. Supplies ordered for the western fields would be delayed, or incorrect quantities would arrive. Tools would go missing overnight. When Arthur requested more workers for a particularly demanding section, Ashworth would assign them to other, less urgent tasks across the estate, claiming unforeseen emergencies or manpower shortages.

"My Lord, the blacksmith is overwhelmed with repairs to the estate carts," Ashworth would report to Lord Harrington, "and the gardeners require additional hands to prepare for the spring planting. Mr. Penhaligon's drainage work, while important, cannot entirely monopolize the estate's resources."

Lord Harrington, while aware of Ashworth's machinations, was also pragmatic. He didn't want to create open dissent among his staff. He allowed Ashworth some leeway, but he also made it clear that the western fields were a priority. "Ashworth, you must find a way to balance these demands. Penhaligon's project is essential. The productivity of those fields is paramount. Perhaps less urgent tasks can be delayed, or contractors brought in for the less skilled labor elsewhere."

Ashworth, feigning dutiful compliance, would then ensure the *most* difficult or troublesome estate workers were assigned to the western fields, hoping to sow discord or slow progress. He selected men known for their grumbling, their inefficiency, or their penchant for drink.

Arthur, however, met these challenges with quiet resilience. He understood that these were tests, not just of his engineering skill, but of his leadership. When supplies were delayed, he and Briggs improvised, using natural materials from the estate, proving the resilience of their "working with the land" philosophy. When tools went missing, he organized the men to create a secure storage area, and made an open appeal for honesty, backed by a quiet promise to Lord Harrington about strict accounting for estate property. And when the difficult workers arrived, Arthur treated them with the same respect as anyone else. He gave them clear instructions, listened to their complaints without judgment, and subtly rearranged tasks to suit their individual strengths, or to isolate the most disruptive elements. He found that a few kind words, a shared flask of tea, and a genuine interest in their well being could often disarm even the most cantankerous individual.

One such worker was Silas, a burly man known for his perpetual frown and his tendency to disappear for 'breaks' that stretched into hours. Ashworth had practically handpicked him for Arthur's crew. One afternoon, Arthur found Silas struggling with a particularly large stone. He had been trying to move it with a crowbar, but the ground was soft, and the stone kept slipping.

Arthur watched for a moment, then walked over. "That's a stubborn one, Silas," he observed, not critically, but with genuine understanding.

Silas grunted, wiping sweat from his brow. "Aye. Feels like it's grown roots."

"Perhaps if we tried a different approach," Arthur suggested. "What if we dug a small trench on the far side, and then used a lever from this angle, rolling it into the trench, then back out?" He sketched a quick diagram in the mud with a stick. "It would reduce the friction and give us better leverage."

Silas looked at the drawing, then at Arthur, a flicker of surprise in his eyes. He had expected an order, or a reprimand. "Could work," he conceded, grudgingly.

Arthur picked up a spade. "Here, let's try it together."

They worked for ten minutes, digging the small trench, then Arthur positioned the lever carefully, and with a coordinated heave, they managed to shift the stone into place.

Silas straightened up, a rare, small smile playing on his lips. "Well, I'll be. That was quicker than breaking my back for another hour."

"Teamwork, Silas," Arthur said with a smile. "And a bit of applied physics."

From that day forward, Silas remained gruff, but he was fiercely loyal to Arthur. He worked diligently, and his 'breaks' became shorter and less frequent. He even began to offer his own practical suggestions, which Arthur always considered thoughtfully. Ashworth's plan to sow discord had backfired.

The weeks stretched into months. Autumn gave way to a particularly mild winter, which was ideal for continuing the heavy earthworks. The western fields, once a waterlogged expanse, began to show the distinct pattern of the new drainage system. The main channels, artfully disguised as natural watercourses, wound their way through the landscape, fed by smaller feeder ditches and a network of underground French drains. Stone weirs regulated the flow, preventing erosion and creating small, clear pools that would attract wildlife. The banks were reinforced with a combination of willow and alder cuttings, their nascent roots already beginning to stabilize the soil.

Lord Harrington, seeing the tangible results, was increasingly impressed. He saw a transformation happening, not just in the fields, but in the morale of his estate workers. He saw Arthur Penhaligon not just as an engineer, but as a leader, a man who understood both the science of the land and the psychology of its people.

"Penhaligon," Lord Harrington said one crisp December morning, as they stood overlooking a particularly well-drained section, the ground firm underfoot, "this is truly remarkable. The change is... palpable. Even the air feels drier, healthier. I confess, I had my doubts about the practicality of Mr. Briggs's methods, despite my respect for his local knowledge. But you, you have given them form and structure. You have made them real."

Arthur felt a surge of pride, but he deflected the praise. "It is Mr. Briggs's wisdom, my Lord, and the dedication of your estate workers. I merely helped to interpret and organize their efforts."

"Modesty becomes you," Lord Harrington replied, a twinkle in his eye, "but I am not blind. You have brought a quiet revolution to this part of the estate. And what of the costings, Ashworth?" he asked, turning to his steward, who had again joined them, as if by happenstance.

Ashworth produced a ledger. "My Lord, as per your instruction, I have kept meticulous records. The initial outlay for labor and local materials has been substantial, perhaps exceeding what was initially projected for the first phase. However," he continued, carefully choosing his words, "Mr. Penhaligon has demonstrated a commendable ability to utilize existing estate resources and to innovate with readily available materials, thereby offsetting some of the expenditure that would have been incurred through external contractors." He paused, then added, "The long term benefits, should the system prove entirely effective, would certainly justify the investment."

Lord Harrington looked keenly at Ashworth. "Your qualification is noted, steward. But I believe 'should prove entirely effective' is perhaps a trifle pessimistic given what we see before us. The fields are visibly improved. The water table is dropping. And we have not yet even begun to cultivate this section properly. What do you project for the coming spring, Penhaligon?"

Arthur's face lit up. "My Lord, with these western fields properly drained, we can begin rotation crops that were previously impossible. Wheat, oats, even some experimental barley. The soil, once it dries and has been worked, will be rich and fertile. I anticipate a significant increase in yield, perhaps twenty to thirty percent above previous estimates for a normal year, assuming favorable weather. And the reduced risk of crop failure due to waterlogging will provide a stability the estate has sorely needed."

Lord Harrington smiled broadly. "Excellent. Truly excellent. Ashworth, I want you to begin preparations for the spring planting in these newly reclaimed fields. Consult with Mr. Penhaligon on suitable crops and cultivation methods. And I want weekly reports on progress, not just on expenditure, but on the practical improvements."

Ashworth bowed stiffly. "As you wish, my Lord." His outward composure was impeccable, but Arthur caught the brief, almost imperceptible tightening around his eyes. Ashworth was losing ground.

The spring arrived, and with it, the planting of the western fields became a triumphant testament to Arthur's and Briggs's vision. The soil, once squelching and infertile, was now workable, dark, and rich. Tractors, acquired by Ashworth for the London plan and now repurposed, moved across the land, breaking ground that had not seen a plough in generations. The seeds went in, and with timely spring rains, they germinated quickly, sending up verdant shoots. The sight of healthy, young crops stretching across the landscape where only mud and reeds had been was a powerful vindication.

But Ashworth, while forced to participate, had not abandoned his quiet war. If he could not derail the project, he would undermine Arthur's position in other ways. He began to spread rumors among the estate staff and even among the local tradesmen who frequented the estate. Whispers of Penhaligon's 'extravagance' with Lord Harrington's money, of his 'unconventional' and 'untested' methods, of his 'youthful arrogance' began to circulate. He hinted that Penhaligon was merely a temporary diversion, that the Lord would soon return to 'proper' engineering once the novelty wore off.

Arthur heard these whispers, of course. The estate was a small world, and gossip traveled fast. But he chose to ignore them, focusing on his work. Briggs, however, was less patient.

"That slippery eel Ashworth," Briggs grumbled one evening, as he and Arthur shared a pint at the local inn. "He's telling folk you're a fly by night, that you're just using His Lordship's purse to play at being an engineer. Heard it myself down at the market."

"Let him talk, Mr. Briggs," Arthur said calmly, sipping his ale. "The fields will speak for themselves."

"Aye, but words can poison a well, even a clear one," Briggs retorted, thumping his mug on the table. "He's trying to sour the Lord's opinion of you, trying to make you seem untrustworthy."

Briggs was right. Ashworth's campaign escalated. He started subtly misrepresenting Arthur's requests to Lord Harrington, making them seem more demanding or costly than they were. He would 'forget' to relay messages, or 'misplace' important documents related to the western fields. He even began to subtly question Arthur's qualifications, suggesting to Lord Harrington that Penhaligon's 'youthful enthusiasm' might sometimes override 'prudent caution.'

The situation came to a head during a planning meeting for the expansion of the drainage system to the north fields, an area even more notoriously waterlogged than the western fields had been. Lord Harrington, Arthur, and Ashworth were seated in the Lord's study, maps spread across the large oak table.

"So, Penhaligon," Lord Harrington began, "having seen the success in the west, I am eager to apply your methods to the north. What are your initial thoughts?"

Arthur, prepared, laid out a detailed preliminary plan, drawing upon Briggs's observations and his own surveys. "The north fields present a greater challenge, my Lord. The underlying geology is more complex, with several significant underground springs and a deeper clay pan. We will need to be particularly careful with establishing the primary outflow channels. I propose a phased approach, beginning with a detailed hydrological survey, followed by selective excavation and the careful establishment of a larger main culvert, probably lined with local stone, to handle the increased water volume."

"And the cost implications of this 'phased approach' and 'detailed survey'?" Ashworth interjected smoothly, before Lord Harrington could respond. "I assume Mr. Penhaligon intends to engage external specialists for this 'hydrological survey,' which would, of course, add considerable expense."

Arthur turned to Ashworth, his voice level. "No, Mr. Ashworth. I intend to conduct the hydrological survey myself, with Mr. Briggs assisting. We have the instruments, and more importantly, Mr. Briggs possesses an unparalleled understanding of the estate's water sources. As for the stone culvert, we can use estate stone, cut and dressed by our own stonemasons, keeping costs localized and controlled. The phased approach is designed to minimize risk and expense by allowing us to adapt as we uncover the land's specific challenges, rather than committing to a rigid, costly design upfront."

Ashworth smiled thinly. "Commendable thrift, Mr. Penhaligon. However, the initial report I received from you for the western fields also suggested 'local stone and estate resources.' Yet, the actual expenditure, when all sundry materials and labor adjustments are accounted for, far exceeded your initial estimates. I am simply ensuring that His Lordship is fully apprised of potential cost overruns for this even larger undertaking." He glanced at Lord Harrington, a picture of diligent concern.

Lord Harrington leaned back in his chair, his gaze shifting between the two men. "Ashworth, are you suggesting Mr. Penhaligon's estimates are deliberately misleading?"

Ashworth blanched slightly. "No, my Lord, not at all. Merely, perhaps, a degree of... youthful optimism regarding the complexities of such a project. As steward, it is my duty to present the full financial picture, however unfavorable it may appear."

"Unfavorable?" Arthur's calm façade finally cracked, a flash of indignation in his eyes. "The western fields are now producing crops at levels not seen in decades, Mr. Ashworth. The cost of drainage, while substantial, is dwarfed by the potential for increased revenue and the long term health of the estate. Your 'financial picture' omits the very real, very tangible benefits that are already being realized."

"And what of the London plans, Mr. Penhaligon?" Ashworth pressed, sensing a weakness, perhaps, in Arthur's sudden passion. "Those plans, dismissed so readily, offered a fixed price, a guaranteed outcome, and a modern, efficient system. Your methods, while perhaps picturesque, are entirely reliant on improvisation and the subjective 'wisdom' of an unlettered foreman. Where is the guarantee of success there? Where is the accountability?"

"The guarantee, Mr. Ashworth," Arthur said, his voice now dangerously low, "is in the soil itself. It is in the crops that stand green and tall in the western fields. It is in the fact that we work *with* the land, not against it. Your London plans offered a fixed price for a fixed *idea*, an idea that failed to understand this specific land. My 'improvisation,' as you call it, is simply intelligent adaptation to reality, something your rigid designs could never accommodate."

Lord Harrington held up a hand. "Enough. Ashworth, your concern for the estate's finances is appreciated. However, your consistent undermining of Mr. Penhaligon's methods, despite the clear evidence of their success, is becoming... counterproductive. The 'cost overruns' you speak of are an investment, not a loss. An investment that is already paying dividends. The very nature of this project, as Mr. Penhaligon rightly points out, requires a flexible approach. To demand a fixed price for a truly adaptive solution is to misunderstand its fundamental nature."

He turned to Arthur. "Penhaligon, your plan for the north fields sounds sound. Proceed with your hydrological survey. Use Mr. Briggs. And continue to engage the estate stonemasons. I trust your judgment. But," Lord Harrington added, his gaze firm, "I also expect thorough and transparent accounting from you. Provide Ashworth with detailed breakdowns of all costs, and justify any deviations from your initial projections. I will review these personally."

"Of course, my Lord," Arthur replied, a profound sense of relief washing over him. Lord Harrington was giving him his trust, but also holding him to account. It was a fair challenge.

Ashworth, for his part, managed a small, tight smile. "Very well, my Lord. I shall ensure Mr. Penhaligon's accounts are rigorously scrutinized. For the good of the estate." The words were outwardly compliant, but the implication was clear: he would find something. He would find a way to discredit Arthur.

As Arthur left the study, the heavy oak door closing behind him, he felt the weight of Ashworth's promise. The battle for the north fields would be even fiercer than the one for the west. Ashworth would be looking for any flaw, any misstep, any perceived extravagance. Arthur knew he would have to be meticulous, not just in his engineering, but in his administration. He would need to be beyond reproach.

The next morning, Arthur informed Briggs of the new commission. Briggs's eyes gleamed with an almost childlike excitement. "The north fields? Now that's a proper challenge, that is! Tricky ground. But she's got good bones, that land. Just needs a bit of coaxing."

Arthur smiled. "And we'll coax her, Mr. Briggs. Together. But we'll have to be even more careful with our figures this time. Ashworth will be watching our every penny."

"Let him watch," Briggs scoffed. "He'll not find us wasting anything. We work with what the land provides. That's always been the cheapest, best way."

And so, the work on the north fields began. Arthur and Briggs spent weeks immersed in the terrain, often from dawn until dusk, meticulously mapping every undulation, every spring, every subtle change in soil composition. Arthur refined his surveying techniques, integrating Briggs's intuitive observations with precise measurements. He created detailed topographical maps that showed not just the surface, but the suspected underground water flows, the 'arteries and veins' of Briggs's analogy.

They discovered several previously undocumented springs, hidden by dense undergrowth and years of neglect. These discoveries were crucial, as they would dictate the placement of the main culverts and the strategy for managing the water table. Arthur spent hours in the estate archives, cross referencing old maps and property deeds, tracing the history of water management on the estate, finding forgotten references to 'boggy ground' and 'perennial springs' that confirmed Briggs's intuitive understanding.

Ashworth, true to his word, became a constant, albeit distant, presence. His assistant, a nervous young man named Davies (no relation to the London firm), would appear almost daily, notebook in hand, to ask detailed questions about expenditures, labor assignments, and material acquisition. Arthur answered every query patiently and thoroughly, providing copies of receipts, detailed work logs, and precise explanations for every decision. He even invited Davies to observe the survey work, explaining the instruments and the reasoning behind each measurement. Davies, initially suspicious, seemed genuinely interested, perhaps even a little intimidated by Arthur's thoroughness.

The actual excavation work on the north fields was even more challenging than the western. The clay was deeper, the springs more powerful, and the terrain more uneven. But the experience gained in the western fields, combined with the meticulous planning, allowed them to proceed with confidence. Arthur, now thoroughly adept at managing the estate workers, divided them into smaller, specialized teams: one for clearing, one for excavation, one for stone cutting and placement, and a smaller, highly skilled team for fine grading and shaping the channels.

One particularly cold morning, during a site visit, Lord Harrington observed Arthur directing the placement of a massive capstone over a newly constructed culvert. The work was precise, slow, and laborious, but Arthur was there in the thick of it, coat stained with mud, shouting instructions, and occasionally lending his own considerable strength to guide a rope. Briggs stood beside him, watching with a critical, but ultimately approving, eye.

Ashworth, arriving on horseback, watched the scene with a familiar, detached air.

Lord Harrington turned to his steward. "Ashworth, tell me honestly. Do you still believe a concrete conduit would have been a superior solution here?"

Ashworth hesitated, glancing at the robust, naturally integrated stone culvert. "My Lord, the cost of labor for cutting and placing such large stones is considerable. A prefabricated concrete section would have been installed with significantly less manpower, and thus, potentially, less overall expense."

"Potentially," Lord Harrington repeated, "and potentially, it would crack under the shifting ground, or fail to blend with the landscape, or simply prove insufficient for the specific flow. What of the longevity, Ashworth? What of the aesthetic? What of the pride the men take in building something so enduring with their own hands?" He then gestured towards Arthur. "Look at Penhaligon. He's not just overseeing a project; he's part of it. He understands the effort, the skill, the very human element involved. That, Ashworth, is something no London firm could ever put a price on."

Ashworth, for once, had no immediate retort. He merely inclined his head, his face a mask. But the intensity of his gaze on Arthur, as if committing every detail to memory for future use, was not lost on Lord Harrington. The Lord understood that Ashworth's opposition was not purely logical or financial; it was deeply personal, a challenge to his authority, a slight to his chosen methods.

By late spring, the north fields, though not yet fully drained, were already showing significant improvement. The main culverts were established, channeling the spring water efficiently towards the lower reaches of the estate. Smaller feeder ditches were being dug, and the ground was slowly, steadily drying out. The initial planting of experimental cover crops in the drier sections was a promising sight. The promise of fertile land, once a distant dream, was becoming a reality.

Arthur's reputation among the estate workers and the local community had grown immensely. He was no longer just the 'gentleman engineer' but a respected figure, a man who understood the land and its people. He had brought hope, prosperity, and a renewed sense of purpose to the estate.

Ashworth, however, was not one to admit defeat easily. His final, desperate gamble came in the form of a complaint to Lord Harrington from a powerful neighboring landowner, Sir Reginald Thorne. Sir Reginald was a notoriously litigious man, always quick to take offense and quicker to pursue perceived grievances.

The complaint was meticulously crafted: the new drainage system on Lord Harrington's estate, particularly the main culvert in the north fields, was, Sir Reginald alleged, causing increased flooding on his own downstream property. The complaint cited an 'unnatural alteration of water flow' and demanded immediate cessation of the work, financial compensation for damage, and a complete re-evaluation by 'competent, independent engineers.'

Lord Harrington was furious. Such a complaint, if pursued, could be ruinous, both financially and in terms of his reputation. He summoned Arthur and Ashworth to his study, Sir Reginald's official letter lying ominously on the desk.

"Penhaligon," Lord Harrington said, his voice taut with suppressed anger, "Sir Reginald claims your new culvert is diverting water onto his land, causing inundation. Is this true?"

Arthur felt a cold dread, but he met Lord Harrington's gaze squarely. "No, my Lord. It is not possible. Our system is designed to channel water efficiently, but always within the existing natural watershed. We have taken great care not to alter the overall flow into any neighboring properties. In fact, by reducing stagnant pools and regulating flow, our system should alleviate downstream flooding, not worsen it."

Ashworth, standing to one side, spoke up. "My Lord, perhaps Mr. Penhaligon, in his admirable zeal, overlooked some of the broader topographical implications. The London engineers' plans, while costly, did include detailed hydrological surveys of the entire regional watershed, precisely to avoid such external complications."

Lord Harrington glared at Ashworth. "Your hindsight is impeccable, Ashworth. But the question remains, Penhaligon. Can you definitively refute this claim?"

"Yes, my Lord," Arthur said, his mind racing. "I can. I have detailed topographical maps and flow calculations. Moreover, Mr. Briggs and I have conducted extensive observations of the regional water patterns for months. We have even noted Sir Reginald's own rather haphazard drainage methods on his property, which often create localized flooding on his side of the boundary. It is more likely his own mismanagement, combined with recent heavy rains, that is causing his problem, and he is attempting to lay the blame elsewhere."

"Assertions are not enough, Penhaligon," Lord Harrington said, though a flicker of hope now appeared in his eyes. "Sir Reginald has threatened legal action. I need irrefutable proof."

"My Lord, I propose a joint inspection," Arthur said, an idea forming in his mind. "We invite Sir Reginald and his own engineers to tour our north fields and then his own property, with myself and Mr. Briggs present. I will present our data, demonstrate our system, and then we can examine his property together. The truth, my Lord, will be self evident to any competent engineer who is willing to look without prejudice."

Lord Harrington pondered this. "A bold move. But if you are as certain as you sound, it might be our best course. Ashworth, arrange for this inspection. Invite Sir Reginald and his people for next Tuesday. Ensure Mr. Penhaligon has all his documentation ready. And make sure Briggs is available."

Ashworth's face was unreadable, but Arthur felt the tension emanating from him. This was Ashworth's final gamble, and if Arthur could prove Sir Reginald wrong, it would be a devastating defeat for the steward.

The following Tuesday dawned clear and bright, a perfect day for an inspection. Sir Reginald Thorne arrived in a fine carriage, accompanied by two stern looking gentlemen in impeccably tailored suits, presumably his 'competent, independent engineers.' Ashworth met them at the estate house, his demeanor one of polite, almost deferential, concern.

Lord Harrington greeted Sir Reginald coolly, his displeasure evident. Arthur and Briggs, dressed in practical field clothes, stood to one side, all their maps, instruments, and records neatly prepared.

The tour began. Arthur calmly and methodically led the party through the north fields. He explained the principles of the drainage system, demonstrated the gentle flow of water through the culverts, pointed out the new growth and the drying soil. He showed them his detailed maps, explaining the watershed and how the new system respected the natural contours of the land, rather than aggressively altering them. Briggs, when asked, added his own observations, his weathered hand tracing lines on the map, his voice quiet but authoritative.

Sir Reginald's engineers, initially skeptical, began to nod slowly as Arthur presented his irrefutable evidence. His calculations of water volume, flow rates, and diversion points were precise. His topographical maps, showing every contour and boundary, clearly indicated that no water was being diverted from Lord Harrington's natural watershed onto Sir Reginald's property. Indeed, the regulated flow seemed to reduce surges that might previously have affected the lower lying areas.

After an hour on Lord Harrington's estate, the party moved to Sir Reginald's adjoining property. There, the truth became painfully clear. Sir Reginald's own drainage system was a chaotic mess of poorly maintained ditches, many of them choked with weeds, some leading nowhere, others simply dumping water directly into low-lying areas. Several of his own natural springs were completely untended, their outflow creating stagnant pools. His 'flooding' was clearly a localized problem, exacerbated by his own negligence, rather than anything caused by Lord Harrington's careful, well-engineered system.

One of Sir Reginald's own engineers, a portly man with a thick moustache, finally spoke, after carefully examining Arthur's maps and comparing them to the reality on the ground. "Sir Reginald," he said, his voice heavy with professional embarrassment, "Mr. Penhaligon's analysis appears to be entirely accurate.
