I began, "Thorne, this prolonged inaction offers but a clear path for the culprit to secure his escape." My companion, a man whose mind was a labyrinth of deductive pathways, paced the small, cluttered study, his hands clasped behind his back, his sharp gaze fixed upon the rain-streaked window overlooking the dreary North Sea. His gaunt features, etched by countless late-night vigils, betrayed a momentary hesitation.

"Escape, my dear Finch, is a relative term," Thorne replied, his voice a low rumble. He stopped abruptly, turning to face Blackwood and Miller, who sat stiffly on the edge of their chairs. "You inquire if I possess the knowledge of the perpetrator’s identity. Indeed, I do. Yet, merely knowing a name is a triviality when compared with the intricate task of apprehending the individual. This, I anticipate, will transpire within the hour. My own carefully laid arrangements are nearing fruition; however, it demands the utmost subtlety, for we confront a mind as devious as it is desperate, a man who, as my observations confirm, enjoys the covert assistance of an equally cunning confederate. So long as this man remains oblivious to the net tightening around him, our chances of success are considerable; but should the slightest suspicion dawn upon him, he would dissolve into the anonymity of the coastal fog, vanishing utterly from our grasp. I mean no disrespect to the admirable constabulary, but I am compelled to admit that I consider these two men to be far superior in craft to the official force, hence my decision to proceed independently. Should my venture falter, I shall, of course, bear the full weight of your condemnation; a burden for which I am quite prepared. Be assured, gentlemen, the moment my operations allow for communication without jeopardising their delicate balance, I shall impart every detail."

Inspector Blackwood, a burly man whose face was the colour of boiled ham, flushed crimson, his thick eyebrows bristling. Sergeant Miller, a younger man with earnest, bewildered eyes, fidgeted with the brim of his helmet, a silent resentment simmering beneath his obedient demeanour. Before either could voice their indignation, a faint scratching emanated from the study door, followed by a tentative knock.

"Enter," Thorne called, his expression unchanging.

The door creaked open to admit a small, wiry boy, Thomas, a local urchin Thorne occasionally employed for errands. His clothes were damp, and his hair, plastered to his forehead, dripped onto the worn rug. "Please, sir," he stammered, pulling a grimy cap from his head, "the fisherman, old Jenson, he's at the back gate, just like you said, sir."

"Excellent, Thomas," Thorne murmured, a hint of satisfaction in his tone. He reached into a drawer of his desk, producing a pair of polished brass manacles. "A fine piece of engineering, these. Observe the mechanism, Inspector. The clasps engage with such precision, do they not?"

"The standard irons serve their purpose well enough," Blackwood grumbled, recovering his voice, "provided we can find the rascal to fasten them upon."

"Quite so, Inspector, quite so," Thorne replied, a faint smile playing upon his lips. "Perhaps Jenson might assist me with the crate of geological samples. Pray ask him to step

him forward, Thomas. Tell him to wait in the scullery until I send for him."

Thomas, relieved to be dismissed, scurried out, his small, clattering footsteps echoing down the narrow passage. Inspector Blackwood shifted uneasily, his gaze flicking from Thorne to the polished brass manacles, then back to Thorne's impassive face. "With all due respect, Mr. Thorne," Blackwood began, his voice rumbling like distant thunder, "we are police officers. Our duty is to apprehend criminals. This clandestine manoeuvring, this reliance on... on urchins and fishermen, it is quite irregular. And these manacles, sir. Do you propose to personally effect an arrest?"

Thorne turned the manacles over in his hand, a slight, almost imperceptible smile playing on his lips. "Inspector, you are quite right. Your duty is indeed to apprehend criminals. Mine, at this particular juncture, is to ensure that the opportunity for said apprehension arises. As for the methods, allow me to assure you that they are perfectly suited to the unique circumstances of this case. And these manacles, they are merely an object lesson, a reminder of the craftsmanship that can be found even in the tools of constraint. They are not for immediate application, I assure you, at least not in the manner you might conventionally envision." He placed the manacles carefully back into the drawer, securing it with a soft click. "Now, if you would both be so kind, please remain precisely where you are. Your presence here, immobile and observant, is precisely what is required of you for the next critical moments."

Before Blackwood could articulate the fresh wave of indignation that threatened to engulf him, Thorne moved with a sudden, fluid grace, passing through the study door and closing it gently behind him. The click of the latch seemed to resonate with a finality that left Blackwood and Miller staring at each other in bewildered silence.

"Well, I'll be damned," Blackwood muttered, rising slowly to his feet, his broad shoulders filling the small room. "Kept in the dark. By a... by a dilettante. A mere civilian, dabbling in matters of serious criminal import." He paced to the window, peering out at the driving rain, his breath fogging the glass. "What in blazes is he playing at, Miller? Geological samples? With a fisherman, and then he bolts out like a scalded cat!"

Miller, who had finally managed to remove his helmet and was now twisting it nervously in his hands, shook his head. "I don't know, Inspector. But he seemed very sure of himself. And that bit about us being required to be 'immobile and observant'... it sounds like he expects us to just sit here and do nothing." His young face was a picture of frustrated helplessness.

"Do nothing?" Blackwood snorted, turning from the window. "That's precisely what we'll *not* do. I'll not have some amateur making a mockery of the constabulary. He claims to know the identity of the perpetrator, claims he'll have him within the hour. And he's got his hands on a pair of regulation manacles. There's something fishy here, Miller, and it's not old Jenson's smell." He strode to the door, his hand hovering over the doorknob. "We'll give him a few minutes, let him think he's got us cowed. Then we'll take a look. See what this 'subtlety' entails."

Meanwhile, Thorne had descended the creaking back stairs, his steps light and silent. He found Jenson waiting patiently in the small, stone-flagged scullery, his oilskins dripping onto the floor. Jenson was a man carved from the very salt and wind of the North Sea; his face was a lattice of deep-set wrinkles, his eyes the colour of a stormy grey sky, and his hands, scarred and calloused, spoke of a lifetime battling the unforgiving waves. He held a small, crudely fashioned wooden box, no larger than a child's shoebox, clutched beneath one arm.

"Jenson, my dear fellow, thank you for your promptness," Thorne said, his voice quiet but firm. He gestured towards the box. "That is the item I requested, I trust?"

Jenson nodded, his gaze unwavering. "Aye, sir. Just as ye described. Found it tucked away under yon gorse bush, where the path bends by the old lighthouse keeper's cottage. Twas a tight fit, but it's here."

Thorne took the box, his fingers tracing the rough-hewn edges. It was remarkably light for its size. "Excellent. You have done admirably, Jenson. Now, the crate. It is a substantial item, and I require it to be moved with utmost discretion. It is in the small shed beyond the vegetable patch. It requires two men to lift comfortably, but I believe your considerable strength, combined with my own modest efforts, should suffice. We must bring it around to the front of the house, by the main gate. And it must appear as though we are genuinely struggling with its weight. Understand?"

Jenson's expression remained stoic. "Aye, sir. As ye say. The crate. And the 'struggle'. It's all part o' the play, eh?"

Thorne permitted himself a faint smile. "Precisely, Jenson. A performance for a most particular audience." He led the way out through the back door, into the biting wind and relentless rain. The small, unkempt garden was a sodden mess, the vegetable patch a dark, muddy expanse. The shed, a ramshackle structure leaning precariously, stood at the far end.

Inside, the crate sat in the gloom, exactly as Thorne had left it. It was a stout wooden box, perhaps three feet long, two feet wide, and two feet high, painted a nondescript dark grey. Heavy iron bands reinforced its corners, and a thick padlock secured the lid. It looked exactly like a shipping crate for scientific equipment, or perhaps, as Thorne had suggested to Blackwood, geological samples. However, Thorne knew the crate was empty save for a carefully placed layer of lead weights at the bottom, just enough to give it substantial heft without making it utterly unmanageable for two men, particularly one as robust as Jenson. The true contents, the "geological samples" that were to be moved, were contained within the small box Jenson had just delivered.

"Now, Jenson," Thorne instructed, rubbing his hands together. "One hand on each handle. We will lift it together. Remember, the appearance of strain is crucial. It must seem as if this is a considerable undertaking. We are moving something of immense value, something that demands careful handling and arduous effort."

They gripped the iron handles. On Thorne's count, they lifted. The crate, though not as heavy as a solid block of granite, still possessed a formidable inertia. Jenson grunted softly, his muscles flexing. Thorne, while possessing a wiry strength that belied his slender frame, genuinely strained, his breath coming in short puffs. They shuffled out of the shed, the rain immediately plastering their clothes to their bodies. The ground was treacherous, slick with mud and fallen leaves.

"Slowly, Jenson, slowly," Thorne murmured between clenched teeth. "Mind the puddles. We wouldn't want to damage our... samples."

They navigated the narrow path around the house, their progress slow and deliberate. Each step was a minor battle against the elements and the simulated burden. The wind whipped at them, threatening to wrench the crate from their grasp. Rain lashed down, chilling them to the bone. Thorne's hat, usually immaculately placed, was askew, water streaming from its brim.

As they neared the front of the house, a peculiar detail caught Thorne's eye. A faint sheen on the muddy ground near the hedge that bordered the main drive. It was almost imperceptible in the fading light and relentless rain, but Thorne's observational skills were unparalleled. A disturbance, a fresh indentation that hadn't been there earlier, partially obscured by a fallen branch. He made a mental note, filing it away for later analysis, without breaking stride or giving any indication of his discovery.

They finally reached the front gate, a heavy wrought-iron affair that usually stood open during the day but which Thorne had instructed Thomas to close earlier. It now provided a sturdy barrier.

"Rest it here, Jenson," Thorne commanded, his voice slightly hoarse. They lowered the crate with a theatrical groan, setting it down gently just inside the gate. Thorne wiped a hand across his wet face, pushing back a stray lock of hair. "Excellent. A truly Herculean effort, my friend. Now, listen carefully. I need you to remain here, out of sight as much as possible, behind this thick hedge. Should anyone approach, anyone at all, you are to give a single, sharp whistle. A common bird call will suffice, something unobtrusive. Do you understand?"

Jenson nodded, his eyes scanning the road beyond the gate. "Aye, sir. A bird call. And then what?"

"Then you are to remain silent and unseen. Your role is simply to provide the warning. Do not engage, do not reveal yourself. The success of our venture depends upon the absolute anonymity of your presence. Wait for my signal." Thorne looked at the small wooden box he still held. "This, my dear Jenson, is the true 'geological sample'. I shall now deposit it in the study. Your part, for the moment, is to observe and warn. And above all, to remain unseen."

With that, Thorne turned and re-entered the house, leaving Jenson to melt into the shadows cast by the overgrown hedge and the driving rain.

Back inside, the quiet of the house was punctuated only by the drumming of rain on the roof and the distant roar of the sea. Thorne walked purposefully back to his study, the small box held securely in his hand. As he reached the door, he heard the faint creak of a floorboard from within. Blackwood, it seemed, had not remained immobile for long.

Thorne pushed open the door. Blackwood stood by the large, mahogany desk, his back to the door, seemingly examining a collection of ancient maps pinned to the wall. Sergeant Miller was pretending to be deeply engrossed in a particularly dusty tome from Thorne's overflowing bookshelves. Both men straightened perceptibly as Thorne entered, an air of feigned nonchalance poorly concealing their surreptitious activities.

"Ah, gentlemen," Thorne said, his voice even, betraying no hint of his exertion or the brief encounter outside. "I trust you found your enforced contemplation enlightening?"

Blackwood turned, his expression a mixture of pique and frustration. "Mr. Thorne, we are not children to be confined to a room. And what was that performance outside? We heard grunting and heavy thuds. What 'geological samples' require such drama and secrecy?"

Thorne walked directly to his desk, carefully placing the small wooden box on its surface. "The drama, Inspector, is entirely a theatrical device, designed to draw attention away from the true operations at hand. The 'geological samples' are merely a cover. As for the secrecy, it is a necessary component of precision. I would not wish to compromise your integrity by involving you directly in an operation that might, at first glance, appear somewhat unorthodox to the public, or indeed, to your superiors."

He produced a small, intricate key from his waistcoat pocket and inserted it into the lock of the wooden box. With a soft click, he opened it. Blackwood and Miller, their curiosity momentarily overcoming their indignation, leaned forward.

Inside, nestled on a bed of dark velvet, lay not rocks or minerals, but a single, exquisitely crafted silver locket. It was antique, its surface tarnished with age, but its intricate filigree work still shone with an understated elegance. In its centre, a tiny, almost imperceptible engraving depicted a soaring falcon.

"A locket?" Blackwood boomed, his brows furrowed in confusion. "This is what all the fuss was about? A trinket?"

"A trinket, Inspector, that holds the key to a man's undoing," Thorne replied, his eyes fixed on the locket. "Or, more accurately, the key to bringing his accomplice into our net. This locket, you see, was found by Jenson, precisely where I instructed him to look. It was dropped, quite carelessly, by the confederate who aided our perpetrator in his escape from the cliff-top house last night. A confederate whose identity has been a source of considerable frustration to me, until now."

Miller, who had been studying the locket with earnest intensity, suddenly pointed a trembling finger. "Mr. Thorne, look closely. There's something engraved on the back."

Thorne picked up the locket with delicate fingers, turning it over. On its reverse side, etched in minute, almost microscopic script, were three initials: "E.V.W."

"E.V.W.," Thorne murmured, his voice barely above a whisper. "Indeed. The final piece of the puzzle. The initials of our elusive confederate." He placed the locket back in its box, closing the lid with a soft click. "And now, gentlemen, we wait. The stage is set. The bait has been offered. Our devious fish, I believe, is about to bite."

"Bait?" Blackwood exclaimed. "What bait? A locket? What does a locket have to do with anything?"

"Our perpetrator, Inspector, is not a sentimental man by nature, but he is exceedingly fond of his accomplice," Thorne explained, pacing slowly. "And his accomplice, a woman named Elara Vane-Whittaker, is exceedingly sentimental. This locket, you see, was a gift to her from him. A memento of their rather sordid affair. He would know, immediately, that its discovery could unravel everything. Its loss would be a grave concern to him, far more so than its intrinsic value. And he would immediately seek to recover it, or at the very least, assess the damage its loss might cause."

"How would he know it's here?" Miller asked, his eyes wide.

Thorne permitted himself a thin smile. "Ah, Sergeant, that is where the 'geological samples' and the struggling fisherman come into play. The sight of Jenson and myself laboriously moving a crate, particularly a sealed and evidently valuable one, past the main gate, was not merely for show. It was a calculated display for a very specific observer."

He walked to the window again, peering out through the rain-streaked glass. "Our perpetrator, knowing the investigation would be concentrated on the cliff-top house, would have established a lookout, a secondary position from which to observe any movements at my residence. He knows I am a man of... certain inclinations, and that I would likely be involved. This lookout, whom I have been observing for the past two hours, is stationed in the abandoned lighthouse keeper's cottage, a vantage point that offers a clear, if distant, view of this very street. The 'struggle' with the crate, the apparent secrecy of its contents, was designed to pique his interest. The movement of such an item, suggesting valuable evidence or recovered stolen goods, would be reported back to our man almost instantly."

"So he saw you moving an empty crate?" Blackwood scoffed.

"He saw me moving *a* crate, Inspector," Thorne corrected patiently, "one which he would undoubtedly surmise contained something of significance. And he would be correct, though not in the way he imagines. The true 'significance' was not in the crate itself, but in the distraction it provided, and the specific moment of Jenson's return with the actual item of interest. He will know, through his lookout, that Jenson was sent out and returned, carrying something small and concealed, immediately after my 'struggle' with the crate. His mind, already fertile with suspicion regarding the whereabouts of the locket, would put two and two together. He would deduce that I have found something belonging to his accomplice, something that was either dropped or intentionally left as a diversion. And he will want to know precisely what that something is."

A sudden, sharp whistle cut through the drumming of the rain. It was a simple, melodious bird call, but in the tense silence of the study, it sounded like a trumpet blast.

Thorne stiffened, his head cocked slightly. "Ah, Jenson. Punctual as ever." He glanced at Blackwood and Miller. "Our guest has arrived. Or rather, our guest's emissary. Now, gentlemen, if you would be so kind as to withdraw behind the curtain, on either side of the window. Make no sound. Make no movement. Breathe shallowly. This next part requires absolute stillness."

Blackwood hesitated, clearly wrestling with the urge to argue, but the gravity in Thorne's voice, combined with the sudden, sharp reality of the whistle, seemed to quell his protest. He glanced at Miller, who, despite his earlier resentment, was now wide-eyed with anticipation. With a reluctant sigh, Blackwood nodded, and he and Miller melted into the shadows behind the heavy velvet curtains, pressing themselves flat against the wall.

Thorne, meanwhile, had dimmed the gas lamp in the study, plunging the room into a soft, ethereal twilight, illuminated only by the faint glow of the grey, rain-swept sky. He walked to the window, but instead of looking out, he moved to a small, antique telescope mounted on a tripod. He adjusted its focus with practiced ease, turning it slightly to aim at the main gate. The faint, metallic squeak of the gate opening could be heard, followed by soft, cautious footsteps on the gravel drive.

"He is alone," Thorne murmured, his eye pressed to the eyepiece. "And he is cautious. Just as I predicted. He sends the less significant of the two. The accomplice. Elara Vane-Whittaker herself."

A figure, cloaked and hooded against the weather, advanced slowly up the drive. The woman was slender, almost ethereal, her movements graceful despite the muddy ground. She moved with an unsettling stealth, her head swivelling constantly, scanning her surroundings with the predatory vigilance of a hunted animal. Her face, partially obscured by the hood, was pale and drawn, but even in the dim light, Thorne could discern a certain desperate beauty.

She stopped abruptly at the front door, hesitating for a long moment, as if listening intently. Thorne knew she was assessing the silence, the apparent lack of activity. She was a woman accustomed to danger, to clandestine encounters. He knew she would expect a trap, or at the very least, a direct confrontation. But Thorne's method was never direct. It was a slow, inexorable tightening of the net.

Finally, with a decisive motion, she reached out and knocked. Not a loud, imperious knock, but a series of soft, precise raps, a pattern Thorne recognised from his research into the woman's background as a coded signal. It was a signal intended for a clandestine meeting, not a conventional call.

Thorne did not move from the telescope. "She is attempting to signal me," he whispered, his voice low enough to barely reach the hidden policemen. "A pre-arranged code, for an illicit rendezvous. She assumes I am expecting her, or at least, that I will respond in kind. But I shall not."

He waited. The soft knocking pattern was repeated. Then again. Each time, a tense silence followed. After a full minute of no response, Elara Vane-Whittaker pressed herself against the door, seemingly listening through the thick oak. Thorne watched her through the telescope, noting the subtle tremor in her hands, the slight tension in her shoulders. She was desperate.

"Now," Thorne said, a ghost of a smile touching his lips, "we allow her mind to work. She will reason that I am aware of her presence, but am choosing not to answer. That I wish to communicate, but not openly. She will then look for another sign, a non-verbal cue."

He slowly, deliberately, reached into his waistcoat pocket and withdrew a small, pristine white handkerchief. With an almost ceremonial slowness, he unfurled it, then draped it over the edge of the desk, where it hung conspicuously against the dark wood. It was a simple, yet profoundly eloquent gesture. A signal for a clandestine exchange, a symbol of truce, or perhaps, a silent invitation.

Outside, Elara Vane-Whittaker straightened. Thorne saw her head turn, her gaze sweeping the windows of the house, lingering on the study window. Her eyes, even from this distance, seemed to bore into the darkened glass. Then, she saw the handkerchief. Thorne could almost feel the gears turning in her mind, the calculation, the weighing of risks.

After another long moment, she moved. Instead of knocking again, she began to walk slowly around the side of the house, towards the back gate, precisely the route Thorne and Jenson had taken.

"She goes to the back," Thorne announced softly. "The place of least observation, or so she believes. She expects me to follow, or perhaps to meet her there. She will assume I wish to avoid the direct confrontation at the front door. She will also be looking for any sign of the 'crate' or any other evidence."

Thorne placed the small wooden box containing the locket into an inner pocket of his coat. "Inspector Blackwood, Sergeant Miller," he said, turning from the telescope, his eyes now gleaming with a quiet intensity. "The moment is upon us. Our trap is sprung. Remain precisely where you are. I shall return shortly with our captive. And you, gentlemen, will have your arrest."

With that, Thorne opened the study door, and stepped out, pulling it shut behind him, leaving Blackwood and Miller in the near-darkness, their hearts hammering in their chests.

Blackwood let out a deep, shuddering breath, emerging from behind the curtain. "The man is either a genius or a madman, Miller. I still can't quite grasp it. A locket, a crate, a handkerchief... it's all so... theatrical."

"But it's working, sir," Miller whispered, his eyes wide. "She's fallen for it. She's gone around the back."

"Aye, but for what?" Blackwood grumbled. "To speak with him? To attempt to retrieve the locket? What if she's armed? What if this 'confederate' Thorne spoke of is actually her accomplice, and they're lying in wait for *him*?" A flicker of concern crossed Blackwood's face. Despite his exasperation, he couldn't deny the intellectual fascination Thorne exuded, nor the very real danger Thorne had placed himself in.

"He seemed so sure of himself, sir," Miller added. "Like he had every move planned."

"Planned or not, I don't like the thought of him out there, alone, with a potential murderer's accomplice," Blackwood said, moving towards the door again, his hand once more on the brass knob. "If anything happens to him, it'll be on our heads for letting him play his games. I say we follow. Cover him. Discreetly."

"But he told us to stay," Miller protested weakly.

"And he also implied we were not as capable as his 'fishermen and urchins'," Blackwood retorted, his face flushing again. "This time, we follow my orders, Sergeant. We will provide backup, whether he wants it or not. If this woman is as cunning as he says, she may have another trick up her sleeve. We shall observe from a distance, just as he wishes, but a distance that allows for intervention should it be necessary."

He opened the door a crack, peering into the dark hallway. The house was utterly silent, save for the relentless rain and the wind whistling around the eaves. "Quietly now, Miller. Don't make a sound."

They moved with surprising stealth for two such bulky men, their footsteps barely audible on the carpeted hallway. They reached the back door, where Thorne had exited earlier. Blackwood cautiously pushed it open, revealing the sodden garden. The air was thick with the scent of wet earth and salt. The rain had intensified, reducing visibility to a few yards.

"Stay close, Miller," Blackwood whispered, his voice barely audible above the deluge. "And keep your eyes peeled. If she's out here, she'll be somewhere in the shadows."

They stepped out into the downpour, hunching their shoulders against the biting wind. The garden was a chaotic tapestry of dark shrubs and dripping trees. Blackwood pulled out his bullseye lantern, shielding its beam with his hand, casting only a faint, diffused light ahead of them. They moved towards the shed, the location where Thorne and Jenson had retrieved the "crate".

Suddenly, a muffled shout, followed by a sharp scuffle, erupted from the vicinity of the front gate. It was indistinct, swallowed by the roar of the storm, but unmistakable.

"Thorne!" Blackwood gasped, his heart leaping into his throat. "It's Thorne! The front gate!" He broke into a lumbering run, Miller scrambling to keep up, their boots churning through the mud.

They rounded the corner of the house, splashing through puddles, their lanterns swinging wildly. The front gate, usually dark, was now faintly illuminated by a single, flickering gas lamp at the corner of the lane.

The scene that unfolded before them was a tableau of violent confusion.

Thorne was indeed at the gate, but not as the triumphant captor. He was wrestling fiercely with a dark, bulky figure, whose face was obscured by a wide-brimmed hat and a heavy, sodden coat. It was not Elara Vane-Whittaker. This was a man, and a powerful one at that. He had Thorne in a vice-like grip, pushing him back against the wrought-iron gate, seemingly attempting to pin him.

And Jenson, the fisherman, lay sprawled on the muddy ground beside them, his head at an unnatural angle, a dark stain spreading on the front of his oilskins. He was utterly still.

"Thorne! Freeze, you villain!" Blackwood roared, drawing his truncheon. Miller, equally shocked, fumbled for his own.

The assailant, startled by Blackwood's sudden appearance, momentarily released his grip on Thorne. Thorne, quick as a viper, used the momentary advantage to twist free, darting back a step. The attacker, seeing the two policemen, hesitated, his head darting around like a cornered animal. He looked towards the open gate, towards the dark, rain-swept lane, and the possibility of escape.

"Don't move a muscle, you brute!" Blackwood bellowed, charging forward, truncheon raised.

But the attacker was faster. With a desperate surge of adrenaline, he vaulted over the fallen Jenson, through the open gate, and vanished into the impenetrable darkness of the lane, swallowed instantly by the rain and fog. Blackwood, winded and enraged, reached the gate a moment too late, staring helplessly into the void.

"Blast it all!" Blackwood roared, pounding his fist on the gatepost. "He's gone! The villain! And Jenson... good heavens, Miller, look to Jenson!"

Thorne, meanwhile, had sunk to his knees beside the fallen fisherman, his face a mask of grim determination. He pressed his fingers to Jenson's neck, his jaw tight. "He's alive, Inspector, but badly hurt. A blow to the head, and... yes, a knife wound. A desperate man indeed. And far more resourceful than I gave him credit for."

Miller knelt, his face pale, shining his lantern on Jenson. The fisherman's breathing was shallow and ragged, a thin trickle of blood staining his grey hair where he had struck the ground. "He's lost a lot of blood, Mr. Thorne. We need a doctor, immediately!"

"Indeed," Thorne replied, his eyes scanning the muddy ground around Jenson. "But first, we must confirm the identity of our attacker. The woman was merely a decoy, a distraction. He knew I would be looking for her. He used her as bait, a counter-trap. A cunning move. He saw Jenson's signal, knew that his accomplice was being sent to investigate, and used the opportunity to strike. He would have known that my attention, and any observation I might have had, would be focused on the woman."

"Who was it, Thorne?" Blackwood demanded, his voice thick with anger and frustration. "Who was that man?"

Thorne rose slowly, his gaze now fixed on something in the mud near Jenson's outstretched hand. He knelt again, pulling something small and metallic from the muck. It was a silver flask, intricately engraved, gleaming dully in the lantern light.

"It is our perpetrator, Inspector," Thorne said, his voice cold and precise, holding up the flask. "The man we seek. Silas Blackwood, the 'devious mind' I spoke of. This flask belongs to him; I recognised it from your own files, Inspector, a unique possession he was known to carry. He came not to retrieve the locket, but to silence Jenson, to eliminate the witness to his accomplice's activities, and then, presumably, to engage me. He knew I would be distracted by the presence of his confederate."

"But the woman? Elara Vane-Whittaker? Where is she?" Miller asked, looking around wildly.

"She, I imagine, is long gone," Thorne replied, rising to his full height. "Her role was fulfilled the moment she created the diversion. She merely knocked at the door, confirming to Blackwood that I had found something of hers. She likely melted into the fog the moment her master appeared to engage me. He left her the most mundane of tasks while he handled the truly dangerous work. A shrewd allocation of resources, if a brutal one."

"So, he knew our game, or at least, he suspected it," Blackwood mused, looking at the flask. "He turned our own trap against us."

"Precisely," Thorne confirmed. "He is, as I stated, a mind as devious as it is desperate. He anticipated my method of using a known weakness to draw out his accomplice, and used the opportunity to eliminate a perceived threat and perhaps even... incapacitate me." Thorne touched his temple. A small cut, now bleeding freely, marred his otherwise unblemished forehead. He had taken a blow during the struggle.

"We need to send out a general alert, immediately!" Blackwood exclaimed, finally taking charge, his professional instincts overriding his earlier indignation. "He's wounded Jenson. He's attempted to assault you. And he's still at large! Miller, you go back to the station, raise the alarm! Get every available man out here, sweep the area, check the trains, the docks, every possible escape route! I'll stay here, secure the scene, and wait for the doctor."

"A futile gesture, Inspector," Thorne said, wiping the blood from his brow with the back of his hand. "He will have planned for this. His escape routes would have been meticulously prepared. He has had weeks, if not months, to prepare for this eventuality. By the time your men are deployed, he will be long gone. He will have vanished like a wisp of smoke into the North Sea fog."
