The town, indeed, had very little else to talk of. “And so, the poor girl is quite ruined, you say, Lady Ashworth?” “Ruined! Oh, my dear Miss Vance, it is beyond all doubt. Did not I tell you, that when Mr. Finch left with such haste for Bath, leaving Miss Sophia Croft quite bewildered, it was for no good purpose? A young man, with barely a ten-pound note to his name, who spends his days riding about in a phaeton, and his evenings at the card-tables, is not to be trusted, I assure you!” “But he had made his intentions so very clear, had he not? And given her every reason to suppose he would return to claim her hand?” “Intention, indeed! A man’s intentions, when there is a fortune to be had elsewhere, are like smoke, my dear; they vanish with the first breath of a richer breeze. He has caught Lady Amelia Davies, they say, a perfect fright, but with fifty thousand pounds, besides the reversion of her uncle’s estate. No wonder he has quite forgotten the pretty Miss Croft and her paltry five thousand! Lord, the ways of men! But I must say, for all her beauty, Sophia was ever a flighty creature; she danced, and she flirted, and quite neglected the sober duties of a clergyman’s daughter.”

“And Lady Amelia, is she then quite agreeable to such a sudden change of heart?” asked Eleanor, with a sigh, for she felt much for her poor friend Sophia, who was now confined to her room in a state of the greatest distress. “Agreeable! My dear girl, when has money ever been disagreeable to a woman of thirty, who has resigned herself to spinsterhood? Lady Amelia, I am told, is quite beside herself with joy. She has always been too stout for elegance, and too fond of her books for company, but a gentleman with a handsome face, even if his pockets are empty, is not to be refused! Her father, Sir Giles, is quite delighted, I hear, for young Finch is a sprig of a fellow, and can talk of horses and hunting, which is quite beyond Sir Giles’s own abilities now. And they say Finch has a terrible temper, but Amelia, poor soul, is so deaf she will scarce hear him, and if she does, will attribute it to the high wind. Oh, it is a perfect match of convenience, depend upon it!”

Eleanor found herself unable to press the matter further, for the thought of Sophia’s suffering made her heart ache. She rose and walked to the window. “I must go and see my sister, Lady Ashworth. She has had a trying day, and will need quiet.” “Nonsense, my dear! Quiet is the very worst thing for a broken heart! What she needs is company, and a good glass of Madeira. I have just the thing, a bottle of ’78, that my poor departed Sir Roger swore by when he had the ague. Come, we shall go together, and cheer her up with a lively tune on the pianoforte. What is her favourite air? Is it ‘The Banks of Allan Water’ or ‘Come, Lasses and Lads’?” “Indeed, Lady Ashworth, I fear any music at all would be quite unsuited to her present state. She requires rest, and perhaps a warm posset. I shall sit with her myself, for I am sure she would rather be alone with me than with any other at this moment.” “Ah, well, if she has you, she has all she needs, I suppose. You were always the sensible one, Eleanor. But do not let her mope for too long! A month at most, and then she must be out and about. There is young Mr. Davies, the curate, a most excellent young man, though he does squint a little; and Mr. Thorne, the widower, with his estate at Highwood. Highwood is not to be despised, my dear. Two thousand a year, and such a fine library! His first wife, poor woman, was quite sickly, and he has been rather lonely these past five years. He will be on the lookout, I warrant you. A handsome girl like Sophia will not be long without a suitor, depend upon it. What is a broken engagement, after all? A mere scratch, I say!”

Eleanor could only offer a weak smile. “I believe, Lady Ashworth, that some scratches feel deeper than others.” She then made her excuses and hurried upstairs to Sophia’s room. She found her sister lying upon her bed, her face buried in the pillows, and the room almost dark save for the faint glow of the setting sun. “Sophia, my dearest sister,” Eleanor whispered, sitting gently beside her. “Are you quite unwell?” A muffled sob was the only reply. Eleanor sat patiently, stroking Sophia’s hair, until at last the sobs subsided a little. “Oh, Eleanor,” Sophia murmured, her voice thick with tears, “how could he? How could he betray me so utterly?” “Hush, my love. He is not worthy of your tears. His character, it appears, was not what we supposed.” “But the cottage at Elmwood, Eleanor! The roses we spoke of planting by the door! He said he would never love another!” Eleanor sighed inwardly. The promises of young men, so lightly made, and so cruelly broken. She knew that no words could truly comfort her sister now, only the gentle passage of time.

Downstairs, Lady Ashworth, having dismissed the idea of Madeira and music, instead decided that a good gossip with her housekeeper would be just the thing. “They say young Finch has even borrowed money from his own mother, Mrs. Dobson, to keep up his dashing ways! And she a woman of such respectable connections, too! I remember her first cousin, Mrs. Perkins, who had a poultry farm near Taunton. Such good eggs, Mrs. Dobson, the best! But her son, oh dear! It is a sad world, Mrs. Dobson, when a man prefers pounds to character, and a fortune to a true heart. Though, mind you, Miss Croft is not without her faults. A trifle too fond of her own reflection, I always thought. Still, it is a sad business, a sad business indeed. And what will become of her now, poor thing? She will be quite an old maid, depend upon it, by the time she finds another suitable match!” Mrs. Dobson, a woman of few words but much wisdom, merely nodded, as she polished a silver tray, having long understood that Lady Ashworth required not answers, but merely an audience for her pronouncements.

Eleanor continued to stroke Sophia’s hair, the silence in the room punctuated only by Sophia’s occasional shuddering breaths. The last rays of the sun, now a deep orange, barely illuminated the dust motes dancing in the air, a stark contrast to the brilliant hopes that had only yesterday filled this very room. “He spoke of children, Eleanor,” Sophia whispered, her voice barely audible. “Little boys with his dark hair, and girls with my eyes. He planned a swing from the great oak in the garden, and a nursery painted blue, just like the sky on a summer’s day.” Eleanor’s heart ached for her sister. These were the dreams that had sustained Sophia through the long winter, the bright promise that had made her eyes sparkle with such joy. To have them so brutally snatched away was a cruelty almost beyond bearing. “Such pretty imaginings, my dear,” Eleanor murmured, choosing her words with care. “But often, the imaginings of men are built on sand. They shift with the tide, and disappear with the first strong wind.”

Sophia lifted her head slightly, her face tear stained and swollen, her eyes red rimmed. “Do you truly believe he never loved me, Eleanor? Was it all a charade? All those tender letters, those stolen glances, the vows beneath the moonlight?” Her voice broke on the last word, and she buried her face once more in the pillow. Eleanor wished she could offer a simple, reassuring lie, but her honesty would not permit it. She had always been the practical one, the one who saw beneath the glittering surface. Mr. Finch had always possessed a certain charm, it was true, a ready smile and a glib tongue, but Eleanor had detected an underlying hollowness, a calculating glint in his eyes that Sophia, blinded by affection, had failed to notice. He had spoken of love, yes, but he had spoken with equal enthusiasm of the Elmwood estate, of its excellent hunting prospects, of the respectable lineage it would afford him. Eleanor had long suspected that Sophia’s considerable dowry had been as attractive to Mr. Finch as Sophia herself, if not more so.

“Love, my dearest Sophia, can be a complex thing,” Eleanor began softly, trying to steer away from outright condemnation, which would only deepen Sophia’s pain. “Perhaps he was fond of you, in his way. Perhaps he even believed his own words, in the moment. But true love, enduring love, does not waver so readily. It does not abandon its commitments for a more advantageous prospect.” She paused, considering. “His actions speak louder than any of his declarations, I am afraid. To sacrifice your happiness for mere pounds and position, to cause such distress to one he claimed to cherish, reveals a character that is, to be blunt, utterly unworthy of you.”

Sophia remained silent for a long while, her body trembling slightly beneath Eleanor’s hand. The darkness in the room deepened, the last vestiges of twilight fading entirely, leaving them in a dim, uncertain gloom. A servant, hearing Eleanor’s voice, tapped gently at the door. “Shall I bring a lamp, Miss Eleanor?” Mrs. Davies, the head maid, inquired respectfully. “Please, Mrs. Davies. And a fresh pot of tea, if you would be so kind. For both of us,” Eleanor replied, her voice steady, despite the turmoil within her.

When the lamp was lit, casting a warm, flickering glow across the room, Eleanor saw her sister’s face more clearly. The ravages of grief were evident, but there was also a dawning comprehension, a spark of anger beginning to replace the raw sorrow in her eyes. “Miss Croft,” Sophia said, her voice hard. “She is a great heiress, is she not? They say her father made a fortune in trade, in textiles. A truly vulgar man, by all accounts, but immensely wealthy. Is that it, then? Is he selling his soul for a larger dowry?”

Eleanor sighed. “It would appear so, my love. It is a harsh truth, but one we must face. Mr. Finch, it seems, placed a higher value on worldly gain than on the affection he professed for you. His ambition, it seems, knew no bounds, and certainly no loyalty.”

Sophia pushed herself up to a sitting position, her eyes fixed on some distant point, as if reviewing her past with Mr. Finch through this new, cynical lens. “He always spoke of the burdens of being a younger son, Eleanor. Of the necessity of making his own way, of the pressure to secure his future. I thought it was admirable, his drive. I thought he wanted to build a good life for us, together.” A bitter laugh escaped her lips. “How foolish I was. I mistook avarice for ambition, and a calculating nature for a romantic spirit. The cottage at Elmwood. The roses. All of it a carefully constructed illusion, designed to entrap me and my dowry.”

Eleanor nodded. “Many men, my dear, are adept at such constructions. We, as women, are often encouraged to see the best in them, to overlook their flaws, to weave our own romantic narratives around their less than noble intentions. It is a dangerous habit, Sophia, and one we must learn to guard against. This pain you feel now, this betrayal, will serve as a harsh but invaluable lesson. It will teach you discernment.”

Sophia took a trembling sip of the tea Mrs. Davies had brought, the warm liquid a small comfort against the chill in her heart. “Discernment,” she repeated slowly. “I suppose I had none where he was concerned. I saw what I wished to see.” She looked at Eleanor, her expression softening slightly. “You always had such clear sight, Eleanor. Why did I not listen to your subtle warnings? You never quite approved of him, did you?”

Eleanor smiled sadly. “It was not my place to disapprove openly, my dear. Your happiness was paramount. I merely wished for you to be certain, to examine him with a clear eye, which I know is difficult when one’s heart is so deeply involved. But do not blame yourself. His deception was skillful, and your heart was open and trusting. Those are not faults, Sophia, but virtues. It is his character, not yours, that is found wanting.”

The tea and the quiet conversation, the presence of her steadfast sister, slowly began to work their balm upon Sophia’s wounded spirit. The immediate agony began to recede, replaced by a dull ache, and a growing sense of injustice. “What will I do now, Eleanor?” she asked, a practical note entering her voice for the first time. “How will I face society? Everyone knows of our engagement. The invitations for the wedding have been sent. The dress is almost finished.”

Eleanor squeezed her hand. “We will face it together, as we always do. We will write to the guests, explaining the unfortunate turn of events. We will send the dress back. It will be difficult, yes, there will be gossip, and whispers, and pitying glances. But we are not weak, Sophia. We are not without resources. And you, my dear sister, are not without worth. A broken engagement is not the end of the world, though it may feel so now. It is merely a redirection, a turning of the page to a new, and hopefully much better, chapter.”

Sophia leaned her head on Eleanor’s shoulder, a profound weariness settling over her. “I feel as though I have been stripped bare, Eleanor. My future, once so clear, is now a blank canvas, and I have no idea how to begin painting upon it again.”

“Then we shall begin by painting it with quiet strength,” Eleanor replied, her gaze firm. “We will allow time for healing, for reflection. And then, when you are ready, you will discover that there are other possibilities, other paths, perhaps even more fulfilling ones than the one that was so cruelly closed to you. You are young, beautiful, intelligent, and kind. These qualities, my dear, are far more valuable than any dowry, and will attract a man of true worth, one who sees *you*, not merely your circumstances.”

Eleanor stayed with Sophia for a considerable time, offering comfort and practical advice, helping her to draft a polite but firm letter to the unfortunate guests, and discussing the return of the wedding finery. The worst of Sophia’s despair seemed to have passed, replaced by a fragile resolve, a quiet determination to mend her broken spirit. It was late when Eleanor finally descended the grand staircase, the house now quiet, save for the distant murmur of voices from the servants’ quarters.

Always money! People say it is the root of all evil, and indeed, one sees it plain enough in this sad affair. Mr. Finch, a charming young man, so well spoken, such a captivating smile, one would never have suspected! But beneath it all, a mercenary heart, I tell you! A mercenary heart, plain as day!” Lady Ashworth paused, taking a dramatic sip of her sherry. “Though, one must admit, Miss Croft has a rather impressive fortune. Her father, a tradesman, yes, but a shrewd one, they say. One of those men who simply cannot help but acquire more pounds than he knows what to do with! And his daughter, an only child, poor thing, though I always thought her a trifle too fond of her own reflection, as I said. Always preening, always adjusting her ribbons. Not a patch on our Sophia, with her sweet nature and her gentle ways, oh no! But money, Mrs. Dobson, money speaks volumes in this world, does it not? A louder voice than any sweet nature, it seems!”

Mrs. Dobson, having finished polishing the silver tray to a blinding sheen, carefully placed it on a side table. She then picked up a rather intricate porcelain figurine, a shepherdess with a lamb, and began to dust it with a soft cloth. “Indeed, my Lady,” she replied, her voice a low murmur, her eyes fixed on the delicate features of the figurine. “Money does speak, for some.”

“Exactly! For some! And for Mr. Finch, it seems to have shouted rather loudly indeed! Imagine, Mrs. Dobson, throwing over a connection such as ours! A family of good standing, with an excellent name, and a respectable dowry, though perhaps not quite so enormous as Miss Croft’s vulgar heap of gold! But still, respectable! He will regret it, mark my words! He will regret choosing a fortune over true breeding, and a gilded cage over a loving home. Depend upon it, Mrs. Dobson, he will!” Lady Ashworth’s tone was definitive, as if she were delivering an unalterable prophecy.

She shifted in her armchair, a rustle of silk and lace. “But we cannot mope, can we? No, indeed not! What is done is done, and one must always look forward! Sophia is young, as I said, and handsome, and with her sweet nature, she will not be long without a suitor. A broken engagement, a mere ripple in the pond, Mrs. Dobson! A mere ripple! We must simply find her another suitable match, and quickly, before the whispers turn to settled opinion, and people begin to think there was something amiss with *her*, poor girl! For society is cruel, Mrs. Dobson, cruel indeed, and quick to judge, particularly a young woman who has suffered such a slight.”

Mrs. Dobson nodded slowly, her hand moving in gentle circles over the shepherdess. “Society can be most unforgiving, my Lady.”

“Unforgiving indeed! But we shall not let it defeat us! I have been thinking, Mrs. Dobson, and one must always be thinking, for one’s family’s future depends upon it! There is young Mr. Davies, the curate. A most estimable young man, though he does squint a little, as I mentioned. But a good heart, I believe, and sound principles. And the living at St. Cuthbert’s, though not wealthy, is secure, and he comes from a respectable family, his uncle being a canon of Winchester Cathedral, a most impressive connection! A gentle soul for our Sophia, perhaps? Someone who would appreciate her quiet virtues, rather than merely her dowry!”

Lady Ashworth paused, her gaze drifting towards the window, as if contemplating the possibilities. “Or, of course, there is Mr. Thorne. The widower from Highwood. His first wife, poor woman, was sickly, and he has been alone these five years. He is of a good age, not too old, not too young. And a stable character, I understand. Not given to these flighty notions of young men like Mr. Finch. He would offer Sophia security, and a position of some consequence. Highwood is not to be despised, Mrs. Dobson, not at all! Imagine our Sophia, mistress of Highwood! What a triumph that would be, a true feather in our cap, after this unfortunate business with Mr. Finch!”

A faint smile touched Mrs. Dobson’s lips. “Highwood is a fine estate, my Lady, and Mr. Thorne a most respectable gentleman, from what I have heard.”

“Precisely! Respectable! That is what we need, Mrs. Dobson! Stability, security, and respectability! None of these frivolous flirtations and broken promises! I shall invite him to dine, yes, next week perhaps. And Mr. Davies, too, though perhaps not on the same evening. One must not appear too eager, nor too transparent in one’s intentions! It must all seem quite natural, quite accidental, you understand. A little garden party, perhaps, when the weather improves. Or a quiet musical evening, though Sophia is not much for the pianoforte these days, poor girl. But she sings so sweetly! Her voice would charm anyone, anyone at all, depend upon it!”

Lady Ashworth patted her chest, as if settling her racing thoughts. “This business with Mr. Finch, it truly is a blessing in disguise, Mrs. Dobson. A blessing, I say! For it has revealed his true character, and saved our Sophia from a lifetime of unhappiness with a grasping, insincere man. One must always look for the silver lining, must one not? And the silver lining here is that Sophia is now free to make a much better match! A far more solid, more respectable, and perhaps even wealthier match! Oh yes, a blessing indeed!”

She paused again, this time her eyes alighting on a new topic. “And speaking of good matches, Mrs. Dobson, have you heard about the Featherstone girls? Such a plight! Two of them, both past their first bloom, and still unmarried! Their father, poor Mr. Featherstone, quite ruined himself with those disastrous investments in the canal project! And now they have barely enough to keep a proper household, let alone dowries for two daughters! It is a lamentable state of affairs, Mrs. Dobson, truly lamentable! I often wonder what will become of them. Governesses, perhaps? Or companions to some dreadful old aunt? A sad fate, a sad fate indeed! It makes one truly appreciate one’s own circumstances, does it not? To have one’s daughters properly provided for, even if one of them has suffered a slight heartbreak. For heartbreak, Mrs. Dobson, can be mended! But an empty purse, well, that is a wound that seldom heals, and often festers!”

Mrs. Dobson, who had by now carefully placed the shepherdess back on its shelf, merely offered a small, knowing smile. She knew her Lady Ashworth well. The pronouncements, the tangents, the constant weighing of fortunes and misfortunes, it was all part of the daily ritual, a way of ordering the world, however chaotically, in her mind. She understood that Lady Ashworth, for all her superficiality, genuinely wished well for her family, in her own peculiar, socially constrained way.

Her mother’s tireless efforts to secure advantageous matches were well known, but to hear Sophia’s recent heartbreak so quickly re framed as a ‘blessing in disguise’ and a springboard for new, even grander marital prospects, was jarring. Eleanor approached her mother, her expression calm but firm. “Sophia is resting, Mother. She has had a difficult day, as you can imagine. Her heart is truly bruised. It will take more than a month for such a wound to heal.”

Two thousand a year, and such a fine library! We shall arrange a small gathering soon, yes, and Sophia will be there, looking her best, depend upon it! A little fresh air, a little polite conversation, it will do her the world of good!”

Eleanor found herself torn between exasperation and a weary understanding of her mother’s intentions. No, Eleanor, you are too soft hearted, too sentimental. This is not a matter for moping and introspection. This is a matter for practical considerations! Sophia must secure her future, and quickly! Mrs. Dobson, who had been silently observing the exchange, now stepped forward. “Perhaps, my Lady, a change of scenery might be beneficial for Miss Sophia.
