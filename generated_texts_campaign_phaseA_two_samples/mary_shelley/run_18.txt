The twilight deepened over my ancestral grounds; my thoughts, ever prone to a melancholic turn, were heavy with the weight of unseen ages; I walked the garden paths, as had been my habit since childhood, by the whispering avenue of ancient yews, on my way to the observatory. A great part of these cultivated enclosures, though tended with meticulous care, seemed to me but fleeting triumphs over the encroaching wild, and fragrant thickets of jasmine and honeysuckle were scattered here and there, their sweetness cloying. The nightingales sang loudly in the cypresses above; mixed with their liquid trills, I heard the faint, distant strains of a revel. It was the eve of the Vernal Solstice. The young people, the children of my tenants and the neighbouring gentry, held a masquerade within the lower hall, to which all the household were invited. The lawn was speckled by lanterns, whose flickering lights and silken streamers, stirring in the gentle breeze, added to the ephemeral gaiety of the scene. On a dais erected beneath the grand archway, a number of the younger part of the assembly were dancing. I leaned against a marble plinth to observe them. The orchestra played a lively contredanse, its volatile notes giving wings to the feet of the dancers, while the lookers-on unconsciously beat time. At first the tripping measure lifted my spirit with it, and for a moment my eyes gladly followed the mazes of the dance. The revulsion of thought passed like keen steel to my heart. Ye are all going to vanish, I thought; already your memory is fading from the annals of time. Awhile, because you are gifted with grace and bloom, you fancy that you live: but frail is the “veil of life” that enshrouds sentience; dissoluble the silvered thread that binds you to its weave. Its streets, once teeming with the vibrant pulse of commerce and life, now echo with the emptiness of a forgotten dream. His words painted a tableau of such desolate finality that a fresh wave of icy dread washed over us. Aella, ever practical, though her face was now pale and her lips tightly pressed, drew her children closer to her, her hands resting protectively on their heads, her gaze once more seeking mine, silently pleading for assurance I could not give. Lysander, roused from his contemplative stance, fixed his gaze upon Callan, his brow furrowed, a silent question in his usually serene eyes. He did not speak, but his posture conveyed an intense absorption, a critical analysis of Callan's account, perhaps searching for a flaw, a thread of hope, or a deeper meaning in the stark horror.

For my part, Callan's terrifying depiction resonated deeply with the fears I had already harboured. The image of the Capital's desolation, of lives extinguished without so much as a preceding cough or gasp, was more horrifying than any fever or visible malady. It was an invisible predator, a thief of breath and being, leaving no trace but the sudden, appalling absence of life. My mind raced, grappling with the immediacy of the threat. The distance from the Capital, which had once seemed a secure buffer, now felt perilously narrow, a mere whisper separating us from the encroaching shadow. Our estate, our meticulously cultivated haven, now felt terribly vulnerable, a brightly lit stage upon which the darkness might descend at any moment.

Her suggestion, while born of desperation, resonated with a faint spark of hope. His wisdom, as ever, was a sobering counterpoint to our emotional turmoil. His words, though devoid of immediate solace, forced a consideration of the deeper complexities. Not yet. But the enemy has established positions in the valley. They are encircling us. The King has been preparing for this. He believes we can withstand a prolonged siege. Our stores are considerable, our defenses strong."

"Encircling us," I repeated, the words chilling. "And the fires? They were not brush fires, were they?"

She sighed, a deep, weary sound. "No, Your Majesty. They are the enemy’s campfires. And… they have been raiding the nearby villages. Lysander has sent out small parties to try and deter them, to bring in any survivors who can make it to the castle. But it is becoming increasingly difficult."

The horror of her words washed over me. Raiding villages. My people. Lysander sending men out into the jaws of danger. I saw the faces of the villagers I knew, the farmers, the merchants, the children. My heart twisted in anguish. "Survivors?" I whispered. "Have any made it?"

Aella shook her head slowly. "A few. Mostly women and children, Your Majesty. They are being tended to in the lower quarters. They are shaken, and some are injured. But they are safe within these walls."

Safe within these walls. The phrase, once a comfort, now felt like a desperate plea. We were not safe. We were trapped. And the world outside was burning. The truth, when it finally came, was a heavy, suffocating blanket of dread. Lysander had not merely been strategizing. He had been fighting for our very survival, holding back a tide of devastation, while shielding me from the full horror of it.

My anger flared, hot and sudden. "Why did you not tell me sooner, Aella? Why did he not tell me?"

"The King’s orders, Your Majesty," she replied, her voice firm once more. "He wished to spare you the anxiety, to allow you to focus solely on the children. He believed the burden would be too great for you to carry both responsibilities."

"My responsibility is to my people as well as my children!" I retorted, my voice rising. "I am Queen! I am not a fragile flower to be shielded from reality. How can I aid him if I am kept in ignorance?"

Aella met my gaze, her own eyes holding a flash of defiance, but also understanding. "Forgive me, Your Majesty. I only followed my King’s command. He loves you. He believes in your strength, but he knows your heart. He wished to protect it for as long as possible."

Her words, though intended as an explanation, only fueled my frustration. Lysander, in his desperate attempt to protect me, had isolated me. He had made me a bystander in my own kingdom's fight for survival. But the anger, intense as it was, quickly gave way to a deeper, more profound fear. If the enemy was encircling us, raiding villages, and establishing positions, then the "long siege" Aella had spoken of was not a prediction, but a present reality.

I took a deep breath, trying to calm the storm within me. Anger would serve no purpose now. Resolve was what was needed. "Very well, Aella," I said, my voice regaining its composure, though it still trembled slightly. "Tell me everything. No more evasions. No more secrets. What is the true strength of the enemy? What are their intentions? What are our chances?"

Aella hesitated for a moment, then nodded. The flicker of defiance in her eyes softened into a shared burden. "As you command, Your Majesty. The enemy is a coalition of Northern tribes, led by a warlord known as Kael. They have been emboldened by recent skirmishes along the border and have united with a singular goal: to sack the capital and claim its wealth. Their numbers are vast, greater than we anticipated. Lysander estimates their forces to be at least triple our own, perhaps more. But we have the advantage of these walls. They are ancient, strong, and well-manned. Our stores are substantial enough to last for many months, perhaps even a year, if rationed carefully. Our greatest challenge is their relentless nature. They will not easily break off the siege. Kael is known for his tenacity. And for his cruelty."

Her words painted a grim picture, far worse than any of my feverish imaginings. Triple our numbers. A ruthless warlord. Months, perhaps a year, within these walls, watching our resources dwindle, our hope erode. The weight of her revelation settled upon me, heavy and cold. It was not just a battle for survival. It was a test of endurance, of spirit, of faith.

"And Lysander?" I asked, my voice barely a whisper. "What is his strategy?"

"He intends to hold the castle until relief can arrive from our allies to the south. Messengers were dispatched weeks ago, under cover of darkness, before the full encirclement. He believes they will reach them, and that they will answer our call. Until then, we hold. We defend every inch of these walls. We break their spirit through unwavering resistance. He expects them to try to breach the gates, to scale the walls. We are prepared for those attempts."

Relief. Allies. Months. It all sounded so precarious, so dependent on so many variables. The thought of Lysander, fighting this battle, making these brutal decisions, alone in his chambers, filled me with a fresh wave of concern.

"I need to see him, Aella," I said, my resolve hardening. "Now. I am his Queen, and I will not be kept from him any longer."

Aella paused, then slowly nodded. "Very well, Your Majesty. Follow me. But be warned, he is… occupied. And he is under immense strain."

Her warning did little to deter me. I needed to see him, to understand the full scope of what we faced, and to offer whatever comfort or counsel I could. The children were safe for the moment, playing their innocent games in the shielded great hall. My place, I realized, was no longer just beside their cradle, but beside my King, on the precipice of our kingdom’s fate. I would protect them, as he commanded. But I would do so with open eyes, and with the full knowledge of the darkness that now surrounded us. The long vigil had truly begun.I wanted to ask what kind of eventuality, what kind of intelligence, what exactly was out there, but her formidable presence always stifled the words on my tongue. Her answers were always measured, precise, and ultimately uninformative.

The silence that had settled upon the castle was not peaceful. It was a suffocating blanket, pressing down on every breath, magnifying every creak of the ancient timbers. My own footsteps echoed unnaturally loudly as I walked the familiar corridors, now stripped of their vibrant life. The tapestries, once rich with stories of ancient kings and mythical beasts, seemed to absorb the light, deepening the shadows. Even the air, once crisp and clean from the mountain winds, had grown heavy, thick with the scent of old stone, dust, and something else indefinable, something like waiting.

My children, bless their innocent hearts, seemed to adapt to this new normal with a resilient grace that both awed and pained me. Elara, ever observant, had stopped asking why we could not go to the gardens, why the stables were quiet, or why the king’s guard no longer paraded with their gleaming armor and banners. Instead, she invented elaborate indoor games, transforming our private chambers into fantastical kingdoms, the grand hall into a perilous jungle. Her younger siblings, still too small to grasp the full implications of our confinement, followed her lead with unreserved enthusiasm. Their laughter, though still present, felt like a precious, fragile thing, a defiant bloom in a barren landscape.

I spent my days weaving a delicate tapestry of normalcy for them. We would read tales of heroes and faraway lands, paint with colors sourced from precious, dwindling supplies, and bake simple breads in the kitchen, the warmth of the oven a temporary solace against the pervasive chill. I taught them songs and riddles, anything to fill the emptiness of the hours, to distract them from the quietude that threatened to swallow us whole. But even as I smiled and played, a cold knot of dread tightened in my stomach. Every innocent question, every carefree giggle, was a stark reminder of what I stood to lose, what Lysander had commanded me to protect.

Nights were the hardest. Once the children were asleep, their small, trusting faces illuminated by the soft glow of a single lamp, I would sit by the window, gazing out at the impenetrable darkness beyond the castle walls. There was no moon for what felt like endless nights, only an oppressive blackness that seemed to press in from all sides. My imagination, a treacherous companion, would conjure images of what lay beyond: armies marching, fires burning, the land ravaged. Lysander’s face would appear in my mind’s eye, his strong features etched with worry, his eyes holding that unreadable depth that always both reassured and unsettled me. I yearned for his touch, for the solid comfort of his presence, for his steady voice to cut through the terrifying silence.

The last time I had seen him, his words had been sharp, concise, military. There was no time for tenderness, no space for comfort. "Protect them. Whatever happens, protect them." That command had been branded into my soul. It was a burden, a purpose, a promise. But how could I protect them from an enemy I could not see, from a threat I could not name? Aella’s vague assurances, her unwavering faith in Lysander’s strategies, did little to quell the gnawing fear. My fear was not for myself. It was a visceral, primal fear for my children, for their future, for the very idea of their innocence shattered.

The castle staff, too, began to show the strain. Their faces grew gaunter, their movements slower. The once bustling kitchens were now hushed, the cooks meticulously rationing ingredients. Meat became a rare luxury, vegetables grown in the castle’s small, sheltered courtyard becoming our staple. The wine cellars, once overflowing, were now under strict lock and key, reserved only for medicinal purposes or rare, somber occasions. I saw the fear in their eyes, the unspoken questions in their averted glances. They cleaned with a quiet diligence, their brooms sweeping away dust that seemed to accumulate faster now, as if the castle itself was shedding its skin in resignation. They served meals with bowed heads, their footsteps barely audible on the cold stone floors. I tried to offer words of encouragement, to assure them of Lysander’s wisdom, but the words felt hollow even to me. What assurance could I give when I had none myself?

One afternoon, I found myself watching the children play a game of pretend knighthood in the great hall. Elara, cloaked in an old velvet curtain, valiantly fought an imaginary dragon, her wooden sword swishing through the air. Her younger brother, barely four, hid behind a massive oak chest, peeking out with wide, frightened eyes. The scene was heartwarming, yet it struck a painful chord. Their play was a reflection of the world outside that we were now cut off from, a world of battles and heroes, of good triumphant over evil. But what if evil prevailed? What if our heroes were not enough?

Aella entered the hall then, her usual brisk pace subdued. She had a rolled parchment in her hand, secured with a wax seal. Her face was even more drawn than usual, her eyes shadowed by dark circles that seemed to grow deeper with each passing day. Her dark hair, typically braided with military precision, had a few stray wisps escaping, a small but significant sign of her mounting exhaustion.

"Your Majesty," she said, her voice low, "I have just come from the King’s chambers."

I rose immediately, my heart leaping into my throat. "Is there news?" I asked, trying to keep my voice steady. The children, sensing the shift in my demeanor, had stopped their play and were looking at us, their young faces mirroring my sudden tension.

Aella glanced at them, a flicker of something unreadable passing through her eyes. "Nothing to concern the children, Your Majesty," she said, her tone firm. She then lowered her voice further, almost to a whisper. "The King has ordered a further reduction in rations. And there are reports from the outer patrols. Nothing conclusive, but activity has been noted."

"Activity?" I pressed, my voice barely audible. My blood ran cold. This was the first concrete indication of anything beyond vague threats.

"Fires, distant. And movements in the forest beyond the valley road," she said, her gaze sweeping the vast hall, as if assessing its defenses. "Scouts report larger groups than previously observed. Not just bandits, Your Majesty. More organized."

My breath hitched. "More organized? What does that mean, Aella?"

She met my gaze directly, her eyes weary but resolute. "It means we must be prepared for a long siege, Your Majesty. The King believes they are testing our defenses, probing our weak points. We must conserve everything. And security will be heightened. No one is to leave the inner keep without express permission from the King himself, or myself, in his stead."

The news, though expected in some abstract way, hit me with the force of a physical blow. A long siege. The words echoed in my mind, a death knell to any lingering hope of a swift resolution, of Lysander’s triumphant return, of the world outside returning to its former state. This was not a storm to weather, but a winter to endure, potentially a permanent frost.

I nodded, unable to speak, my mind racing. The children. The food. The morale of the staff. Lysander. He was cloistered away, making these grim decisions, bearing the weight of an entire kingdom on his shoulders, alone. I longed to be with him, to share the burden, to offer what little comfort I could. But his orders were clear. My place was here, with our children.

"I understand," I finally managed to say, my voice a thin thread. "Thank you, Aella. Please convey my… my thoughts to the King." I wanted to say more, to send a message of love, of support, of longing, but I knew Aella would simply convey it as "Her Majesty sends her thoughts." She was a soldier, not a messenger of sentiment.

She nodded curtly, her eyes already distant, no doubt planning the logistics of further rationing, tighter security. "Of course, Your Majesty. I must return. There is much to do." With another brief bow, she turned and left, her footsteps once again swallowed by the vast, silent hall.

I stood there for a long moment, the news settling upon me like a shroud. The children, sensing the somber shift, had gathered around my skirts, their small hands clutching the fabric. Elara looked up at me, her brow furrowed. "Mama, what is a siege?" she asked, her voice small and tentative.

I knelt, gathering her close, her siblings pressing in around us. "It means we must be very brave, my love," I whispered, burying my face in her soft hair. "It means we must be very patient. And we must trust your father." The words tasted like ash. Trust. Patience. Bravery. These were the only weapons I had.

The days that followed were an exercise in disciplined suffering. The reduction in rations was immediate and noticeable. The meals became even sparer, the portions smaller, though still sufficient to keep us from outright hunger. But the joy of eating, the communal pleasure of a shared meal, was gone. It was replaced by a grim necessity. The servants moved with an even greater sense of urgency, their faces set in grim lines.

Security tightened visibly. More guards patrolled the inner keep, their heavy footsteps a constant, unsettling rhythm. The iron grates that protected the inner courtyards were lowered at dusk and remained so until dawn. All windows overlooking the outer walls were barred or boarded, plunging many of the rooms into a perpetual twilight. The castle began to feel less like a home and more like a tomb.

I found myself increasingly withdrawn, my interactions with the staff and even Aella becoming more functional, less personal. My focus narrowed to the children. Their well-being became my sole universe. I watched them constantly, charting their moods, their appetites, their questions. Elara, with her sharp mind, noticed everything.

"Mama, why are the bread portions so small now?" she asked one morning, looking at her plate with a thoughtful frown.

"Because we must be careful with our food, my darling," I explained patiently, forcing a smile. "There are many people in the castle, and we must make sure everyone has enough for a long time."
