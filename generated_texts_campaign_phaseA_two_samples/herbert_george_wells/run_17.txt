Dr. Alistair Finch, a man of precise habits and even more precise intellect, sat among the quiet shadows of his study. It was late; the grandfather clock in the hall had long since chimed the hour of midnight, and a thin, chill air pressed against the windowpanes. He was cataloguing his latest acquisition, an elaborate automaton from the late eighteenth century, purchased at no small expense from a crumbling estate sale. It stood in a corner, a life-sized figure of a courtier in faded velvet, its mechanisms stiff and silent. Alistair considered it a triumph; a beautiful, if inert, piece of human ingenuity.

He heard a faint click, a metallic tick that seemed to emanate from the corner. Alistair paused, quill poised over parchment. The old house often creaked, he told himself; a settlement of beams, perhaps, or a playful gust of wind finding its way through a neglected crack. He made a note about the automaton’s original maker, a certain Monsieur Dubois, a craftsman of minor repute. The automaton stood motionless, its painted eyes fixed on an unseen point beyond the window. He rubbed his temples. He was tired; his mind was playing tricks.

The figure shifted, a minute movement, yet Alistair’s gaze, sharpened by years of scrutinizing ancient texts and delicate mechanisms, caught it. He was a man of science, a staunch disbeliever in the supernatural, and yet his breath caught in his throat.

“Eh?” said Alistair, his mouth agape.

“Do not be alarmed,” said the Voice, though its tone offered little reassurance. “I am quite intact.”

Alistair made no answer for a space, simply stared at the automaton’s moving mouth. “Intact,” he said.

“I am quite intact,” repeated the Voice. The theories he had been active to dismiss only that morning, regarding latent energy and the resonance of ancient metals, rushed through Alistair’s brain. “Yes,” said the Automaton.
